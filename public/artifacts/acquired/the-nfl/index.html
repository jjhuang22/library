A college ritual before a professional business

Acquired reading companion · Spring 2026

The NFLThe league
comes first

A fiercely competitive game became an extraordinary cooperative business. Its owners learned to share the proceeds—and keep finding new ways to sell the spectacle.

Released January 27, 2026 · Created October 19, 2026
About 34 minutes to read · The remastered history and complete 2026 follow-up
Based on Acquired: The NFL, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01

1869–early 1900s · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:05:24

A college ritual before a professional business

The game’s prestige and its violence developed together.

On November 6, 1869, students from Princeton traveled to Rutgers to play a version of football very different from the modern game. Twenty-five players on each side contested a round ball that could not be carried. The hosts use this meeting to begin a history in which rules, institutions, and the meaning of participation kept changing.

Football became part of the American collegiate experience. It was associated with elite education, physical courage, and character building, while serious injuries and deaths were common. The crisis around 1905 brought presidential pressure from Theodore Roosevelt for a safer, more standardized game. The hosts connect that pressure with the institution that became the NCAA and reforms including the forward pass.

The pass also helped create the game’s distinctive visual appeal. A sport built around violent contact could contain a suspended moment of flight, strategy, and extraordinary coordination. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal return repeatedly to this mixture of beauty and harm. It is central both to the product’s appeal and to their complicated feelings about watching it.

Professional teams initially threatened the cultural meaning of college football. Barnstorming players taking money were seen as profaning a formative amateur ritual. Baseball could be an accepted business while professional football remained suspect. The NFL would first have to persuade Americans that its very existence was legitimate.

02

1920–1930s · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:14:11

A league meets in an automobile showroom

Shared standards and a star’s reputation offered a starting point.

In 1920, representatives of several professional teams met in a Canton, Ohio, automobile showroom to form the organization that became the NFL. George Halas, who organized and played for the A. E. Staley company team in Decatur, was one driving force. Sponsorship was embedded in the teams themselves before it became a sophisticated separate revenue line.

The organizers wanted a clear separation from college: no signing current college players, more consistent rules and standards, and a recognizable public face. Jim Thorpe became the first president. His extraordinary athletic reputation gave a fragile group of teams credibility that administrative paperwork alone could not supply.

The hosts emphasize the irony of Thorpe’s Native American identity and the presence of Black pioneers such as Fritz Pollard, a star player and coach. The league later excluded Black players during the 1930 s. They associate that exclusion with the influence of George Preston Marshall and describe Washington’s especially prolonged resistance to integration. The league’s early history was not a steady progression toward a more inclusive product.

The economics were precarious. Small-town teams folded, moved, or survived on owners willing to subsidize losses. Halas’s team became the Chicago Bears; other enduring franchises found larger markets. Green Bay remained the remarkable small-market exception. Without a national medium, the number of people who could physically attend constrained the business.

03

1944–1946 · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:24:34

A rival sees the postwar opportunity

The NFL’s reluctance to expand created room for another league.

Returning servicemen, rising household incomes, and the growth of radio and television changed the potential audience after World War II. Many of these new consumers did not share the elite suspicion of professional football. People wanted entertainment, and more cities wanted teams.

The NFL’s existing owners were reluctant to admit newcomers. In 1944, excluded investors organized the All-America Football Conference, or AAFC. It brought substantial backing in major cities and recruited Paul Brown to coach the new Cleveland Browns. Brown’s reputation made the new team a formidable threat to the Cleveland Rams.

Rams owner Dan Reeves wanted to move to Los Angeles, but other NFL owners resisted the travel and disruption. Competition made inaction harder. In 1946, the league turned to Bert Bell as commissioner and adopted a coordinated response: expand its geography, improve the game, and persuade more Americans to care.

Moving the Rams west also helped force reintegration. The publicly controlled Los Angeles Coliseum would not accept a segregated home team. The Rams signed UCLA star Kenny Washington. A young public-relations worker, Pete Rozelle, helped shape the move’s public face. The institutional pressure and the talent opportunity mattered alongside the league’s stated ideals.

04

1946–1949 · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:31:18

The best team can make the league worse

Paul Brown’s excellence exposed the entertainment value of uncertainty.

Paul Brown treated coaching as a systematic profession. Players studied and took written tests; film review and preparation mattered alongside physical ability. He employed a year-round staff and sought the strongest players without accepting the racial barrier prevalent elsewhere. The hosts admire the rigor without claiming one person invented every modern coaching practice.

His Browns dominated the AAFC, winning all four championships. That was wonderful for Cleveland and damaging to the wider competition. If an away crowd expected its team to be overwhelmed, buying a ticket became less appealing. Losing attendance meant less money for talent and operations, which could make the imbalance worse.

The NFL learned from the counterexample. The product was not simply the excellence of the best team. It was a compelling contest across the whole schedule. Bert Bell’s enduring formulation was that any team should have a chance on any given Sunday.

When the AAFC ended, the NFL absorbed selected teams, including Cleveland and San Francisco. The surviving organization had been forced to improve by a competitor that did not itself survive. The hosts see this pattern recur: the NFL often resisted a change until a challenger demonstrated why it mattered.

Bert Bell’s system · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:41:51

League first, team second

Owners had to trade a local advantage for a more valuable common product.

Bell used the schedule as a competitive lever, matching stronger teams with stronger opponents and weaker teams with weaker ones early in the season. The aim was to keep more teams in contention and more fans interested. The hosts distinguish that management of the appearance of parity from building a genuinely balanced talent base.

The reverse-order college draft directed early choices toward weaker teams. In an era without modern free agency, the arrival of new players was an especially important way for a struggling team to improve. The draft later became a major media event in its own right.

Gate sharing expressed the same principle financially. The home team kept sixty percent; the remaining forty initially went to the visitor and later developed into a broader shared pool. A strong opponent was not merely someone to defeat. Its ability to field a competitive team supported the home team’s ticket business too.

The hosts call this cooperative or communist capitalism: fierce competition on the field within an arrangement designed to grow a shared economic pie. The owners did not stop wanting to win. They accepted that an impoverished or hopeless opponent was bad for the business they owned together.

06

1950s · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:45:58

The game comes into the living room

Television offered reach while threatening the gate.

Television spread rapidly after the war, creating a new way to reach fans beyond a stadium. The football owners had less existing ticket income to protect than baseball, but they still feared replacing attendance with a home viewing experience. The fear had evidence behind it: the hosts describe a Rams television experiment in which attendance fell sharply.

Early broadcasts were crude. A few cameras, limited commentary, and a tiny black-and-white screen could still substitute for buying a ticket. Teams often sold local rights to away games while protecting home attendance. The later language of blackouts obscures that some early local stations had never purchased home-game rights in the first place.

Individual contracts created enormous differences between markets. A popular big-city team could earn many times what Green Bay received. The national sport existed as a patchwork of local businesses, with television threatening to widen the gap Bell wanted to narrow.

The 1958 championship between the Giants and Colts, ending in sudden-death overtime, drew an audience the hosts put around 45 million. It was not yet a Super Bowl. It showed how much demand a dramatic professional football game could command on national television. The next breakthrough would again come partly from outside the NFL.

07

1959–1960 · Remastered 2023 recording · 00:53:23

Lamar Hunt finds the other rejected buyers

A closed ownership club created its strongest challenger.

Lamar Hunt wanted to own an NFL team. An heir to a Dallas oil fortune, he had the money and enthusiasm, but repeated efforts to obtain a franchise failed. He realized there were other wealthy people with the same problem. Instead of continuing to ask the club for permission, they could form a rival.

The American Football League began organizing in 1959. Hunt drew on ideas about centrally sold television rights and equal revenue sharing, including discussions with baseball figure Branch Rickey. The upstart did not have established local contracts to give up, making a shared national deal easier to pursue.

CBS and NBC already had NFL relationships. ABC was a less established network looking for an opening, and Roone Arledge saw a chance to build sports programming. The AFL’s league-wide deal gave it money before it had played a game and made football a national programming event rather than only a collection of local broadcasts.

The hosts’ stated figures for that first agreement are not fully consistent, but their causal point is clear: the new league had a multiyear national contract while the NFL did not. Bert Bell’s death in 1959 left the incumbent facing that challenge without a commissioner. Its owners then struggled to agree on his replacement.

08

1960–1963 · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:03:22

The compromise candidate understood the new America

Pete Rozelle brought public relations into the center of the league.

After prolonged voting, the owners selected thirty-three-year-old Rams general manager Pete Rozelle. He was a compromise, not the obvious senior candidate. The hosts regard that accident as fortunate. His background in Los Angeles, television, promotion, and merchandise fit the emerging media environment better than the habits of the older owners.

As Rams general manager, Rozelle had made a struggling on-field team commercially successful. He understood that branded goods, newspapers, broadcasters, and advertisers were parts of one relationship with the audience. As commissioner, he moved the league’s headquarters from Philadelphia to New York, close to television, magazines, and Madison Avenue.

He hired professional statistical services so reporters could easily cover the games and cultivated Sports Illustrated to tell human stories. He and his staff sometimes effectively supplied the narrative a busy newspaper could adapt. The hosts admire the execution while recognizing how tightly the league learned to manage its image.

Expansion and competition still mattered. The NFL moved to meet the AFL in important markets, while Rozelle worked to make the whole product polished and coherent. The objective was not publicity for its own sake. More attention could become more viewers, better contracts, and stronger teams.

09

1961–1962 · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:11:23

A collective contract needs political permission

Some owners gave up better local deals to sell the league together.

Rozelle persuaded owners to surrender their individual television rights. Cleveland, Pittsburgh, and Baltimore had good local arrangements and faced a short-term sacrifice. Their agreement is one of the hosts’ clearest examples of league-first thinking: accept less immediately to create a larger shared opportunity.

CBS agreed to pay $4.65 million annually under a two-year contract, with the proceeds divided equally. The deal raised an antitrust question. Were independent teams improperly coordinating their sales, or was a league selling a single joint product? The courts did not accept the contract as it stood.

Rozelle’s political relationships helped secure the Sports Broadcasting Act in 1961, allowing this form of collective sports broadcasting. The hosts describe cooperation from the Kennedy administration and the league’s argument that widely available football served a broader social and economic purpose.

This exemption was specific to the television arrangement. It was not a blanket declaration that every NFL activity was immune from competition law. The distinction would matter again when two separate leagues wanted to merge. Football’s business history required public decisions as well as private ingenuity.

1962–1965 · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:16:43

The league learns to remember itself beautifully

Ed and Steve Sabol turned games into an enduring narrative.

Ed Sabol, an enthusiastic filmmaker who had recorded his son’s games, bid $5,000 for the 1962 championship-film rights after learning prior winners paid less. Rozelle met someone proposing an actual cinematic treatment: close angles, slow motion, dramatic editing, and a story rather than an undistinguished record of plays.

The result gave the NFL something different from a television broadcast. High-quality film preserved events, faces, movement, and atmosphere from many angles. While much ordinary broadcast material disappeared, this footage could become an archive and the raw material for future programming.

NFL Films became an in-house operation in 1965. Rozelle’s instruction, as the hosts recount it, was not to maximize immediate profit; avoiding losses while promoting the game was enough. The investment could make every other part of the league more valuable.

Sending crews to games, returning footage, and editing rapidly created substantial organizational capacity. The full value was not obvious at the outset. The archive would soon support Monday Night Football highlights and decades of storytelling. A film division was both a creative project and infrastructure for deepening fandom.

11

1960s · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:22:45

The same check in Cleveland and Green Bay

Merchandise and history reinforced the national product.

Rozelle brought branded merchandise under a common league organization, setting a more consistent standard and sharing the proceeds. A larger market could no longer simply keep all the benefit of selling more jerseys and pennants. The owners were again being asked to give up a revenue opportunity in order to increase the value of the shared league.

The Pro Football Hall of Fame in Canton added institutional history. Film, merchandise, statistics, magazine stories, and the Hall all helped the audience experience something larger than a weekly result. The hosts describe a cycle in which polish drove attention, attention raised television income, and shared income supported a stronger, more competitive game.

Green Bay’s ownership made a useful exception within the system. Its widely held nonprofit corporation sells shares without the ordinary promise of financial return or concentrated control. The hosts see that structure as helping keep the team in its small hometown. Its public reports also offered a window into economics otherwise hidden in privately owned teams.

The television contracts grew quickly. A later CBS agreement paid $14.1 million annually, roughly three times the earlier deal. The network was buying an audience and an advertising opportunity that expanded faster than the contract could be reset. For a time, several participants could prosper together.

12

1964–1966 · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:30:19

The other league creates a national celebrity

Media money paid for players who could attract more media attention.

The AFL did not collapse when the NFL obtained its larger contract. Sonny Werblen, the Jets’ entertainment-industry owner, understood that losing networks still needed football. NBC signed a five-year AFL deal worth $37.5 million, or $7.5 million a year. With fewer teams sharing it, the upstart could compete for talent.

Werblen and the Jets invested in Joe Namath. Broadway Joe was not only a promising quarterback but a star who could move across talk shows, fashion, advertising, and the wider culture. The hosts see his appeal to women and families as evidence against the assumption that football could draw only a narrow male audience.

The two leagues fought over college players, ignoring one another’s drafts. Rookie contracts escalated, and NFL representatives sometimes hid recruits in hotels to prevent the other league from reaching them. Competition improved athletes’ bargaining position even as it threatened owners’ economics.

By 1966, some owners recognized that the AFL would endure. A merger became attractive, but starting open negotiations could be dangerous. Cowboys general manager Tex Schramm and Hunt began quiet discussions, with Rozelle acting on his owners’ instructions despite his own desire to win the war.

13

1966–1970 · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:42:31

The war over a kicker becomes a merger

Al Davis supplied leverage while other owners worked toward peace.

Al Davis became AFL commissioner with a mandate to confront the NFL. The leagues had largely confined their bidding war to incoming rookies, leaving existing players alone. When the Giants signed an AFL veteran kicker, they broke that understanding and gave Davis his opening.

He targeted quarterbacks, including the Rams’ Roman Gabriel, then encouraged wider signings. The hosts retell the exchanges as a dramatic negotiation story. Davis’s aggression imposed costs both sides wanted to avoid, improving the AFL’s position while Hunt continued the quieter merger discussions.

The June 1966 agreement preserved all AFL teams, provided for a common draft, and set 1970 as the first fully combined season after existing television obligations ran out. Rozelle would remain commissioner. The hosts describe $18 million in payments over twenty years, chiefly addressing the New York and Bay Area market conflicts, rather than the much larger initial demands made of the AFL.

The combined league needed another congressional exemption. The hosts recount political bargaining in which a New Orleans franchise helped secure support. This was a separate competition issue from pooling television rights: two rival organizations were eliminating their direct competition.

The merger was a triumph for owners and helped create a unified entertainment property. It also ended an especially valuable source of bidding competition for players. The common draft’s benefit was not distributed equally among everyone involved.

14

1966–1969 seasons · Remastered 2023 recording · 01:56:42

Invent a championship after the rights are sold

The new game became a separate national media event.

Before the leagues fully combined, their champions would meet in a new world championship. Existing contracts covered each league’s own games; the additional contest was a fresh product to sell. CBS and NBC each paid $1 million to broadcast the first game and committed substantial promotion.

The hosts contrast roughly 65 million television viewers with a Los Angeles Coliseum that was only about two-thirds full. The event no longer depended entirely on filling the stadium. Its television audience could be vastly larger than its physical capacity.

Rozelle built a week around it: interviews, a commissioner’s press conference, parties, and events for advertisers, broadcasters, and the press. Treating the partners well could influence how those partners presented the game. The spectacle around the contest was deliberately produced rather than an accidental accumulation of attention.

The Packers won the first two games. Then Namath’s guarantee and the Jets’ upset of the Colts supplied the story the AFL needed. A painful defeat for one NFL owner was a victory for the attractiveness of the whole merged product. When the Chiefs won the next meeting, the pre-merger series ended level. The competitive uncertainty at the heart of the business had been demonstrated on its biggest stage.

15

1970 · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:09:40

One game, the whole country, every week

Monday Night Football concentrated resources into appointment viewing.

The combined league retained separate Sunday packages for CBS and NBC, then created another event. Rozelle and Roone Arledge believed one game in prime time could reach people who did not watch ordinary Sunday coverage. ABC, left outside the main contracts, had both the incentive and the creative leadership to attempt it.

Monday Night Football cost ABC $8.5 million per season for far less game inventory than the Sunday packages. The hosts explain why that could still make sense. One nationally watched production could deliver a large audience while concentrating cameras, talent, and engineering instead of spreading crews across many simultaneous games.

The broadcast treated football as show business. Additional field-level cameras, stronger sound, more angles, split screens, interviews, music, and a three-person booth made it feel unlike a distant static view from midfield. Howard Cosell became a personality viewers could react to, part of an ongoing relationship rather than a neutral voice reading the action.

The hosts’ point is the coordinated experience, not that every individual production technique had never existed anywhere before. The game became a recurring national program with recognizable characters and a style. That changed both what viewers expected and how much the rights could be worth.

16

1970 onward · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:21:41

Sunday’s drama returns on Monday

The film operation made a second product from the same games.

At halftime, Monday Night Football showed highlights of the previous day’s games. NFL Films could gather material from across the country, edit it, and get it to the Monday broadcast location in roughly a day. Cosell’s commentary helped turn the package into a story about the league.

Today the availability of highlights can feel inevitable. In the hosts’ telling, it required the earlier decision to capture high-quality footage even when the immediate financial return was unclear. The archive and the production organization became useful in a new setting.

Rozelle was less farsighted about home blackouts. He continued protecting ticket sales long after the hosts believe broader television reach would have been better. Political pressure, including from President Nixon, helped force changes. Their admiration for the commissioner includes this strategic mistake.

The episode does not chronicle every later relocation or rival league. By the 1970 s, its central system was in place: compelling games, polished presentation, expanding distribution, and shared proceeds. The later story tests how well that system could survive new money and new forms of attention.

17

1970s–2023 perspective · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:26:19

A league-first system develops local exceptions

Stadium revenue could grow without being shared in the same way.

Bigger, more elaborate stadiums introduced luxury suites, sponsorships, premium experiences, and valuable local commercial relationships. These made attending different from watching at home and created substantial new income. Much of that income belonged to the individual team rather than the shared pool.

The hosts regard this divergence as a central vulnerability. A team in a large market with a powerful brand and a lucrative stadium could pull away financially while a smaller team depended mainly on national distributions. Their historical figures put unshared revenue at roughly 12 percent in 1994, 21 percent in 2003, and above 30 percent by the original recording.

Modern free agency arrived with the 1993 collective bargaining agreement alongside a salary cap. The cap and player share linked spending on talent to league revenue, helping preserve competition on the field. The hosts describe the players as collectively receiving close to half the relevant revenue, while emphasizing how uneven individual careers and earnings remain.

A common payroll system does not erase every business difference. If national pay obligations rise with large teams’ local success, a weaker team may have less left for coaching, facilities, and other support. Sharing can keep a league together without making each member’s economics identical.

18

The original 2023 business snapshot · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:31:03

Sell different ways to watch the same season

National reach and deep fan engagement support several products.

The original analysis describes a league around $18 billion in annual revenue and a major package of multiyear media agreements. Sunday afternoon, Sunday night, Monday night, and Thursday night each offered a different window and audience proposition. They were separate products for distributors rather than one undifferentiated rights sale.

Sunday Ticket added an out-of-market bundle of games already produced for other broadcasters. The league could monetize access for fans who wanted more than their local offering while partners supplied much of the production. Video-game licensing, merchandise, and other rights extended the reach of the same teams and players.

Fantasy football encouraged people to care about games beyond their own team. The hosts cite tens of millions of participants, making football a recurring subject among friends, families, and coworkers. Betting could similarly increase attention. Their 2023 snapshot spoke of roughly 46 million Americans betting on the NFL; the later follow-up revises the scale and the business consequences.

The original revenue breakdown was roughly 61 percent media, 10 percent general seating, 10 percent premium seating, 10 percent sponsorship and advertising, and 9 percent other. It is a dated approximate picture, not a current segment statement. The wider argument is that media dominated a sport once dependent on the gate.

19

The original 2023 discussion · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:39:15

The product’s appeal does not remove the harm

Player safety was a trust issue as well as a business risk.

The hosts turn explicitly to the long-term neurological harm associated with repeated head impacts. David recalls playing football while thinking mainly about immediate injuries, without understanding the later mental and neurological risks. Both distinguish loving the sport from accepting how the league handled evidence about its consequences.

They criticize the NFL’s research and public response, describing years of denial and delayed acknowledgment. The concussion settlement and the public attention around CTE represented a major break in trust. Their concern extends beyond participation numbers to what parents believe they are consenting to when children play.

The business could remain powerful despite that failure. The hosts discuss generational preferences and compare the NBA’s visibility among younger fans, but those judgments belong to the original recording. The 2026 follow-up later walks back confidence in some of the demographic conclusions.

The ethical and commercial questions are related without being identical. A durable revenue stream does not settle whether the risk is acceptable or whether people were treated honestly. The hosts’ own continuing enjoyment carries that unresolved tension.

20

2016, discussed in 2023 · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:43:06

Who the commissioner works for

Control of the message became harder in a world of individual platforms.

Colin Kaepernick knelt during the national anthem in 2016 to protest police brutality and racial inequality. After that season, no team signed him. The hosts acknowledge injuries and disappointing play while plainly stating their view that the league blackballed him; they also note his grievance and confidential settlement.

Their explanation begins with governance. The commissioner works for the owners collectively, not as a neutral president of football or a representative of fans and players. The league office is a coordinating layer over independently substantial team businesses. Its tax status is therefore not the same thing as the tax status or profitability of those teams.

Ben and David criticize the response as both wrong and strategically clumsy. Attempting to contain the protest helped make it a much larger national story. Their comparison with the NBA concerns the willingness to let players possess voices and platforms of their own.

The story exposes a limit of Rozelle’s inherited command of the narrative. Supplying polished stories to a few media partners was different from managing public figures with direct access to an audience. The league could remain commercially strong while mishandling that change.

21

Original 2023 concluding analysis · Remastered 2023 recording · 02:48:39

The entertainment package captures the value

The hosts admire the system while questioning who bears its costs.

The original playbook emphasizes staying power. The NFL had become a habit and a shared cultural calendar, and large technology companies were joining broadcasters as rights buyers. The hosts thought the business could survive the decline of linear television without having to build distribution technology itself.

They still questioned its ability to find more fans, especially internationally and among younger people. Those concerns were genuine parts of the 2023 conclusion, not predictions the remaster silently erases. The follow-up supplies their revised view after three more years.

The college game provides developed players and ready-made stories without the NFL directly operating a conventional minor-league system. The hosts also examine bargaining sequence: agreeing to a long player contract before resetting media rights can change negotiating leverage, even though player compensation remains connected to revenue.

Their analysis of broadcaster economics originally stressed advertising, scarce live audiences, and competition among distributors. They ask how much profit the networks can retain as rights prices rise. In 2026, Ben explicitly adds an important missing element—retransmission and subscription fees—so the earlier ad-only view should not be treated as the final explanation.

Original 2023 Seven Powers and value capture · Remastered 2023 recording · 03:09:19

The scarce resource is the football itself

Brand fame alone does not explain the league’s bargaining position.

The strongest power in the hosts’ account is a cornered resource: the best professional players and the coherent competition around them. A rival cannot offer the same product merely by attaching a different logo. Unifying the AFL and NFL helped secure that position.

They also identify historical counter-positioning against baseball’s gate-centered economics and scale economies in producing sports entertainment for a huge audience. They hesitate to call the famous NFL brand a separate pricing power, because an equivalent alternative with the same players and contest does not exist. Antitrust permissions are discussed as another scarce institutional advantage without forcing a settled classification.

Value capture extends to broadcasters, players, and communities. The hosts criticize taxpayer-supported stadium arrangements while noting that not every stadium is publicly financed. They see the players’ revenue share as an improvement over earlier bargaining conditions, not evidence that risk or compensation is now fair for every athlete.

Scarce franchise ownership also contains prestige value beyond cash flow. Their 2023 forecast that team values might plateau would prove too conservative. The remaster preserves it so the later revision has context.

The original bull case was durability; the bear case was damage to the cooperative system, player safety, younger audiences, and failed international expansion. Neither the pleasure of a family Super Bowl party nor the cost of neurological harm fits neatly into a league revenue total. The hosts end that original discussion with enjoyment and unease together.

January 2026 follow-up · 2026 follow-up · 03:19:00

Three years later, the answer changes

The hosts revisit the forecasts rather than merely adding larger numbers.

The 2026 follow-up opens by reconsidering international expansion. There were now seven international games across five countries and a stated ambition to reach sixteen games annually. The hosts regard the free global YouTube stream of a game in São Paulo as evidence of a more substantial strategy than they credited in 2023.

Regular-season viewing had also improved. They cite 18.7 million viewers per game and a ten-percent annual gain, then compare that with 17.5 million in 2011. The long view looks more like recovery and modest expansion than an uninterrupted high-growth curve. The Super Bowl had reached a record 127 million viewers.

Ben corrects the earlier description of television economics. Broadcast networks earn not only advertising but retransmission fees from distributors carrying their channels. As cable shrinks, direct subscriptions and digital carriage can play related roles. These fees cover a broader programming bundle; they are not a payment attributed solely to a single NFL game.

That second stream helps explain why valuable sports rights can support more than an ad-sales calculation would suggest. It also makes the NFL’s influence on subscription decisions central to the distributors’ interest.

January 2026 follow-up · 2026 follow-up · 03:24:53

More bettors, several routes to revenue

Direct sponsorship is only part of the estimated impact.

The hosts compare roughly 46 million Americans betting on the NFL in the original discussion with an estimated 76 million in the update. Legalization and easier access broadened participation beyond the earlier informal and limited legal markets.

They estimate gambling-related sponsorships around $200 million annually. A gentler entry into the game

Flag football broadens participation, with uncertain long-term results.

The college relationship is less settled

A once-clear institutional hierarchy has partly reversed.

The hosts are sharply critical of the way college-player compensation and transfers evolved. 2023 sale, explained in 2026 · 2026 follow-up · 03:54:12

The ownership rules become the bottleneck

The league needed an individual with billions in accessible equity.

The update’s last major story asks who could actually buy a team. Apart from the grandfathered Packers exception, the NFL required a principal natural-person owner whose family held at least thirty percent in equity. The hosts describe a normal debt ceiling around $800 million, with a higher allowance for acquisitions, plus a limited group of silent minority partners.

They see the structure as part of the league’s success. A small room of owners, deeply invested personally and financially, could agree on long-term collective decisions. It was different from a shifting set of corporate investors optimizing a portfolio.

Dan Snyder’s scandals and pressure to sell Washington’s franchise exposed the financing limit. The hosts describe harassment, financial impropriety, and investigations as the background. Other owners had the power to force a divestiture through a supermajority; in practice, Snyder initiated a sale rather than take that confrontation to its end.

At a sale price just above $6 billion, a thirty-percent principal stake required at least $1.8 billion of equity. The buyer also needed partners able to supply several billion more. Josh Harris assembled the group, but the difficulty suggested that future sales could become hostage to a very narrow pool of eligible liquid families.

The league’s success had raised values faster than its buyer rules expanded. Resolving one distressed ownership situation led the owners to change who could supply minority capital.

2024 rules, discussed in 2026 · 2026 follow-up · 04:03:13

Let capital in without giving up control

The NFL set terms for investors eager to enter.

In 2024, owners voted to permit selected private-equity investment. The hosts describe four central restrictions. Only league-approved firms could participate; institutional holdings were capped at ten percent of a franchise; the investors were silent minority partners; and part of their eventual investment return would flow back through the league to all thirty-two teams.

The last provision astonishes them. They compare it economically to carried interest while admitting they do not know the exact legal mechanics. They do not disclose a rate. The point is that a sale involving one team could create a shared benefit, including for teams that chose not to sell a stake.

This connected a capital-market change with the old parity logic. Some owners could obtain liquidity and reset a valuation, but the league wanted a mechanism that let others participate in part of the upside. It allowed new money without turning principal control over to the funds.

The hosts’ numerical example assumes a ten-percent investor stake in an $8 billion team, thirty-percent appreciation, and an entirely hypothetical ten-percent skim on the gain. Those assumptions produce $24 million. That is an illustration of sensitivity to a rate they do not know, not a reported payment or an NFL fee schedule.

A wider buyer pool changes the clearing price

Minority transactions complicate the old scarcity calculation.

The hosts cite Forbes estimates of $7.1 billion for the average team and $228 billion collectively, up around 62 percent from their original $140 billion total. They explicitly distinguish estimates from liquid quoted prices. The earlier forecast of a plateau had missed the change.

Private equity’s role was not limited to the stakes funds actually bought. Its presence as an eligible bidder affected prices in transactions with individuals and family offices too. The update lists a wave of minority sales rather than a wave of complete franchise transfers.

They cite a revenue-multiple expansion from 6.4 times five years earlier to 10.7 times, and hear anecdotes of wealth advisers treating minority stakes as annuity-like holdings. That comparison describes investor thinking; a team stake is not a bond with a guaranteed coupon.

The business had improved and the buyer pool had widened. The hosts regard the ownership change as especially important to the speed of asset appreciation, while leaving open how much reflects confidence in future cash flow and how much reflects willingness to own a scarce prestige asset.

January 2026 final synthesis · 2026 follow-up · 04:11:54

The cooperation still has to hold

The larger league contains a widening range of team economics.

The Cowboys supply the most dramatic final example. The hosts cite an estimated $13 billion valuation, 2024 revenue around $1.2 billion, and operating income around $630 million. They contrast that with roughly $127 million for an average team and $21 million for the least profitable in the cited estimates. These are operating-profit comparisons, not identical dividend checks promised to every owner.

That disparity is the continuing bear case. A very profitable team may see the shared system differently from a team near the bottom, even when both receive national money and face the same broad competitive structure. The private-equity return-sharing provision is another attempt to keep their interests connected.

The hosts conclude that their 2023 question—could the NFL grow further?—has received a clear affirmative answer, particularly in asset value. Streaming, cultural reach, and new capital have made the business stronger than they expected.

Their admiration ends where the story began: competition on the field depends on cooperation around it. The scale has changed enormously since small-town teams met in Canton. The need to keep owners choosing the league’s long-term value over an immediate private advantage remains.
