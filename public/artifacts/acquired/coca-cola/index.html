A pharmacist searches for another market

Acquired reading companion · Fall 2025

Coca-ColaThe drink
and the system

A pharmacist made the syrup. Entrepreneurs carried it into everyday life. Advertising made the bottle mean something more than refreshment.

Released November 24, 2025 · Created October 18, 2026
About 25 minutes to read · Origins through the hosts’ 2025 analysis
Based on Acquired: Coca-Cola, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01

1860s–1886 · 00:12:14

A pharmacist searches for another market

The refreshment business emerged from the patent-medicine trade.

John Pemberton came out of the Civil War wounded and dependent on morphine. After moving to Atlanta, he pursued remedies in a market full of extravagant medical promises. The hosts place him within the expanding patent-medicine industry: inexpensive ingredients, national advertising, and customers looking for relief. Many products carried the suggestion of proprietary protection without actually being patented. The salesmanship was often far stronger than the evidence behind the claims.

Pemberton’s early success was French Wine Coca, a wine infused with coca leaves and kola nuts. He was adapting a fashionable French product while adding caffeine. This was a drug-oriented tonic, not yet the familiar soft drink. When Atlanta adopted prohibition in the mid-1880s, he needed a nonalcoholic alternative. Experimentation led him toward a different commercial possibility: something inexpensive enough to buy for enjoyment rather than only when seeking a cure.

The product he developed in 1886 combined sweetness, acidity, flavorings, caffeine, and coca extract in a concentrated syrup. A pharmacy could mix it with carbonated water and sell a refreshing drink for five cents. The initial formulation contained cocaine and substantially more caffeine than the modern drink. Ben and David discuss estimates of the original drug content, but the surviving information does not support an exact recipe or dose; those estimates are not needed to understand the business.

The place of consumption mattered as much as the ingredients. Drugstores and soda fountains were social gathering places, and carbonated water already had associations with health tonics. The new drink could inhabit both worlds: an affordable pleasure and a product marketed with medicinal language. Frank Robinson, Pemberton’s business partner and bookkeeper, supplied its name and then its script logo. Coca-Cola was beginning to acquire an identity that would outlast the substances that gave it its name.

02

1887–1892 · 00:25:07

A free glass aligned the whole chain

Sampling gave customers, shopkeepers, and salesmen a reason to participate.

Robinson helped turn the drink into something people could find and try. He and Pemberton distributed tickets redeemable for a free glass at local fountains, mailing them to Atlanta addresses and putting them in the hands of traveling salesmen. A consumer received a low-risk introduction. The fountain gained a visit and a possible repeat customer. A salesman gained a small benefit to offer while making his ordinary calls.

That arrangement was economical because syrup was cheap relative to the final drink’s selling price. Rather than preserving every cent of early margin, the company could put some of it into trial and distribution. The hosts emphasize the incentives throughout the chain: each participant had a reason to help another participant encounter Coca-Cola. Sampling was not simply a message sent from headquarters to the public; it gave other businesses a useful way to serve their own customers.

The ownership behind that growing demand was much less orderly. Believing he was dying, Pemberton sold interests in the formula without telling Robinson, leaving conflicting claims. Robinson brought in Atlanta businessman Asa Candler to assemble those interests. The hosts report that Candler paid a total of roughly $2,300 for the rights he consolidated. In 1892 he incorporated the definitive Coca-Cola Company, with Robinson helping create the organization around a product that was already attracting customers.

03

1890s · 00:36:10

The syrup company learned to blanket a country

A low-cost concentrate supported expensive visibility.

Candler’s business could be small in headcount and large in reach. It sold syrup; fountains supplied the water, dispensing equipment, premises, and customer service. The hosts cite an early year with roughly $46,000 in company revenue and $12,000 in profit after production and advertising expenses. They separately describe much larger spending by consumers at the fountains. Those are different levels of the chain, not interchangeable measures of Coca-Cola’s revenue.

The margin made visibility affordable. Coca-Cola paid for signs, murals, trays, calendars, glasses, and clocks. A drugstore could get an attractive sign with its own name and Coca-Cola’s name together. The store wanted the foot traffic and the drink’s retail margin; Coca-Cola wanted its identity to become part of the surroundings. By the hosts’ account, the company was distributing more than a million promotional objects annually before 1900.

Concentrate was compact and relatively easy to transport. Coupled with local preparation and sampling, that gave the company a way to expand nationally without constructing a chain of its own shops. The episode describes a presence in at least one fountain in every US state and territory by 1895. The business did not yet have the dense reach it would eventually obtain, but its geography had already escaped Atlanta.

Robinson also moved the message away from treating ailments. A remedy addresses the people who have a particular problem; refreshment can address almost everyone. Advertising increasingly emphasized pleasure and the drink itself rather than headaches or nervousness. The script trademark became a recognizable constant. Coca-Cola was starting to sell a positive association as well as a flavor.

04

1899–1920s · 00:42:06

The contract that made a system

A decision to avoid bottling created a much larger bottling network.

Benjamin Thomas and Joseph Whitehead believed bottles could carry a carbonated drink beyond the fountain. Candler doubted the technology and did not want the expense. Their offer removed the immediate burden: they would buy his syrup, finance bottling themselves, and risk their own money. In July 1899 the parties signed an agreement for a token dollar that Candler never collected.

The agreement gave them assignable, exclusive bottling rights across most of the United States. Coca-Cola would supply syrup at a dollar per gallon and retain control of advertising; the bottlers were to maintain quality, use its syrup, meet demand, and preserve the five-cent retail proposition. Crucially, the agreement had no expiration and no ordinary mechanism for changing the syrup price. The hosts call it both a remarkable mistake and the foundation of an extraordinary model.

Thomas and Whitehead soon divided their territories. Each then assigned smaller local rights to entrepreneurs who would perform the expensive work of buying bottles, building plants, cleaning returns, and delivering drinks. The two original organizations became parent bottlers sitting between Coca-Cola and the first-line bottlers. The latter did not initially have direct contracts with the Coca-Cola Company. This extra layer would remain a source of conflict long after it had helped organize the network.

Local capital made expansion fast. The hosts describe roughly 400 bottling proprietors within ten years and about 1,200 by 1925. Bottles reached groceries, stands, rural communities, and homes where a fountain was unavailable. Coca-Cola did not have to finance each production line before entering the next town. The entrepreneurs earned attractive local businesses while the company retained the more capital-efficient activity of supplying syrup and building demand.

The bargain nevertheless constrained its creator. Perpetual prices and rights could not simply be revised when costs or opportunities changed. Attempts to remove the parent bottlers led to litigation rather than immediate control. Later management would buy, improve, and resell bottling operations when necessary. The system’s success did not eliminate the need to negotiate with its owners.

05

1900s–1920s · 00:54:32

Making the real thing recognizable

The name and the package became defenses against substitution.

Expansion brought imitators. Coke protected its name aggressively through trademark litigation, seeking to make Coca-Cola a single source rather than one variety within a generic category. In the Koke dispute that reached the Supreme Court in 1920, the rival argued that the name was misleading after changes to the ingredients. As the hosts explain the result, the name’s established meaning to consumers was more important than a literal reading of its botanical components.

By then the product had also moved away from its original drug profile. The company obtained coca material from which cocaine had been removed and reduced caffeine substantially. Ben and David see the persistence of demand as evidence that the attachment was no longer simply a response to the original stimulants. They also describe a later, disturbing episode in which a company employee at a Peruvian operation sold the cocaine byproduct while trying to generate revenue. That is an anecdote in their closing discussion, not an ingredient in today’s drink.

A wordmark did not solve every problem for bottlers. If a drink was poured into an unmarked bottle, a customer might not know which product was being sold. The bottlers therefore sought a proprietary package that would remain identifiable even by touch or as a broken fragment. The Root Glass Company produced the contour design, patented in 1915 and introduced in this period. Its distinctive shape emerged partly from a mistaken reference to a cocoa pod, a different plant from coca.

The bottle made packaging part of the identity customers recognized. It also required investment from the bottlers who would buy and use it. The company’s appeal was that protecting the package protected their businesses as well as the central trademark. This was a recurring feature of the system: headquarters could set exacting standards because partners expected to earn from the shared brand.

06

1919–1930s · 01:09:40

Woodruff made the promise consistent

A truck executive took charge of a drink, its image, and the places it could be bought.

In 1919, banker Ernest Woodruff assembled a syndicate that bought the Candler family’s interests for $25 million. Financing the transaction placed the written formula in a bank vault as collateral, adding another layer to its celebrated secrecy. Ernest did not want to run the business indefinitely. In 1923 the board turned to his son Robert, a successful young executive at White Motor in Cleveland. Robert accepted only after negotiating for authority independent of his father.

Robert Woodruff and Archie Lee of the D’Arcy advertising agency expanded what Coca-Cola could mean. The message became shorter and the imagery more evocative. Instead of explaining every property of the drink, campaigns associated it with a break, companionship, romance, and an idealized American life. The hosts distinguish this advertising of feelings from the earlier advertising of a product’s intrinsic attributes.

Christmas helped make refreshment an all-season proposition. In 1931, artist Haddon Sundblom began the Coca-Cola Santa illustrations. Coke did not invent Santa Claus; the hosts explicitly reject that myth. Their argument is that the company’s enormous advertising apparatus helped popularize a consistent, warm, red-clothed image. Billboards, magazines, and shop displays repeated the association until the drink felt at home in winter as well as summer.

Woodruff pushed the physical product toward the same consistency: taste, temperature, packaging, and presentation should feel familiar wherever someone encountered it. Market research pointed toward more occasions to buy, not merely more people. Gas stations and coolers placed a cold drink along the growing automobile network. The episode describes 32,000 coolers installed in the first year of that push, followed later by vending machines.

Quality control sometimes required ownership. Woodruff was willing to buy weak bottlers, improve them, and return them to local entrepreneurs. He also carried the model overseas. These interventions complicate a simple claim that Coke never had to touch bottling. The desired structure remained independent local operations, but maintaining the promise could require active repair of the system.

07

1930s–1941 · 01:37:39

Twice as much for a nickel

The package that protected Coke also limited its response.

The long five-cent price gave Coca-Cola an affordable position, supported by scale. The hosts initially discuss the Depression as if it were inflationary; the official correction is clear that it was deflationary. The useful competitive observation survives without that error: a familiar, inexpensive drink was an attractive proposition when household budgets were under severe pressure.

Pepsi had struggled through failures and changes of ownership. In 1934 it tried selling twelve ounces for a nickel in inexpensive reused beer bottles. Coke’s contour bottle held only six and a half ounces. The extra liquid did not double the cost of the package, so Pepsi could offer a striking increase in quantity without a corresponding increase in price. Consumers could see the difference immediately.

Coke could not answer casually. Its bottlers had invested in the distinctive small bottle, while its positioning relied on being the authentic product rather than a bargain substitute. Ben and David describe Pepsi’s move as counter-positioning: what was sensible for an insurgent was difficult for the incumbent to copy without disturbing its own structure.

Trademark litigation then took a turn. The hosts recount Pepsi’s discovery of improper tactics against another rival and Woodruff’s decision to settle rather than continue the fight. Pepsi kept using Cola, weakening Coke’s attempt to monopolize that word as part of its name. The larger point is that a commercial opening and a legal opening reinforced each other. Coke now faced a legitimate competitor with a reason for customers to try it.

08

1941–1950s · 01:48:35

A wartime route to the world

Government support accelerated a global distribution system.

Coca-Cola entered the Second World War already associated with American life. For soldiers overseas, a bottle could represent home. Woodruff promised that an American serviceman should be able to obtain one for five cents wherever he was stationed. The company’s commercial interest and the military’s concern for morale aligned in a way Pepsi could not easily reproduce.

The details matter. Coke did not obtain an unlimited exemption from all sugar rationing. The hosts describe favorable treatment for military supply that extended through bottlers serving areas near bases. Coca-Cola technical personnel also participated in the wartime infrastructure effort. Production could move closer to troops instead of relying solely on shipments of finished drinks from America.

The episode cites 64 portable bottling plants sent to Europe, Asia, and North Africa and billions of bottles distributed during the war. Ben and David mention differing estimates for the bottle count, so the exact total is uncertain. What they consider decisive is the infrastructure and familiarity left behind. The company itself described the effort as a vast sampling program, one that accelerated international market development by decades.

This history includes the German bottlers’ creation of the first beverage called Fanta when wartime conditions cut off supplies. The official correction distinguishes that drink from modern Fanta, which originated in a Naples bottling plant and reused the existing trademark. The name’s continuity should not be mistaken for an unchanged product. After the war, independent bottlers could turn Coke’s new global presence into locally owned businesses, combining an American-associated brand with strong local incentives.

09

1950s–1960s · 01:57:26

Pepsi found the people Coke overlooked

A competitor challenged habits in marketing, customers, and packaging.

Former Coke executive Alfred Steele joined Pepsi and brought more disciplined operations alongside a willingness to challenge tradition. He continued efforts to reach Black Americans with a dedicated sales organization and advertising. The hosts contrast that with Coca-Cola’s reluctance and Woodruff’s support for segregationist politicians at the time. They also note that Woodruff’s position changed later. The opportunity existed because Coke had not treated everyone as equally worth pursuing.

Pepsi used television to reach younger customers and positioned itself as lighter and more modern. That description was a marketing proposition, not an established nutritional advantage. Coke’s identity as the familiar drink could become a liability if young people regarded it as their parents’ choice. The hosts see the competition of these years as more inventive than a simple contest over which liquid tasted best.

Coke eventually moved its account to McCann Erickson, embraced television more fully, and sought consistent messaging across media. It increased its appeal to Black consumers and entered diet drinks with Tab. The hosts discuss an unconfirmed story that bottling royalties helped explain why the company did not initially use the Coca-Cola name for a diet drink. That remains unconfirmed; the clearer strategic hesitation was lending a protected brand to a different proposition.

A different partnership began in 1955 when Ray Kroc approached Coke for the expanding McDonald’s business. Its importance grew with the restaurant chain. The hosts describe special treatment and close operating cooperation, and relay several possible reasons customers find its fountain Coke distinctive. Those taste explanations have varying levels of certainty. More firmly within their account, a large customer and its beverage supplier helped one another expand, including through Coke’s established overseas relationships.

10

1960s–1971 · 02:17:31

A global feeling, assembled in an advertisement

The company learned to keep its identity while borrowing the mood of a new generation.

Coke’s response was not limited to the fountain. Cans and other packages made the drink available in more settings; Minute Maid took the company beyond its original category. Pepsi’s acquisition of Frito-Lay, an opportunity Coke had declined, gave its rival another powerful business. But Coke’s most memorable achievement in this stretch was an advertisement.

McCann’s Hilltop commercial grew from Bill Backer’s account of stranded airline passengers sharing conversation over Coke. He saw a way to connect the beverage with cross-border companionship. The eventual production gathered a diverse cast to sing on a hillside. Rain ruined attempts in Britain and then near Rome, forcing reshoots, new performers, and a substantially larger budget than the initial $100,000.

Released in 1971, the commercial joined Coke to a language of international friendship and harmony. Its song became popular enough to be rerecorded with different lyrics as a commercial music release. Ben and David admire the craft while noticing the appropriation: a large corporation had attached a drink to countercultural aspirations. The commercial did not describe ingredients or manufacturing. It supplied another emotional setting in which the brand could feel familiar.

11

Late 1960s–1980s · 02:27:58

The challenge came from a card table

Local demonstrations confronted a national advertising machine.

John Sculley joined Pepsi from advertising and helped identify the opportunity for a larger package for home consumption. Heavy, breakable glass limited the appeal of a family-sized bottle. Working with DuPont, Pepsi adopted PET plastic and a production method bottlers could use without the same heavy new investment. Coke took years to answer. The hosts also confront the environmental consequence: the success of convenient disposable packaging helped create an enduring waste problem.

Sculley then noticed a local bottler’s campaign in Dallas. An advertising agency running taste research for another cola had found that many participants preferred Pepsi to Coke. The bottler turned the result into filmed challenges at ordinary shopping locations. People saw neighbors choose a drink without its label rather than actors delivering a polished claim.

The national expansion preserved that local character. Pepsi supplied equipment and encouraged bottlers to stage, film, and advertise their own tests. Coke’s centralized approach was poorly matched to the attack. McCann had actually produced similar unfavorable findings for Coke much earlier; the hosts recount Woodruff ordering that the research be suppressed rather than repeated.

By 1977, Pepsi was outspending Coke on advertising and had passed it in the US bottled market, according to the episode. Coke still led overall because fountains, including McDonald’s, were a different channel. The distinction prevents a claim that Pepsi had overtaken the entire business. Sculley’s success eventually helped bring Steve Jobs to recruit him to Apple in 1983. For Coke, the unresolved challenge remained: its advertising had spent decades teaching people the product was unique, while a simple comparison challenged its taste.

12

1980–1984 · 02:42:45

A new formula could be a separate promise

Diet Coke showed that the brand could stretch without erasing the original.

Coke’s difficulty responding was partly managerial. Woodruff retained enormous influence as he aged, while the hosts describe CEO Paul Austin’s declining health and a company unable to make decisions. Roberto Goizueta, a Cuban-born chemical engineer who had risen through technical research, became chief executive at the start of the 1980s. Don Keough became his operating partner, pairing external leadership with Goizueta’s product and strategic strengths.

Their choices included the switch toward high-fructose corn syrup in the American drink, driven by relative input costs. They also acquired Columbia Pictures. The hosts describe the studio transaction as financially successful when later sold to Sony and emphasize the relationships it opened with Allen & Company and the media world. The beverage company’s most consequential product decision, however, was Diet Coke.

It was not simply original Coke with the sugar removed. The company had developed a separate taste while studying the competitive challenge and the expanding diet market. Using the Coca-Cola name risked both the established trademark’s meaning and the position of Tab, already a successful diet product. Management decided that the size of the opportunity justified competing with its strongest name.

Diet Coke was announced in July 1982 at Radio City Music Hall. By the end of its first full calendar year it led the American diet category, and by 1984 it ranked behind only Coke and Pepsi among US soft drinks, in the hosts’ account. Its proposition emphasized taste rather than apologizing for a compromise. It also avoided sugar’s cost. The success demonstrated how a new offering could win customers; it did not establish that those customers wanted the old offering taken away.

13

1984–April 1985 · 02:54:43

The research measured the wrong decision

A preferred taste was mistaken for permission to replace a cultural possession.

The Pepsi Challenge continued eroding Coke’s position despite Diet Coke’s success. Coke’s own celebrity advertising began answering Pepsi directly, while Pepsi aligned itself with Michael Jackson and the image of a new generation. By the end of 1984, management decided to replace original Coca-Cola with a new formula that had performed strongly in extensive taste testing.

The hosts describe tests involving about 200,000 participants. New Coke could beat both Pepsi and old Coke in that setting. What the testing failed to capture was the decision consumers would actually experience: the familiar drink would disappear. Liking a sample and agreeing to the removal of an existing product are different questions. Management expected some protest, but believed it would be manageable.

Goizueta later said that Woodruff privately gave his blessing on New Year’s Day 1985. The episode explicitly notes that Goizueta was the other person present, so the account cannot be treated as independently witnessed. Woodruff died in March. The hosts place the formula change’s announcement the following month, after the leader most associated with preserving the product was gone.

The launch was already on the defensive when the press conference began. News leaked, Pepsi claimed victory, and Coke executives tried to avoid describing their decision as an imitation. They asserted confidence while fielding questions framed around defeat. Then letters and calls exposed the depth of attachment. Customers were reacting to a perceived loss of childhood, continuity, and ownership in the brand, not simply registering a score in a blind taste test.

Ben and David debate whether a second formula could have coexisted with the original. Management feared dividing Coke’s market share and giving Pepsi the number-one position. The hosts regard that concern as a poor reason to remove the old drink, and they disagree about how inevitable a full replacement really was. The failure should not be flattened into a tidy claim that any product change is wrong: Diet Coke had just shown the opposite.

14

July 1985 onward · 03:05:43

The return was stronger than the retreat

A reversal revealed the value of the attachment the company had underestimated.

Seventy-nine days separated New Coke’s introduction from the announcement that the old taste would return. On July 10, 1985, the company brought it back as Coca-Cola Classic while leaving New Coke available. The hosts also describe a contractual reason for the naming: treating Classic as another product offered an opening to revisit bottler arrangements. Even an emotional brand crisis ran through the inherited structure of the system.

Management expected Classic to satisfy a particularly vocal group. Instead, customers returned to it in overwhelming numbers, while New Coke became a small part of the market. The company had underestimated what it had removed and then underestimated how many people wanted it back. The original product’s resurgence made the disastrous change look, in retrospect, like a huge publicity event. The hosts emphasize that this was an unintended consequence, not evidence of a secretly planned stunt.

Berkshire Hathaway subsequently accumulated its famous stake. Ben and David discuss roughly $1.3 billion invested and the large dividends that eventually flowed from it, then question the long-run return in comparison with other investments. The official correction revises that assessment: Berkshire reinvested Coke’s dividends profitably as part of its capital-allocation strategy. A comparison that treats distributions as idle cash understates their role. The companion therefore does not reproduce the episode’s simple underperformance conclusion as a settled finding.

The advertising model evolved as well. Through the Columbia relationship, Coke connected with Michael Ovitz and CAA. Ovitz argued that fragmented television audiences needed more creative approaches than a handful of uniform advertisements. CAA won the account in 1992 and used its talent network to develop a larger variety of work under a consistent identity. The polar-bear advertisements became another enduring association with Christmas. Repetition did not require every individual commercial to be the same.

15

1990s–2010s · 03:16:42

The original business was hard to escape

Coke had distribution to offer, but no guarantee it would own the next successful category.

Consumers increasingly chose among sports drinks, bottled water, juice, energy drinks, and other alternatives to cola. Health concerns made the central challenge especially difficult: Coke needed to diversify without undermining the product that still supplied so much of its strength. Ben and David regard the company’s cautious response as understandable but costly. A business with unusually high margins can make a smaller, uncertain new category look unattractive until someone else has established it.

Powerade did not displace Gatorade. The hosts recount an announced Coke transaction for Quaker Oats and Gatorade that the board rejected, followed by Pepsi’s purchase. They compare this with the earlier opportunity to buy Frito-Lay. These misses helped make Pepsi’s collection of businesses different from the company one would infer by looking only at the two cola labels.

Monster produced a more cooperative outcome. Coke had opportunities to pursue it earlier but hesitated over valuation and the durability of energy drinks. In 2015 it invested more than $2 billion, exchanged certain energy and non-energy brands with Monster, and became its preferred global distribution partner. Coke gained a significant minority interest and a role in the growth rather than complete ownership. The distinction matters: Monster is not simply one more wholly owned Coca-Cola label.

Other acquisitions brought Vitaminwater, Smartwater, BodyArmor, and additional categories into the portfolio. Yet the hosts keep returning to Diet Coke and Coke Zero as unusually successful extensions of the original family. Meanwhile, the company continued to buy, reorganize, and refranchise bottling assets. The ambition was not permanent ownership of every production line; it was a stronger and more efficient network capable of carrying a broader portfolio.

16

The business at recording · 03:27:51

The company and the system are different sizes

The brand travels through a much larger organization than the listed company.

By the hosts’ recording-era account, Coca-Cola had reduced a portfolio of more than 500 brands to roughly 200 and had 30 brands exceeding a billion dollars in annual revenue. Some had been created internally, some bought at scale, and others grown after acquisition. Those brand-level claims should not be confused with the parent company receiving every dollar consumers spend on its products.

The official page also corrects the episode’s account of Sprite. Although it launched under the Fanta Beverage Company subsidiary, Sprite was intended as a standalone drink and predated Fanta Lemon-Lime. It was not merely that beverage renamed for the United States. Like the Fanta correction, this separates a shared corporate history from an inaccurate claim about the identity of a product.

The hosts describe about 2.2 billion servings daily, supported by roughly 200 bottling partners and 950 facilities. Their company-versus-system comparison puts the Coca-Cola Company at approximately $47 billion in revenue and 70,000 employees, against a wider system reported at about $175 billion and 700,000 employees. The system figure is a broader operating measure; it is not additional consolidated revenue belonging to Coca-Cola shareholders.

On the parent-company revenue, they cite roughly $10.6 billion of net income, net margins around 23%, and gross margins around 60%. Most revenue still came from sparkling soft drinks despite the diversification effort. North America and international markets both mattered, but a majority of the business extended beyond the United States. The local bottlers made the system local in ownership and employment even when the brand was associated with America.

Scale did not mean rapid recent growth. The hosts contrast the company’s extraordinary construction over its first century with growth of only a few percent annually in the later period. The question for shareholders was no longer merely whether the original drink was a remarkable business. It was how much growth a mature global system could produce, at what cost, and through which products. These are their recording-era figures and judgments, not subsequent financial updates.

17

The hosts’ analysis · 03:38:01

Branding made scale more valuable

A low price and high margins could coexist because the reach was so large.

Ben and David’s explanation begins with a huge market: people everywhere drink something every day. Coke built a product many people wanted, made it widely available, and repeatedly connected it with feelings beyond thirst. Local bottlers provided speed as well as capital efficiency. They could establish production and relationships in many places at once rather than wait for a single company to build every facility.

In their Seven Powers discussion, scale economies and branding work together. Manufacturing and advertising costs can be spread over enormous volume. Advertising reinforces demand; more visible consumption reinforces the identity that the advertising has created. The brand’s value is not expressed only through a large premium over Pepsi. Coke often keeps the selling price accessible, using latent pricing power to support volume and distribution.

They identify little consumer switching cost and no meaningful network effect from another person drinking a Coke. Process power is harder for them to assess from outside. They credit Pepsi, rather than the incumbent Coke, with repeated counter-positioning. A list of all seven powers would therefore misrepresent their conclusion: the episode argues for particular strengths, not every possible source of advantage.

The secret formula produces a real disagreement. One host sees value in the meaning attached to its exclusivity, even if the taste itself could be reproduced. The other thinks the formula’s standalone value is negligible without the trademark, marketing, and distribution. Their discussion shifts toward the bottling network as a potentially cornered resource. Neither a laboratory copy nor possession of a recipe would automatically recreate the system that makes it commercially valuable.

18

The hosts’ conclusion · 03:50:22

A durable system still needed decisions

Surviving a mistake is not the same as proving management never mattered.

The hosts return to the claim, associated with Warren Buffett, that Coke could be run by a ham sandwich. David points to the endurance of the original franchise, even after the New Coke disaster. Ben argues that there were periods when active management was essential: Pepsi had been taking share, and later the market moved away from full-sugar cola. The business could be extremely resilient without automatically delivering the growth an investor wanted.

One conclusion is organizational. Bottlers, retailers, restaurants, and other partners all had a reason to sell more of the drink. The same economics that made headquarters comparatively capital-light also gave local businesses something valuable to own and operate. The hosts see Woodruff’s insistence that participants should profit as central to the system’s durability, rather than a charitable addition to it.

The other conclusion is about repetition. Coca-Cola repeatedly associates the same basic promise with ordinary human occasions. The campaigns and media change, but refreshment and companionship remain recognizable.
