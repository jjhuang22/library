Spring 2026 · Acquired · Episode 3

Vanguard

The owners are the customers

A fired executive kept one seat at the table—and used it to build a company whose growth would belong to its investors.

A reading companion to Acquired, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal

Released · May 18, 2026Created · October 18, 2026About 25 minutes to read

The full episode’s story and concluding analysis, based on the supplied transcript and Acquired’s official corrections. Figures retain the recording’s timeframe.

01

1929–1951

The brother who got to go to college

Listening cue · 0:05:30

Jack Bogle was born into a prominent New Jersey family in May 1929, just before the stock-market crash. His grandfather’s business had helped form American Can; his great-grandfather had founded a mutual fire insurer. By childhood, that security was gone. In the hosts’ account, the Depression destroyed the family’s wealth, his father left, and his mother struggled with severe depression. Jack, his twin David, and their older brother Bud worked several jobs to support themselves and her. A paper route beginning at three in the morning offered Jack a rare stretch of peace.

The family retained connections even after losing its money. Scholarships brought the brothers to Blair Academy for their last two school years, where Jack flourished. But college presented a harder choice. The boys decided that two would keep earning and only one would go. Jack’s academic promise won him that opportunity. David Rosenthal, drawing in part on conversations with the family, describes a lifelong sense of obligation: Jack had to justify a chance his brothers never received.

Princeton did not begin with effortless distinction. Jack earned a D+ on his introductory economics midterm and finished the course with a C−. He nevertheless chose economics, kept working on scholarship, and needed a subject for his senior thesis. In the university library he encountered Fortune’s article about the emerging open-ended investment-fund industry. It offered both an intellectual problem and a possible livelihood.

02

1949–1965

Learning the business of other people’s money

Listening cue · 0:12:34

An open-ended fund pooled investors’ money in a portfolio without fixing the fund’s ultimate size. Customers could enter and leave; the manager could keep gathering assets. To a young man responsible for helping his family, a growing industry with substantial management fees was attractive. It also exposed a conflict that would occupy the rest of his life.

The hosts separate the fund from its management company. The fund held customers’ assets. A separate company earned a percentage of those assets for investment decisions, administration, and distribution. Brokers commonly collected a sales load of roughly 7.5 to 8.5 percent: a customer putting in $100 might start with only $91.50 invested. Annual management charges and portfolio trading costs added further drag. A manager could grow revenue by attracting more assets even without delivering superior results.

Bogle’s 1951 thesis argued that investment companies would become important and that lower costs could improve investors’ returns. It stopped short of proposing the full Vanguard structure. Nor do the hosts treat his observation that investors collectively constitute the market as proof that every early professional manager lacked an advantage. In the less professionalized market of the period, skilled managers could trade against relatively uninformed individuals.

Walter Morgan, a Princeton alumnus who had founded Wellington, read the thesis and hired Jack as his assistant. Wellington’s balanced approach combined stocks and bonds; its fund had about $150 million in assets when Jack joined in 1951. Morgan became a mentor and something like a surrogate father. Jack learned the company from the inside and rose toward its leadership.

The original conversation describes a direct 1965 handover from Morgan to Bogle. Acquired’s subsequent correction changes that sequence: Joseph E. Welch served as Wellington Management’s president and CEO from July 1962 until 1972; Bogle became executive vice president in 1965 and chairman in 1970. The important transition in the story remains Jack’s increasing authority at a firm facing pressure to change, but those titles and dates should follow the correction.

03

1958–1973

Buying the people who could beat you

Listening cue · 0:30:38

Wellington’s restraint was becoming a disadvantage in the market for new customers. Fidelity’s Edward Johnson had backed a different style. In 1958 he launched the Fidelity Capital Fund under Jerry Tsai, whose concentrated positions and rapid trading made him a star. By 1965, the fund had grown to roughly $340 million. Customers increasingly wanted conspicuous performance rather than the steadiness Wellington had sold.

Bogle tried to recruit managers who could compete in that environment. When hiring proved insufficient, he turned to a merger with four young Boston partners: Thorndike, Doran, Paine, and Lewis. Their Ivest fund had only about $17 million under management against Wellington’s roughly $2 billion. Yet the partners received 40 percent of the management company’s equity. Jack paid for the talent and the possibility of changing the firm’s trajectory, rather than simply for the assets they brought.

The partnership imported a style just before that style fell apart. The go-go market gave way to the early-1970s collapse. Wellington had already suffered from being too conservative; now, after taking more risk, it suffered again. By 1973, the Wellington Fund’s assets had fallen to about $483 million. The hosts stress that this was not solely investment loss: customers were also redeeming. For a company paid on assets, either mechanism reduced revenue while much of its operating cost remained.

The hosts initially say Ivest was shut down after its severe drawdown. Their official correction says it was reorganized and became Vanguard US Growth, retaining its 1959 inception date. They also correct the impression that Wellington had only offered balanced investing before the merger: Windsor began in 1958 as a contrarian equity fund, with John Neff taking over in 1964. The collapse still drove the partnership crisis; it did not erase those distinct fund histories.

04

1974–1975

A firing leaves one crucial chair occupied

Listening cue · 0:53:28

As clients lost money, Jack began questioning why the management company should continue taking its accustomed fees. He proposed mutualizing the operation: the funds could own the machinery that served them and eliminate the separate company’s excess profit. The idea was not a response to a popular campaign or a regulatory order. In the hosts’ telling, it was Jack’s own moral and commercial break with an accepted business model.

To the partners and public shareholders, it looked like destroying a valuable enterprise. The four Ivest partners assembled sufficient support, and on January 23, 1974, Bogle was fired from Wellington Management. But the management company and the funds were different legal entities. Jack still chaired the funds’ board. A detail that had usually seemed procedural became his route back.

The next day he asked that board to separate the funds from Wellington and run the operation for fundholders. The directors wanted alternatives and evidence. Jack returned with a 250-page feasibility report. Their eventual authorization was much narrower than his ambition: a subsidiary, collectively owned by the funds, could take over administration. Investment advice and distribution would remain with Wellington. Vanguard began as a back office, rather than a fully independent investment house.

That distinction matters to the hosts’ portrait of the founder. He believed in mutual ownership before the firing, but it was also his last viable route to continuing his career. Idealism and revenge reinforced one another. He named the new operation after the HMS Vanguard, whose image appeared among naval prints offered by an antiques dealer. The nautical name suggested steadiness and leadership; the hosts also hear a fired executive’s appetite for victory. Incorporation came in September 1974, with Vanguard opening its doors on May 1, 1975.

05

1974–1976

An investment product that required no picking

Listening cue · 1:13:03

Mutual ownership alone did not yet transform the customer proposition. Vanguard was doing administrative work that another company could have provided. The second revolution came through an apparently awkward restriction: Bogle was prohibited from offering investment advice. A fund that simply followed an index might require no such advice at all.

Paul Samuelson’s 1974 Journal of Portfolio Management article supplied an argument for that product. He had found no persuasive evidence of systematic market outperformance by active managers and proposed a low-cost vehicle that followed the market. Earlier institutions had experimented with indexing, so the hosts do not credit Bogle with inventing the concept. Vanguard joined a particular motivation, legal opening, and moment of technical feasibility.

The calculation was attractive once costs entered the comparison. Getting an average market result before fees could deliver a substantially better result than many active funds after fees. Ben’s example uses $100,000 invested for forty years: at a hypothetical seven percent annual return it grows to about $1.5 million; reducing the return to six percent through a one-percent annual charge leaves about $1 million. These are illustrative assumptions from the episode, not a forecast or a comparison with a literally cost-free Vanguard fund.

Bogle assigned Jan Twardowski to write the tracking software in APL on a time-sharing computer. He also negotiated an early S&P index license for $25,000 a year, in the hosts’ anecdote almost an improvised price. The future importance of index licensing was not apparent to either side. For Vanguard, what mattered was that the board agreed the proposed fund fitted its mandate: it would follow an existing basket rather than hire stock pickers.

In 1976, the First Index Investment Trust launched. To avoid its distribution restriction, Vanguard used an initial public offering. The target was $150 million. The actual raise was only $11.3 million. It could not even buy conventional round lots of every constituent. The hosts describe a portfolio of 280 stocks, combining large positions with a representative sample, and a part-time employee maintaining the operation at nights and weekends. The promise of easy, average ownership began with awkward, underfunded execution.

06

1976–1988

The revolution survives on the old business

Listening cue · 1:35:02

The first retail index fund did not immediately sell itself. Its initial expense ratio was around 0.68 percent, far above the tiny fees later associated with the product. Customers also withdrew money. A pitch that sounded like settling for average performance was particularly unappealing before scale made its cost advantage substantial.

In late 1977, merging the $58 million Exeter Fund into the index fund supplied a badly needed capital transplant. The majority of the early asset base thus came from a legacy active fund rather than fresh enthusiasm for indexing. The hosts acknowledge that they did not resolve every governance detail of that merger. They present its importance to survival, rather than a complete legal explanation.

Around 1981–1982, Vanguard obtained room to take distribution in-house by arguing it was eliminating distribution: it would stop paying brokers a sales load. Customers instead sent orders and checks directly. The accounting distinction did not remove the work. Vanguard now needed its own employees to advertise, communicate, and process purchases. It also gave up the powerful incentive that had made brokers sell other firms’ products.

The index fund finally reached $100 million in 1982, six years after launch and still below its original offering target. Another six years passed before it reached $1 billion. Meanwhile, money markets, fixed income, and active equities helped carry the organization. In particular, the hosts credit John Neff’s successful Windsor Fund with supporting overhead while indexing remained small. Vanguard’s later passive identity can obscure how much it depended on active investing in those early decades.

Bogle’s central commitment was therefore low cost, rather than an absolute rejection of active management. The fixed-income business made the cost advantage especially visible because the available returns were more constrained. In equities, the hosts also identify a behavioral attraction: an investor who has accepted the market’s result may feel less compelled to trade with every new piece of news.

07

1988–1996

The machinery finally begins to turn

Listening cue · 1:44:32

After the $1 billion milestone in 1988, growth accelerated. The hosts place the index fund around $10 billion in 1992, when Vanguard also launched its Total Stock Market Index Fund. Better computing and a larger asset base made owning the whole domestic market feasible. The second product broadened the same proposition: pay little to participate in the market, rather than paying a manager to promise something exceptional.

Vanguard’s fundholders owned the service company through their funds. When scale allowed it to operate more cheaply, there was no outside equity holder demanding that the saving become a dividend or a higher margin. It could be returned through lower fees. Ben and David connect this directly to their Costco idea of sharing scale economies, while noting that even Costco must still satisfy outside shareholders.

The episode traces the index fund’s expense ratio from roughly 68 basis points at launch to 59 in 1979, 50 in 1985, and 35 in 1987. These are historical figures for that product, not Vanguard’s contemporary average across all funds. Lower fees made the proposition stronger, growth spread fixed costs more widely, and further fee reductions became possible.

By 1996, the hosts put Vanguard’s total assets around $180 billion. The earlier sacrifices—limited distribution, a small fund, and costs spread over too little money—were beginning to pay off. Competitors also had to respond. A firm with other profit pools could offer a very cheap index product to retain customers, even without adopting Vanguard’s ownership structure.

08

1995–1999

A new heart and a company that had moved on

Listening cue · 1:49:06

Jack’s career had unfolded alongside a serious inherited heart condition, arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia. The hosts’ original wording repeatedly calls his episodes heart attacks; their correction explains that these were electrical rhythm events, rather than the coronary-blockage events usually meant by that term. The distinction does not lessen the danger or the pressure under which he worked.

By 1995, his doctors urged a transplant. Vanguard announced that John Brennan, who had joined as Bogle’s assistant in 1982 and later served as CFO, would succeed him. Jack spent 128 days in the hospital awaiting a suitable heart and continued working. Brennan formally became CEO on January 31, 1996; the transplant followed in February. Bogle recovered and lived another twenty-three years.

The recovery created an unexpected governance problem. The company had begun preparing for life without its founder. Bogle, still on the board, was again energetic and strongly attached to the spare original mission. Brennan wanted to expand products, invest in technology and marketing, and retain capable employees in a highly paid industry. The hosts stress that both men believed in serving fundholders. They disagreed about what that mission now required.

Those differences were larger than personal stubbornness. An organization that returns its economies to customers still needs to pay for future systems and service. A successor could preserve the central values while changing products and operations. The hosts use the transition to distinguish the focused founder who creates a company from the people who must scale it.

09

1992–2001

The easier product the founder did not want

Listening cue · 2:00:06

Nathan Most had approached Bogle in 1992 with a way for fund interests to trade on a stock exchange. For investors, an exchange-traded fund offered easier distribution and an observable trading price during the day. The hosts also describe structural tax benefits. Vanguard looked like a natural partner: its popular index could reach anyone with a brokerage account.

Bogle saw the same feature as a behavioral hazard. Intraday trading would tempt people to speculate, incur transaction charges, and abandon long ownership. Brokers could build businesses around the trading activity; the product could also be shorted. His objection followed his principles, but the hosts judge the decision to reject ETFs a mistake. The distribution opportunity would develop with or without Vanguard.

Most instead worked with State Street, whose SPDR became a major product. By 1999, Brennan’s team believed Vanguard had to enter the market. Bogle remained opposed. The board enforced its retirement-age rule against him at seventy, even as the hosts note that an older director remained. His departure from the board at year-end was a substantive leadership break, dressed in the language of retirement.

It was not a clean expulsion from the institution. Bogle had become a public symbol, with a statue on campus and a growing community of Vanguard enthusiasts. A research center gave him a continuing platform to write, speak, and advocate, while management gained room to act. Vanguard launched ETFs in 2001. The hosts see an unusually productive eventual compromise: his mission and public authority survived, while the company adopted a product he had resisted.

The hosts also trace indexing’s growing appeal to changes beyond Vanguard. As professional investors displaced less informed individual traders, active managers increasingly faced other professionals on the opposite side of a trade. Ben argues that this made consistent outperformance harder and reduced indexing’s relative disadvantage. David adds that indexing itself may have removed some of the inexperienced traders whom active managers had profited by trading against. They describe a changing competitive environment, rather than claiming that every manager’s opportunity disappeared.

Distribution incentives changed with it. A commission-paid broker benefited when customers traded; an adviser paid on assets had more reason to want customers’ wealth to grow. Low-cost index funds fitted that second relationship even though the adviser still charged a separate fee. The internet then made benchmarks and underperformance visible on demand, instead of leaving customers dependent on occasional statements. Online brokerage made funds easier to buy, while the dot-com boom drew more people into equities. Alongside those changes, the rise of the 401(k) gave individuals both responsibility for retirement investing and an accessible vehicle for doing it. The hosts treat these as reinforcing accelerants: Vanguard’s product needed a market, incentives, and channels capable of bringing it to a much wider public.

10

2008–2019

A crash makes the modest promise persuasive

Listening cue · 2:24:18

Index funds fell with the market in the financial crisis. They did not protect customers from the crash, and the hosts do not claim otherwise. The turning point was that much of active management failed to provide the protection its higher fees had seemed to promise. Investors who had trusted sophisticated strategies could now prefer a simpler offer: receive the market’s result at low cost.

Vanguard’s promise was particularly persuasive when public trust in Wall Street collapsed. It would not extract an outside owner’s profit from the relationship. Its own cost structure still had consequences. Ben points out that fees rose during the downturn as a smaller asset base had to support ongoing expenses; the mutual model was not a guarantee that the fee percentage could only move down.

Warren Buffett supplied a striking public comparison. He challenged the hedge-fund industry to beat the Vanguard 500 Index Fund after fees over ten years beginning in January 2008. Ted Seides accepted with a selection of five funds of hedge funds. The hosts report cumulative returns of 126 percent for the index fund against 36 percent for the selected portfolio. This was one defined comparison, with an additional fund-of-funds fee layer, rather than proof that no individual hedge fund could outperform.

The hosts describe Vanguard’s share of new mutual-fund money rising from roughly fifteen cents per dollar before the crisis to thirty cents afterward. It passed Fidelity as the largest mutual-fund manager in September 2010. From 2014 to 2019, they put Vanguard’s inflows around $1.2 trillion against roughly $500 billion for the rest of the industry combined.

Under Bill McNabb, Vanguard also built a human advisory offering accessible at much smaller account sizes than many traditional wealth managers required. Low-cost service could extend beyond choosing a fund. When Bogle died in January 2019, Vanguard managed about $5 trillion for twenty million clients. The hosts contrast his reported estate of roughly $80 million with the fortunes created at competing firms, then qualify the comparison: Vanguard might never have achieved its position had it been designed to capture a founder’s fortune.

11

The business at the 2026 recording

The product can win while someone else owns the relationship

Listening cue · 2:41:28

Fidelity and BlackRock did not disappear under Vanguard’s pressure. They found other ways to build valuable businesses around low-cost funds. That fact changed the hosts’ starting question. They had expected to ask how competitors survived such an aligned model; research made them ask where the same model might constrain Vanguard.

Fidelity reached customers through employer retirement plans and retail brokerage. A customer could hold a Vanguard ETF inside Fidelity and give Fidelity the continuing relationship. Ben describes doing exactly that. ETFs broadened distribution, but also loosened the connection between investing in Vanguard’s funds and using Vanguard’s own platform.

David raises the possibility that a rival could subsidize fund prices from other profit pools. Ben is less concerned about a difference between three basis points and zero than the difference between a good and a frustrating product experience. The hosts describe Vanguard’s technology and customer-service problems, especially during the pandemic, as a more tangible weakness. Running cheaply creates a hard question about how much to invest before a service failure damages trust.

BlackRock’s route was different. It acquired iShares from Barclays in 2009 and built an extensive ETF range, with institutional and international reach beyond Vanguard’s predominantly American individual base. The hosts see a commercially successful market for specialized products, even when those products depart from Bogle’s preferred simplicity. The low-cost core and the rest of the financial industry could thrive together.

Salim Ramji, formerly head of iShares, became Vanguard’s first outside CEO following the 2024 announcement. The hosts discuss expanding advice, fixed income, retirement services, technology, and access to private assets. Their questions remain open: how should present customers pay for future investment, and can private-market access be offered in a meaningfully Vanguard-like way? Private companies must choose their investors; buying a publicly available index basket does not face that same access constraint.

12

The circle closes

The adversary remained a partner

Listening cue · 3:04:43

The original Wellington partnership also survived. After the separation, the Ivest partners took the management company private, rebuilt it, and developed a generational ownership arrangement in which younger partners gained equity as senior partners aged out. The hosts put the firm around $1.3 trillion in assets at the time of the episode, all within an active-management business.

More surprisingly, Wellington continued managing the Wellington Fund inside Vanguard, with that fund around $110 billion in assets in the hosts’ account. The service company and outside investment adviser could still work together. Bogle and his former partners reconciled over dinner in the early 1990s. The story ends with a durable working relationship rather than the annihilation the nautical name had seemed to promise.

At the 2026 recording, the hosts describe Vanguard itself as managing about $12 trillion, including around $2 trillion actively managed. They put its average mutual-fund and ETF expense ratio at 0.07 percent against an industry average of 0.44 percent, with roughly fifty million investors and twenty thousand employees. The comparison is across the broader fund industry, not exclusively passive competitors, many of which had already matched very low index fees. These are episode-time measures, not updated figures.

13

The hosts’ concluding analysis

The structure makes the promise credible

Listening cue · 3:08:23

Why did other industries not produce a Vanguard? Ben and David first point to access to capital, the inherited active business that supported the lean years, and the operating leverage of finance. But they also recognize that these conditions explain how a founder might keep a company privately owned, rather than why the founder would give the economics to customers. Their final explanation rests heavily on Bogle’s unusual situation and willingness to make a decision without conventional founder upside.

Their original discussion overgeneralizes about cooperatives staying small. Acquired’s official correction names large agricultural cooperatives, including Land O’Lakes, CHS, Ocean Spray, Organic Valley, and Tillamook. These are producer-owned rather than customer-owned businesses, but they limit the claim that member ownership prevents substantial scale. The hosts’ narrower point about Vanguard’s combination of capital access, economics, and founder incentives is more defensible than a claim that large cooperatives barely exist.

In their Seven Powers discussion, the hosts adapt a profit-based framework to an organization that does not seek excess profit. They ask what protects its market position instead. Scale economies are central: a new fund starting from little capital cannot easily cover its operations at Vanguard’s tiny percentage fees. Counterpositioning is also unusually strong in their view. Matching a structure designed to surrender profits challenges a conventional owner’s reason for operating.

Switching costs produce a useful disagreement. David initially thinks ETFs eliminate them; Ben points to capital-gains taxes that a taxable investor could trigger by selling an appreciated fund to buy another. They distinguish moving a customer’s brokerage relationship from selling the underlying Vanguard holding. The tax consequence is conditional on the account and gain, not a universal charge for moving any account.

They also credit branding built through Bogle’s public work, Buffett’s endorsement, and the Bogleheads community. Ben sees a mission-driven culture as a possible source of process power. They do not identify meaningful network economies or a cornered resource. Rather than forcing every category to fit, they return to the ownership system that makes lower fees a continuing purpose.

Success brings responsibilities beyond fee competition. The hosts consider whether passive ownership could weaken price discovery or encourage companies with common owners to stop competing. Ben is skeptical of both arguments: marginal active traders still set prices, and a larger arbitrage opportunity would attract trading. Both find it implausible that common index ownership alone would make competing executives cooperate. These are their judgments, not settled answers to the economic debate.

They treat voting and accountability as a more substantial question. Large fund complexes exercise voting rights across much of corporate America. Investor-choice programs can offer different ways to express preferences, but the episode does not resolve how that responsibility should be allocated if ownership concentrates further. David also raises the systemic implications of an operational failure at a very large provider. Neither host regards the discussed issues as an existential threat to passive investing.

14

Risks, quintessence, and the close

A commodity sleeve, and the person who made it possible

Listening cue · 3:30:58

Ben’s concluding formulation is that Bogle recognized an opportunity to make market exposure a commodity: the relevant competition could be on cost, scale, and trust rather than a promise of unique stock-picking genius. David refines it. Bogle did not show that every investment product was interchangeable; he separated out a large commodity sleeve while leaving a thriving market for differentiated strategies, some of which do deliver superior results.

David’s final emphasis is personal. Indexing might have arrived as computing improved, but the hosts doubt that competitors would have pushed prices to the same floor without an organization willing to operate without outside profits. Ben asks where competitive pricing might otherwise have settled; David’s answer is that the usual minimum acceptable profit is not zero. Their admiration is for a specific institutional choice, rather than a claim that an index idea alone inevitably created Vanguard.

The conversation closes with the difficulty of holding individual long-term winners through deep drawdowns, Bogle’s charitable book proceeds, and the hosts’ personal recommendations. They discuss their new Wall Street Journal columns, Ben’s computer upgrade, Michael MacKelvie’s sports videos, a father–daughter cinema trip, and Brooks shoes named Vanguard. Those closing diversions leave the business argument where it belongs: lower costs matter over long periods, and an organization’s owners help determine who receives the benefit.
