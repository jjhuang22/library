Sixty-five thousand invitations to borrow

Acquired reading companion · Fall 2023

VisaA promise
between banks

A California lending experiment became a global agreement among rivals. Building the agreement was only half the work; making it function at a shop counter was the other.

Released November 27, 2023 · Created October 19, 2026
About 34 minutes to read · Origins, infrastructure and complete concluding analysis
Based on Acquired: Visa, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01

September 1958 · 00:05:37

Sixty-five thousand invitations to borrow

Bank of America mailed a new financial relationship to Fresno.

The cards arrived before their recipients had asked for them. In September 1958, Bank of America sent roughly 65,000 unsolicited BankAmericards into Fresno, California. People had to discover what this unfamiliar object entitled them to do. Merchants had to discover whether accepting it meant they would be paid. A coordinated launch could create both sides of a market at once, but it also created opportunities for confusion and abuse.

Bank of America was unusually positioned to attempt it. Descended from the Bank of Italy, it had built relationships with ordinary consumers and immigrant communities rather than concentrate only on large industrial clients. California allowed statewide branch banking, while banks could not operate across states. A bank with branches throughout a large, populous state could reach households and merchants on a scale that most local banks could not.

Its consumer-loan business supplied a practical motive. A customer buying a washing machine or refrigerator might take out a small installment loan, generating paperwork and servicing work for a relatively modest balance. The bank wanted a repeatable relationship that could cover many purchases. A reusable card might consolidate that work and make borrowing easier.

This was not yet Visa, and the institution taking the credit risk was the bank. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal begin with that distinction because it remains essential to the entire story. Visa eventually became the network connecting financial institutions. It does not become the consumer’s lender merely because its name appears on the card.

02

Before the bank card · 00:11:11

A ledger behind every purchase

Convenience already existed, but it was trapped in separate relationships.

Long before plastic cards, merchants extended credit to customers they knew. A local store could maintain a ledger, add up a household’s purchases, and ask for payment at the end of the month. This simplified shopping but tied the merchant’s finances to the honesty and solvency of particular people. A customer could run up separate tabs all over town without any shop seeing the whole picture.

Checks solved some problems and introduced others. Moving a paper claim between distant banks required transport and reconciliation. A merchant accepting it might receive less than its face value after the cost and uncertainty of collection. The hosts use that earlier discount to explain why payment acceptance had an economic price before modern card fees.

Fuel companies and retailers developed their own cards. Standard Oil of Indiana sent 250,000 unsolicited cards to customers in 1939, the hosts recount. Such programs could strengthen loyalty and simplify a familiar transaction. They did not automatically create a general-purpose payment system: the same card could not necessarily pay the grocer, the hotel, and a competing service station.

Retailers sometimes tried shared arrangements. Cooperation offered a wider acceptance network and a better view of a borrower’s obligations, but someone still had to operate the common machinery. Competitors had different interests, and administrative complexity increased with participation. The central problem was already recognizable: broader usefulness demanded coordination among parties that wanted to keep their own customers.

03

1949 onward · 00:17:26

A card for the business dinner

Diners Club began with a concentrated, valuable use case.

Diners Club found a promising place to connect buyers and sellers: Manhattan business entertainment. A businessperson could pay at participating restaurants, collect expenses in one statement, and enjoy the status of belonging. Restaurants gained access to desirable customers who might spend more freely when their employers ultimately paid the bill.

The familiar origin story has founder Frank McNamara forgetting his wallet at dinner and calling his wife to rescue him. The hosts explicitly explain that this story was fabricated. It is memorable marketing, not reliable evidence of the moment the business was conceived. The underlying insight was less cinematic and more commercial: a concentrated group of restaurants and expense-account diners could make a useful network before acceptance was universal.

The card was a charge card. Its statement had to be paid in full rather than become a revolving bank loan. The merchant discount could be five to seven percent, according to the hosts, far above the rates that cause arguments today. Diners Club had to build acceptance, acquire members, and process the transactions. Its restaurant partners were paying for those costs and for access to the audience.

That audience also made the service less price-sensitive. Someone paying a personal grocery bill might question every fee. Someone entertaining on the company’s account could care more about convenience and prestige. The initial market made the economics possible, while the need to recruit merchants one by one limited expansion.

04

1850 origins; 1958 card launch · 00:23:48

An existing network enters the room

American Express did not have to build every relationship from scratch.

American Express arrived with an infrastructure of its own. The hosts trace its origins to the express-mail business associated with the same entrepreneurial world as Wells and Fargo, then to financial services and travelers’ checks. Travelers’ checks put money in the company’s hands before it had to redeem the claims, creating valuable float.

They also created relationships with travelers and establishments that served them. When American Express launched its charge card in 1958, it could use that distribution rather than begin with an unknown name and a blank merchant list. The hosts cite roughly 700,000 members in the first year or two, approaching a scale that had taken Diners Club much longer to assemble.

That was a different advantage from Bank of America’s. American Express could exploit an established travel and payments franchise. Bank of America could exploit consumer banking relationships and extend credit. Both could make a card more useful than a small independent entrant could, but they were bringing different assets and incentives into the market.

The distinction would matter decades later. A company controlling its own relationships on both sides could deliver a more coherent experience. A system recruiting many independent banks could spread much faster, if it could persuade them to operate together.

05

1958–1966 · 00:29:12

The bank survives its own experiment

A convenience product became an unsecured lending business.

BankAmericard combined two propositions. Customers could use one account for many purchases, and they could pay the balance or let it become a loan. The bank no longer needed to originate an entirely separate installment agreement whenever a household bought another appliance.

The convenience was inseparable from a change in risk. A refrigerator loan could be secured by the refrigerator. General consumer purchases on a card were harder to repossess, and mailing cards indiscriminately did not produce a carefully screened borrower pool. The hosts describe about $20 million in early losses and delinquency around twenty-two percent, several times the bank’s experience in traditional lending. Their discussion mixes fraud and credit problems; these figures should not be read as separate, neatly audited categories of loss.

A smaller institution might have abandoned the experiment. Bank of America could absorb the losses, refine its operations, and keep expanding. Within the first year, the hosts say, the program reached roughly two million cardholders and twenty thousand merchants in California. By 1961, it had become profitable.

The bank kept that success quiet. Outsiders heard the early failure stories and assumed the business was unattractive. Only about ten new cards appeared between 1960 and 1966 in the hosts’ account. Once Bank of America began licensing its system and revealed that it worked, the imitation accelerated dramatically: approximately 440 cards appeared between 1966 and 1968. What had looked like an expensive mistake became a business other banks urgently wanted.

06

1966–1968 · 00:40:42

Sell the name, leave the machinery

The franchise spread faster than its operating system.

Bank of America created the BankAmericard Service Organization in 1966. A bank could pay an entry fee of about $25,000 plus a share of transactions to participate. By 1968, a couple of hundred banks had joined and roughly six million cardholders carried the program’s cards. Barclays brought it to Britain. The bank could earn from a much larger territory without opening branches across prohibited boundaries.

Inside California, a purchase could remain within Bank of America: its customer bought from a merchant whose banking relationship also sat there. With independent banks, the customer’s issuing bank and the merchant’s acquiring bank could be different institutions. Money, information, and obligations had to cross between them. This was the shift from a closed arrangement toward an open network.

The franchise did not supply a complete solution. Merchants produced paper sales drafts; acquiring banks mailed claims to issuing banks; participants struggled with different discounts and procedures. Bank of America had sold entry into a market without furnishing all the infrastructure needed to operate it. To the frustrated licensees, it was rather like buying a restaurant franchise and then being left to work out how to make the food.

The card’s name nevertheless solved a real acceptance problem. Competing regional associations, including the organization that became MasterCard, could present a confusing patchwork of local brands. A traveler needed to recognize a mark that meant the card would work. BankAmericard offered that reassurance. The hosts recount a California landscape explanation for its blue, white, and gold colors as a story about the branding, rather than establish the precise provenance of every design choice.

07

October 1968 · 00:55:47

A committee nobody expected to matter

Dee Hock treated a complaint meeting as permission to redesign the system.

The licensees demanded a summit in Columbus, Ohio. Their programs were costly to run, the interchange arrangements were chaotic, and Bank of America appeared insufficiently concerned. Sending two midlevel marketing representatives to face them only intensified the grievance. A committee to examine the problems seemed a convenient way to absorb the anger and postpone a difficult decision.

One participant saw a different opening. Dee Hock represented the National Bank of Commerce in Seattle, later associated with the Rainier name. Rather than merely catalogue complaints for Bank of America, he proposed examining the entire arrangement and designing something that could actually work. The committee had no obvious power to impose this vision. He acted as though the absence of a functioning solution gave it a reason to try.

Hock was not a conventional grandee of banking. Raised in poverty in rural Utah during the Depression, he had an associate degree, a habit of self-education, and a difficult relationship with authority. He had been fired from consumer-finance jobs for insubordination. The hosts describe a restless reader and debater whose lack of deference was both a liability in ordinary organizations and an asset at this moment.

His ambition was far larger than improving a credit-card franchise. He imagined an organization for electronic exchange of value across institutions and countries. It would have to let competitors cooperate without requiring any one of them to surrender its entire business to another. The improbability of the person carrying that argument is part of the story: an obscure banker was preparing to ask one of the country’s largest institutions to relinquish control.

08

1968–1970 · 01:11:40

The value of a smaller share

Every participant had a right that could obstruct the larger network.

Bank of America owned the franchise. Hock wanted it to exchange sole ownership of that asset for participation in something much larger. The choice was not simply generosity versus greed. The existing system was becoming dysfunctional, and a functioning global network might be worth more to the bank even if it could not command the whole enterprise.

The licensees also had privileges to surrender. Territorial exclusivity made a particular bank’s membership valuable by excluding rivals. Yet a network that aimed to become ubiquitous needed broad admission. Hock could not promise every incumbent a protected local monopoly and simultaneously recruit the institutions required for global reach.

This made persuasion unusually demanding. He had to convince parties that already possessed something valuable to amend existing contracts. He was not recruiting a group with no alternatives into a fresh startup. Bank of America’s assistance mattered: gaining the support of an influential first group of banks made agreement by the others more plausible.

The hosts compare the result with a sports league whose teams compete fiercely inside a shared enterprise. The analogy explains the incentive, not a guarantee of harmony. Hock still had to create rules that made cooperation credible after the inspiring speeches were over.

09

Sausalito; 1970–1971 organization · 01:15:00

An enterprise its members could not sell

The structure joined ownership, participation, and a common operating law.

At an offsite in Sausalito, Hock and the committee worked toward a constitution for the new organization. Its first principle was a for-profit, non-stock membership corporation. “Non-stock” did not mean nonprofit. Members could participate in the economic benefits, with ownership and contribution linked to the value they brought through the network.

But membership was not an ordinary security that a bank could sell to a third party. The right was tied to participation rather than a tradable piece of a holding company. The hosts call it a reverse holding company: the participating banks owned the organization above them, while it did not own their independent banking businesses.

The second principle allowed members to propose and vote on rules, budgets, and fees. A high threshold—eighty percent in the hosts’ description—made casual changes difficult and encouraged consensus. This was not a claim that Hock lacked practical executive authority. He remained a forceful leader inside an unusually participatory legal structure.

The remaining principles gave cooperation a defined purpose and required members to accept the organization’s operating law, including future changes made through its procedures. Early restrictions on joining rival networks were part of that original arrangement, though they did not survive indefinitely. Agreement meant accepting an ongoing process, not signing one static promise.

Getting every member to agree took years of meetings. Bank of America helped convene them, and influential early signatories helped the commitment spread. The hosts also describe a Justice Department letter allowing the cooperative arrangement. That episode should not be inflated into a general exemption from antitrust law: the organization’s later legal disputes are a major part of the story.

10

International organization from 1972 · 01:25:18

A globe with two halves

National banking champions had reasons to hesitate.

The domestic agreement did not settle the international question. Banks in Europe, Canada, Latin America, and Asia had their own ambitions. A strong national institution might reasonably believe that control of its home market was worth more than a minority place in somebody else’s global organization. The future benefits of cooperation were uncertain; the local rights being surrendered were concrete.

Hock again gathered the prospective participants in Sausalito. The hosts tell a story of negotiations close to failure and a personal gesture designed to change their tone. He presented gold cufflinks whose two halves formed a globe. Their inscriptions invoked the will to succeed and the grace to compromise. The symbolism made an abstract institutional bargain into something the participants could carry with them.

The account is about emotional persuasion alongside economic design. Hock also involved spouses in board gatherings, believing that their presence would encourage more generous and civil behavior. The hosts find these methods striking; describing them does not establish that such tactics are universally appropriate or sufficient to govern an institution.

The international organization, IBANCO, created a way for local banks to join a wider system while retaining local relationships. That was the network’s expansion engine. It did not have to reproduce a single American bank in every country. It needed institutions already serving those markets to agree that a shared rail would make their own businesses more valuable.

11

1976–1978 · 01:30:24

A passport that still belonged to the bank

Visa supplied one acceptance mark and room for many local identities.

BankAmericard was an awkward name for a genuinely international system. It foregrounded one American bank when the organization was asking institutions everywhere to regard it as partly theirs. The name Visa, taking effect in 1977 in the hosts’ chronology, suggested recognition across borders without requiring a translation of Bank of America.

The hosts recount a naming reward in which multiple employees appeared to have submitted Visa and everyone received a fifty-dollar check. They enjoy the irony of paying for the name of an electronic payment system by check. The anecdote belongs to their account of the rebrand, rather than supply evidence of a uniquely identifiable inventor.

The new mark left space for a bank’s own identity and, later, affinity relationships. Visa promised acceptance; the issuer could distinguish its customer offer. That division let the common brand grow without swallowing all the independent institutions that distributed it.

The transition also intensified competition. Banks encouraged people who already carried a card to apply for the newly named Visa, sometimes capturing customers from other member banks. The hosts cite a twenty-percent rise in participating banks and a forty-five-percent rise in active cards in 1977–1978. Master Charge had previously led in banks and cardholders; the common rebrand helped Visa gain momentum.

The comparison with American Express makes the tradeoff clear. A closed network could control more of the customer experience. Visa could recruit banks with existing customers, merchant relationships, and local capabilities. Its experience could be less uniform, but its distribution did not require one company to do every job itself.

12

1975 and the ensuing competition · 01:39:20

Two roads become the default

Allowing banks onto both networks helped stabilize the pair.

Banks wanted to participate in both Visa and MasterCard. Hock opposed the change, arguing that it would reduce the pressure to build genuinely different payment systems. A bank could satisfy its customers by joining the two existing networks rather than investing in an alternative.

The practice became known as duality. It looked like more competition because banks and customers could choose between two marks. Hock’s concern was that it would also create a durable duopoly: two nearly universal systems, both supported by the same institutions, leaving little incentive for those institutions to fund a third.

The hosts regard that prediction as perceptive. They do not argue that Visa and MasterCard ceased competing. Their point is that competition between the two could coexist with extraordinary collective protection against new entrants. That distinction becomes essential when they later apply Seven Powers.

The organizational achievement had produced permission to cooperate. It had not yet made the transactions fast enough, reliable enough, or cheap enough to deliver the full promise. For that, the story returns to the computers and telephone lines behind the counter.

13

Manual authorization; BASE I · 01:49:13

Twenty minutes at the checkout

The first system connected the banks, not every cash register.

A merchant did not always authorize a small card purchase in real time. Below a floor limit, it could accept the card and the associated risk under the system’s rules. Above that threshold, an employee might telephone the merchant’s bank, which telephoned the customer’s bank, which checked the account and sent an answer back along the chain. Business hours and time zones complicated the process. A purchase could take twenty minutes to approve.

The immediate task was to automate that interbank conversation. Bank of America had been working on a technology venture with American Express, later involving the rival network too. The hosts describe Justice Department objections to the combined arrangement. They also preserve Hock’s alternative account that its technical approach was bad. They do not pretend these explanations are a single uncontested cause.

An outside proposal from IBM looked too expensive and slow. Hock recruited Aram Tatoulian from TRW to lead an internal team, promising resources and an unusually demanding schedule. In roughly nine months, they assembled the authorization system that became BASE I. It required computers, communications, participating banks’ installations, and training, not just an elegant central program.

The distinction in scope matters. This first system digitized communication between banks. It did not instantly put a connected terminal at every merchant or eliminate every telephone call at a checkout. It removed a critical layer of manual coordination and established an infrastructure on which the next layers could be built.

14

BASE II and resilient operations · 01:58:26

Stop mailing each other the money story

Authorization and settlement were different engineering problems.

Approving a transaction did not move the final money. Banks still had piles of paper claims to exchange and reconcile. As participation expanded, the number of possible bank-to-bank relationships made the paper system increasingly awkward. The same cooperative structure that admitted more members multiplied the need for common processing.

BASE II computerized clearing and settlement. Rather than physically moving every individual sales draft through the old process, the system could calculate what institutions owed after offsetting payments in opposite directions. The hosts compare this with the development of automated clearing elsewhere, but explicitly say they found no evidence of coordination with the Federal Reserve’s parallel work.

They describe settlement shrinking from roughly a week to overnight batch processing and savings of about $15 million in labor and postage in the first year. These are the episode’s reported historical benefits, not a claim that final settlement became identical to instantaneous checkout authorization.

Reliability was another design problem. An engineer warned Hock about the physical vulnerabilities of the California data-center location: surrounding hazards and earthquake exposure were not solved by good software. Hock sent a team east to build a second operating site. Both locations would run concurrently instead of leaving one as an idle backup that might fail when finally needed.

The hosts speculate that this was the first or among the earliest examples of that approach. Their uncertainty is preserved here. The supported achievement is a deliberate move toward continuous, redundant operations for a service whose failure could stop commerce far beyond its own offices.

15

Magnetic stripes, terminals, and communications · 02:06:26

The last distance was across the counter

Open standards needed hardware and an incentive to install it.

The remaining gap was between the merchant and the acquiring bank. Cards needed to be readable electronically, and merchants needed affordable equipment to read them. The hosts contrast an open magnetic-stripe standard with proprietary approaches associated with Citi. A common system could spread without letting one participant control every piece of the stack.

Visa specified the kind of inexpensive terminal it needed and invited hardware vendors to build it. Verifone became the crucial supplier in the account. The aim was a small device costing less than about five hundred dollars, distributed to enormous numbers of merchants. Visa was organizing an ecosystem rather than manufacture every terminal itself.

Merchants still needed a reason to adopt. Lower fees for electronically handled purchases gave them an economic incentive to buy and install equipment. The network and banks could share some of the savings instead of retaining every efficiency improvement. This changed behavior at the point where the infrastructure actually met its users.

Communications came partly from an unexpected source. CompuServe had capacity serving consumers whose usage peaked outside the main business day. Visa rented available capacity to connect merchants. Existing infrastructure could serve another market with a different daily rhythm.

The hosts cite an eighty-two-percent reduction in chargebacks in a pilot, a result tied to that test rather than a universal fraud statistic. With more transactions authorized electronically, the old below-floor-limit opportunities for abuse diminished. Tiny messages could now travel through a largely digital system at very low additional cost, while the organizational promise finally became practical at scale.

16

1984 departure; 1986–1988 Olympics campaign · 02:15:37

Make paying with it feel desirable

Acceptance was an advantage; stigma was still an obstacle.

Hock left in 1984, and Visa’s expansion did not end with him. One remaining obstacle was cultural. A card associated with borrowing for ordinary necessities could feel less prestigious than American Express. The hosts play a 1993 news report about Burger King accepting cards, including a customer who found it sad that someone might need credit to buy fast food. The report shows how slowly everyday attitudes changed; it does not mean fast-food cards or electronic payments began that year.

Visa’s marketing team chose to compete with American Express over the meaning of carrying a card. John Bennett, its marketing leader, had spent twenty years at Amex. When the Olympics offered a global sponsorship, Amex declined the proposed opportunity. Visa saw a way to connect its broad acceptance with travel, aspiration, and international belonging.

The hosts describe Visa paying about $17 million for the 1988 winter and summer Olympic rights and committing another $23 million in advertising. The important spend was the combination: an exclusive association amplified through media. Being the card accepted at the Games supplied the basis for a campaign emphasizing places American Express could not go.

The number of people physically paying at an Olympic venue mattered less than the worldwide audience seeing the association. Visa could turn its distributed merchant network into a prestige claim rather than apologize for being the everyday credit card. At the 2023 recording, the hosts describe the Olympic relationship as extending through 2032; this companion does not update that contract from later sources.

17

2005 litigation; 2008 IPO · 02:23:37

The banks turn membership into a security

Public ownership required a way to allocate unresolved legal costs.

For most of its history, Visa was a profitable membership corporation without ordinary tradable stock. It was not a charity waiting to discover commercial ambition. Going public changed the ownership and financing arrangements of an already enormous business.

A merchant class-action suit begun in 2005 complicated the transition. Investors would need to understand who bore the costs if litigation over the card system produced a large settlement. The hosts describe separate share classes: bank owners retained exposure through Class B, while the publicly sold Class A shares received protection from that legacy burden.

Competition also pushed toward a conventional public-company structure. MasterCard was going public, American Express already was public, and stock could help compensate employees and support transactions. The benefits were broader than raising operating cash for a business that already generated substantial profits.

Visa’s 2008 offering arrived during the financial crisis. The hosts describe roughly $18 billion raised at an initial valuation around $90 billion, with proceeds going to the bank owners rather than functioning as a new primary capital injection into Visa. That is their characterization of the transaction. It gave institutions liquidity when the banking system badly needed it.

By the recording, large investment institutions had become major shareholders and the original banks were much smaller owners. The company’s market value exceeded that of its former owners individually. The cooperative network had become a publicly owned operator, while the banks remained essential participants in the business underneath.

18

The hosts’ illustrative 2023 US credit purchase · 02:27:36

Follow one hundred dollars

The merchant discount is not all Visa revenue.

A card purchase connects four participants: the cardholder, the merchant, the issuing bank, and the acquiring bank. Visa operates the network connecting them; it is not a fifth side. This follows an explicit correction on the official episode page to the hosts’ wording in the recording.

The merchant’s system sends an authorization request through its acquiring side and the network to the issuing bank. The answer returns along that path. Settlement is a separate step. The customer’s account and the merchant’s bank balance belong to financial institutions, not to a Visa lending business.

For a large US merchant accepting a domestic credit-card purchase, the hosts illustrate a $100 sale producing about $98 for the merchant. Most of the two-dollar discount goes to the issuer: about $1.60 in their example. Roughly twenty cents supports the acquiring and technology side, and roughly fifteen to twenty cents supports the network. These are rounded amounts and ranges, not a universal schedule that must sum with penny-level precision.

Interchange primarily compensates the issuing bank. The network’s assessment and related fees are different charges. The acquiring bank and payment-technology providers do their own work onboarding and serving merchants. Calling all of it a Visa fee hides both where the money goes and which institution supplies which service.

The issuer funds rewards and bears consumer credit exposure and associated costs. Visa’s attraction is a very different economic profile: passing small packets of information through a large existing system. Smaller merchants, debit purchases, regulated rates, foreign transactions, and premium cards can all produce different economics. None should be mechanically priced using this single example.

19

Rewards, affinity cards, and merchant resistance · 02:34:13

An envelope with an absent negotiator

The participants allocating the economics do not share identical interests.

The hosts describe the merchant discount as an envelope of value that the institutions inside the system can divide. Higher interchange can let an issuer offer richer rewards. Richer rewards can attract desirable cardholders and encourage spending. That gives the issuer and network a way to describe higher fees as commercially beneficial to a merchant, even when the merchant would prefer lower acceptance costs.

A merchant is not necessarily at the table when that bargain is designed. Premium cards can arrive carrying more expensive economics, and refusing them may mean refusing customers. Visa’s common acceptance promise gives the system negotiating power precisely because a retailer wants access to the people carrying its cards.

Affinity partners add distribution. A bank can share economics with a company whose customers it wants, while the partner promotes the card. The hosts use Amazon and Chase as an example of this mixture of issuer, retail relationship, and rewards. That arrangement does not imply that every reward is funded solely from one simple interchange line item.

For consumers who pay in full and receive rewards, the result can feel excellent. Banks gain customers and lending opportunities; networks gain transactions. A retailer trying to create a cheaper alternative must persuade customers to abandon benefits they already enjoy. Replacing the technical rail is only part of that challenge.

The hosts also discuss who pays indirectly. If merchants raise common prices to cover acceptance costs, cash users and people without rich rewards can subsidize cardholders who receive them. Their criticism is distributional as well as competitive. A system can generate useful convenience while allocating its costs unevenly.

20

2023 financial snapshot · 02:44:59

A very small message, repeated billions of times

Payment volume, revenue, and profit are different quantities.

The hosts cite about $14 trillion of annual payment volume and more than 190 billion processed transactions, alongside roughly 4.1 billion cards. Those amounts describe activity on the system. They are not Visa’s revenue. The company’s reported net revenue in the discussion is about $29 billion and net income about $15 billion.

The resulting net margin, around fifty percent, astonishes them. They also cite gross margins near ninety-eight percent as an expression of the extraordinary economics of the network. These are the figures and characterizations in the 2023 episode, not independently reconstructed accounting measures or a later financial update.

Scale does not mean the organization has no people or operating obligations. The hosts mention roughly 27,000 employees, sixteen thousand participating banks, six data centers, and a presence across about two hundred countries. Their argument is that the data moving through each ordinary transaction are remarkably small relative to the value represented.

They calculate payment-volume growth at about 17.3 percent annually from 1971 to 2022, a fifty-one-year interval. That is growth in payments flowing through the network, not a stock investor’s return and not the rate at which profit must grow forever.

The banks’ protection of customer relationships also constrains the system. They do not want the common operator to become the owner of every customer detail. The hosts connect that boundary with resistance to Hock’s ambitions in debit and ATMs before his departure. What looks technically natural can threaten the institutions whose cooperation makes the network possible.

21

Fees and additional services in 2023 · 02:54:42

Visa wants a swipe; the bank may want a balance

The network’s incentives differ from those of its issuers.

The hosts turn from the elegant infrastructure to the burden of borrowing. They discuss widespread revolving balances and interest rates around twenty-two percent at the time. Those interest payments go to lending institutions; they are not Visa’s ordinary transaction revenue. The official episode page corrects the hosts’ historical location for the usury-law change to South Dakota, not North Dakota.

That difference changes incentives. Visa benefits when more value moves across its rails, whether a cardholder pays the bill in full or carries a balance. An issuer can benefit from lending income as well as transaction-related economics. The two institutions are partners without being identical businesses.

The hosts cite US Visa and MasterCard system fees growing from about $33 billion in 2012 to about $93 billion at recording. The comparison concerns fees across the associated system, not Visa’s own net revenue. Higher payment volume is part of the explanation; they question how much additional economic value the system should capture as commerce shifts onto its rails.

Visa also had roughly $6 billion of value-added services in their discussion, included within its revenue rather than added on top of the $29 billion. Fraud tools, analytics, digital services, and related offerings could create new ways to charge as pressure increased on familiar fee categories.

The debit story illustrates the power of participant incentives. Starting with direct access to deposit accounts might seem simpler than inventing consumer credit. But banks were protective of core customer relationships. Credit created an acceptable initial opening; debit expanded later as customers demanded the convenience of using familiar cards for money they already held.

The hosts’ Seven Powers discussion · 03:00:38

Power against whom?

Visa and MasterCard are harder to displace than to distinguish.

The hosts deliberately analyze Visa and MasterCard together when asking what protects their extraordinary returns. They find it difficult to show that one has an overwhelming, durable advantage over the other. Both can nevertheless possess powerful defenses against a new payment network. Combining them for this analysis is a choice of competitive reference point, not a claim that they are one company.

Brand produces a disagreement. Visa historically gained from its common mark and Olympic positioning. Yet a consumer choosing a card today may care more about a bank, a rewards program, or an affinity partner than the network logo. One host emphasizes that the brand once helped Visa pull ahead; the other questions how much it now independently determines a purchase decision. The chapter does not resolve that disagreement by declaring branding either irrelevant or decisive in every era.

Scale economies and network economies are clearer. The infrastructure spreads its costs across immense activity. The four participant groups reinforce one another: more cardholders attract merchants, merchant acceptance attracts cardholders, and banks recruit and serve both. The banks are not simply passive intermediaries; their distribution helps the network grow through other networks.

Switching between Visa and MasterCard is much easier than leaving card acceptance altogether. Duality lets banks use both. The hosts do not identify an equally strong, separate process-power explanation. They regard Bank of America’s original position as historically difficult to reproduce, but do not turn that early circumstance into a claim that the present company is a new counter-positioned entrant.

Their image is a toll road with two broadly similar routes. Competition between the routes can persist while either is extraordinarily hard for outsiders to bypass. The strength they admire—and the economic concentration they question—comes from that combined structure.

23

The hosts’ synthesis · 03:09:08

A constitution and a computer system

Neither half of the achievement could substitute for the other.

The hosts return to the novelty of a business created by institutions that kept competing. Bank of America had to surrender control; licensees had to surrender exclusion rights; national champions had to accept a wider system. The achievement was not simply inventing a clever set of incentives on a blank sheet.

They describe the cooperative membership structure in deliberately provocative political language, mixing democracy, communism, and capitalism. The useful substance is more concrete: shared rules and common ownership let independent profit-seeking banks create something none could operate alone. Those comparisons are their characterization of governance, not literal claims about a national political system.

The technical accomplishment deserves equal attention. Agreeing to a constitution would have meant little if every purchase still required a long chain of calls and mailed paper. Authorization, net settlement, resilient operations, readable cards, terminals, and communications turned institutional consent into a service people could actually use.

The two stories reinforced one another. Governance recruited the institutions that made the technology useful; the technology made membership valuable enough to sustain cooperation. The hosts’ admiration rests on the combination, including the often overlooked engineers, rather than give all credit to one charismatic organizer.

Value creation and value capture · 03:14:18

How much should the toll be?

Merchants can value the system and still object to its price.

The hosts question whether fees reflect the ongoing cost of moving a payment or the difficulty of escaping the network. A very efficient rail can keep charging far more than its marginal processing cost when replacement requires coordinating customers, merchants, and financial institutions at once.

A coffee shop paying a percentage plus a fixed per-transaction amount can spend a striking share of a small purchase on payment acceptance. A large retailer with thin margins may surrender a substantial fraction of gross profit even when the fee looks small beside total sales. A percentage of revenue and a percentage of profit are different comparisons.

That does not make the service valueless. Guaranteed authorization, fewer uncertain checks, less cash to count and protect, and remote purchasing all produce real benefits. The hosts argue that ecommerce would not have developed in the same way without payment systems and logistics capable of serving strangers at a distance. They treat this as a major contribution, not merely an excuse for the fee schedule.

Nor can the card percentage be applied to every conceivable purchase. Real estate, cars, and very large invoices often travel through different arrangements because a card-like percentage becomes prohibitive. A large total payments market is not proof that its entire value can be charged at retail-card rates.

The unresolved question is how much of the benefit the network and its partners should retain. Visa can have created enormous value and still participate in an allocation the hosts regard as insufficiently disciplined by competition.

The 2023 bear case · 03:19:20

The ways around the two roads

A maturing core and different national rails could limit the opportunity.

The first risk is maturity. Moving consumer spending from cash and checks to cards supplied a tailwind for decades. As card penetration rises, that particular source of growth becomes less powerful. The hosts distinguish a slowing conversion opportunity from a prediction that Visa’s business must suddenly shrink.

Closed-loop systems offer another path. China’s large platforms and domestic infrastructure show that digital payments need not always develop through the same card model. Governments can favor local rails for cost, control, and security reasons. A foreign market can represent a large volume of payments without becoming an equally large Visa opportunity.

Real-time bank-payment systems—including the examples the hosts discuss in India, Brazil, Britain, and the United States—could move money without the familiar card chain. But the hosts also stress product features: refunds, disputes, trust, and ease of use matter to merchants and consumers. A cheap transfer rail does not automatically supply the whole retail experience.

Not every wallet displaces the network. Funding a Starbucks account with a card still sends activity onto card rails. A phone payment can change the visible interface while leaving the underlying network in place. The important question is where the transaction actually travels, not whether the plastic card stays in a pocket.

Cross-border transactions are especially profitable in the hosts’ account, making alternative international connections strategically important. Countries linking domestic systems could affect future growth even before replacing the installed base of card purchases.

26

Apple Pay and the hosts’ unresolved debate · 03:24:32

Whose brand is on the phone?

A powerful interface can be a partner and a potential threat.

Apple Pay makes the card less visible at the moment of purchase. The customer sees a phone and a wallet interface, while Visa or MasterCard may continue processing underneath. That can increase convenience and transaction volume for the existing networks even as it weakens consumers’ awareness of their brands.

The official correction is explicit about fees: banks pay Apple when Apple Pay is used. The recording’s suggestion of an extra swipe fee charged to the merchant is not carried forward here. The commercial relationship should not be confused with an extra merchant charge simply because Apple supplies the interface.

The hosts then speculate about a more ambitious future. Could Apple combine its customer relationship with merchant-side technology, perhaps through an acquisition of a company such as Square or Verifone, and build a more closed system? These are hypothetical paths in their discussion, not announcements of acquisitions or evidence of a settled Apple plan.

One host sees a company of Apple’s size as capable of coordinating a challenge that would defeat smaller entrants. The other emphasizes the regulatory and institutional consequences of moving deeper into financial services, as well as the availability of many other large opportunities. They disagree about how attractive that step would be.

The debate returns near the episode’s end rather than resolving neatly. The prize could be enormous and the interface strategically powerful. Entering the regulated financial arena could also create burdens that a technology platform might prefer to avoid. Neither prediction is presented as the inevitable outcome.

27

The bull case: durability and tokenization · 03:29:01

A wallet can carry the old rails further

A new interface does not necessarily mean a new network.

The bull case begins with endurance. Many people have proposed replacing card systems; the networks have continued growing through successive waves of technology. The hosts take that history seriously without treating it as a guarantee or an instruction to buy the stock.

Digital wallets can make the existing system easier to use. If a phone transaction still runs over Visa, the network can benefit from a shift that makes its own logo less prominent. The bear case about brand and the bull case about volume can therefore both be plausible at once.

Tokenization strengthens this argument. Instead of giving a merchant the raw identifying card details, a transaction can use a representative token. The hosts discuss security and privacy benefits and the possibility of additional Visa services built around that architecture. The company can adapt its role within a changing interface rather than insist that every payment look like swiping plastic.

The hosts also identify a control tradeoff. With raw card information, they argue, merchants and processors had more flexibility over how to route a payment. Visa-controlled tokens can create more dependence on its proprietary services. Security and privacy benefits can therefore coexist with greater network control, additional high-margin products, and less routing flexibility. This connects to their broader playbook observation: consumer-facing improvements can also increase transactions, margins, or lock-in.

This does not remove every vulnerability. It illustrates why a technological change that seems to bypass a card at the surface may expand the relevance of the underlying network. Deciding whether a product threatens Visa requires following the payment through the system.

28

The bull case: B2B, payouts, and cross-border · 03:32:01

The payments that were never card purchases

Large opportunities need different products and different economics.

The hosts cite roughly $200 trillion in overall payment flows compared with about $20 trillion on cards, framing the gap as opportunity rather than forecast. Within that broader market they discuss around $120 trillion of business-to-business activity and $30 trillion of business-to-consumer payments. These are market-flow estimates in the episode, not Visa revenue or promises that the company will capture them.

Business invoices are different from retail purchases. A million-dollar payment cannot simply inherit a typical consumer-card percentage without changing the economic bargain. The hosts therefore describe purpose-built products and other payment services, not just mailing more credit cards into a larger addressable market.

Visa Direct illustrates a different direction of movement: sending funds out rather than only taking payment in. Insurance claims, gig-worker payouts, and refunds are among their examples. A customer might receive money through the network without having made an original purchase from the sender.

Cross-border activity supplies another potential source of growth, especially because the hosts describe it as disproportionately profitable. Yet the earlier discussion of domestic rails and international links still applies. A market can be attractive and contested at the same time.

The episode ends with a small historical reversal. At recording, the hosts observe that Bank of America’s contemporary BankAmericard runs on MasterCard. The institution that began the story could choose the other road. That is an apt closing image for their larger argument: Visa became enormously powerful by building a shared system, while never eliminating the independence and competing incentives of the institutions inside it.
