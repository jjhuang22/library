Qualcomm

Sharing the air.
The long road from a theoretical signal to an indispensable business.

Based on Acquired: Qualcomm — Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal
Released November 15, 2022 · Created Unpublished
Full episode · approximately 25 minutes to read

The story and analysis of the November 2022 recording, told from its perspective. Technical comparisons are conceptual; future outcomes remain the hosts’ expectations.

01 / 1930s–1960s

An unlikely inheritance

A movie star, an information theorist, and a future hotel manager.

Before Irwin Jacobs built Qualcomm, he prepared to return to his family’s restaurant. Born in 1933 in New Bedford, Massachusetts, he excelled at mathematics and science. His guidance counselor saw little local future in either and steered him toward Cornell’s hotel management school. Jacobs followed that advice for a year and a half. Then he discovered electrical engineering: there was a way to make a living from the subjects he loved.

In Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal’s telling, the detour mattered. Accounting and business were useful preparation for the entrepreneur he became. After transferring, Jacobs went on to MIT, completing his doctorate in 1959 under Claude Shannon. Shannon’s information theory supplied a way to reason about a communication channel’s limits: how much information could travel through a medium, given noise and the properties of the signal? Jacobs would spend his career trying to bring practical systems closer to those limits.

The episode reaches back further for another part of this inheritance. In the hosts’ account, actress Hedy Lamarr escaped her marriage to Austrian arms dealer Friedrich Mandl in 1937, reached Hollywood, and wanted to help the Allied war effort. Radio guidance could steer a torpedo, but an adversary transmitting on the same frequency could interfere. Lamarr and composer George Antheil proposed moving the transmitter and receiver together between frequencies according to a synchronized pattern.

Their 1942 patent described a frequency-hopping system, with player-piano mechanisms furnishing an improbable model for synchronization. The hosts describe 88 possible frequencies corresponding to piano keys. The practical problem was making the two ends change together with sufficient precision. The idea preceded the electronics needed to make such systems broadly useful; this was no neat wartime invention-to-victory story.

Lamarr and Antheil’s spread-spectrum work and Shannon’s theory enter the story as intellectual ancestors, not as the same invention as Qualcomm’s later cellular system. One suggested spreading a transmission rather than confining it to a single frequency; the other gave engineers a language for information and noise. Jacobs was among the people able to turn such ideas into working communications equipment.

MIT kept him as a professor. He taught digital communications and coauthored a textbook with a fellow faculty member. A 1964 sabbatical at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory brought him to California and into the orbit of Andrew Viterbi. An invitation to help establish electrical engineering at UC San Diego then gave Jacobs a reason to move his family west. Academic work and occasional consulting began to grow into something larger.

02 / Late 1960s–1985

Learning to sell the whole solution

Satellite expertise becomes a business before it becomes a cellular company.

Jacobs, Viterbi, and UCLA professor Leonard Kleinrock formed Linkabit to organize their consulting. The initial ambition was modest: pool assignments, manage the work more efficiently, perhaps hire help. Kleinrock soon departed; the hosts delight in the fact that his subsequent work helped create the early internet. Jacobs and Viterbi continued building a concentration of communications talent around NASA and defense projects.

Satellite links rewarded precisely their expertise. Bandwidth was scarce, so extracting more useful communication from a narrow channel mattered enormously. Their work exposed them to military applications of spread spectrum. As more professors and graduate students joined, the consulting arrangement became an engineering business. In 1971 Jacobs took a UCSD sabbatical to organize it. He never returned to his university job.

There was a business constraint alongside the technical one. Linkabit supplied differentiated engineering while established defense contractors held the prime contracts and captured more of the economics. Competing directly with those entrenched firms was difficult. Commercial work offered another route. The episode’s unexpected early customer is Walmart, whose private satellite network connected its stores and headquarters. Linkabit also developed scrambling technology used in pay television, including HBO.

The company sold to M/A-COM for $25 million in 1980. Over the ensuing years its operations grew to roughly 1,500 people, as the hosts describe it. After a leadership change, Jacobs, Viterbi, and colleagues left in 1985. They had already earned more than they once expected. They also saw a communications market whose limitations were becoming visible in everyday life.

Analog cellular service had intense demand and scarce capacity. Expensive car phones required substantial power; carriers could not accommodate everyone who wanted one. Dividing geography into cells had helped, but within each cell there were still only so many channels. The former Linkabit engineers believed digital communications could do better. In July 1985, seven founders met at Jacobs’s house and formed Qualcomm: “Quality Communications.”

The opportunity was much bigger than inventing a clever radio technique. Carriers needed base stations, handset manufacturers needed compatible electronics, regulators controlled spectrum, and an industry needed to agree on what to build. Qualcomm could see the eventual destination. It could not afford to construct that entire world at once.

03 / 1985–1988

Sharing the air

A different answer to the question: who gets to speak?

Qualcomm began with consulting, as Linkabit had. Work with Hughes on a proposed mobile satellite network prompted a new application of spread-spectrum ideas. The proposal itself did not become the business they hoped for, but the engineers developed code division multiple access, or CDMA, for allowing many conversations to share a communications medium.

Ben explains the alternatives through a dinner party. Frequency division is like giving each conversation its own room. Time division puts conversations in one room but gives each a turn, switching quickly enough that a digital receiver can reconstruct continuous speech. Code division instead resembles people speaking different languages simultaneously, with each listener attending to the language they understand.

This is an analogy, not a literal description of radio decoding. In the episode’s explanation, digital messages are encoded with different codes, combined in a shared transmission environment, and distinguished at the receiving end. The promise was to use the available spectrum more effectively. Qualcomm applied for its foundational cellular CDMA patent in 1986, well before entering the cellular market directly.

The difficult part was doing the work quickly enough and in a small enough device. Voice had to become digital information, be encoded and transmitted, then decoded without a disruptive delay. A base station might have substantial computing resources; a handheld phone had a much tighter budget for power, size, and cost. The hosts credit the founders with anticipating where semiconductor progress would be by the time they shipped, rather than judging the system only by what chips could do immediately.

Three ways to share a medium

ABC

Separate rooms: each conversation has a different frequency channel.

Conceptual comparison of the hosts’ dinner-party analogy. Letters identify conversations; spacing, color, and area do not measure capacity, bandwidth, or signal strength.

A code alone did not remove the engineering obstacles. The near-far problem was especially daunting: a nearby phone could drown out one farther from the tower. Qualcomm needed fast adjustments to transmitted power so signals could coexist. Timing and the emerging availability of GPS were further parts of the system the hosts discuss. Each practical solution also became an opportunity for additional intellectual property. The original patent was the beginning of a portfolio, not the complete machine.

04 / 1988–1989

The road that paid for the radio

Before the handset breakthrough came a satellite service for trucks.

The interim business arrived through OmniNet, a customer seeking a satellite network for commercial trucking. Qualcomm’s engineers could connect vehicles on the road to the companies dispatching them. The work fit their satellite background and reused commercial relationships from Linkabit. Walmart became an early customer; David thinks Schneider was the first.

Building useful technology did not mean that customers wanted to operate it. Walmart was willing to integrate sophisticated systems itself. Other trucking companies and retailers were less interested in constructing their own dispatch and messaging operations. Qualcomm learned to sell an operated service: the customer would receive a working solution rather than a pile of components.

Money was tight enough that Qualcomm merged with its customer in 1988. The combination raised $3.5 million but diluted the existing owners by half, according to the episode. Ben emphasizes the discomfort of giving up so much when the product was already well along. The choice was made with the financing available then, not with knowledge of the revenue that would follow.

Omnitracs reached market at the end of 1988 and produced $32 million in revenue in 1989. This was hardware and service revenue, with substantial costs, rather than a modern software subscription windfall. Still, the hosts describe a business that doubled repeatedly over its first several years. It gave Qualcomm cash flow and a base against which it could borrow or raise equity.

That bridge changed what the founders could attempt. The satellite service was a real business in its own right, but it also financed their much larger cellular ambition. By the time an opening appeared in the American standards process, Qualcomm had more than a patent and a prediction.

05 / 1988–1993

Making disbelief expensive

Carrier economics create a reason to try an improbable technology.

In September 1988, American industry associations published performance requirements for the move from analog first-generation cellular networks to digital second-generation systems. Europe was already moving with GSM and time division. Established suppliers had a technology people could understand and trust. Qualcomm was asking the industry to believe several difficult new things at once.

The founders saw an opening in two features of the American process. First, they believed time division would struggle to deliver the requested capacity. Second, industry standards were not a government mandate to use a particular technology. They went to Washington to confirm that a carrier could choose another approach if it met the requirements. That distinction let Qualcomm pitch individual operators rather than first winning the whole standards establishment.

The episode calls the conflict the wireless “holy wars.” Skepticism was reasonable: a system could look elegant in theory and fail in real time. Qualcomm’s persuasive argument was economic. The hosts describe CDMA as promising roughly three to five times the capacity of time division on a given spectrum allocation. A carrier with more subscribers on the same infrastructure could earn more revenue or reduce prices. This was the hosts’ account of the historical proposition, not a universal performance guarantee.

PacTel Wireless put up $1 million to fund a prototype in February 1989. At a San Diego demonstration that November, vans carried the equipment around the city while a base station operated at Qualcomm. The prototype phone was so large that Ben likens it to a miniature refrigerator with a handset. Before the demonstration, an engineer signaled Jacobs to keep speaking: the GPS system needed rebooting. The former professor could oblige.

The demonstration worked. Skeptics then asked for something harder than San Diego’s relatively accommodating geography. Qualcomm took the system to Manhattan and demonstrated it successfully in February 1990. NYNEX signed, followed by Ameritech that August. Proof was accumulating one operating environment and one customer at a time.

International expansion supplied another opening. Countries building new networks had fewer commitments to old systems. South Korea adopted CDMA as a mandated standard; David recalls it reaching close to 40% of Qualcomm’s revenue for a period. The hosts relish the contrast: freedom from a mandatory standard helped Qualcomm enter America, while a government decision helped it establish itself in Korea.

Qualcomm went public in December 1991, raising $68 million. In 1993 the American industry associations formally accepted CDMA as a second standard. Further public offerings brought in $150 million and later another $500 million. Winning belief had required demonstrations. Turning belief into a functioning industry would require much more capital.

06 / 1990s–1999

Build everything, then let it go

The temporary manufacturer finds its lasting place in the chain.

A willing carrier still faced a circle of dependencies. Would the infrastructure exist? Would consumers have handsets? Would anyone make the chips needed for both? Qualcomm had the intellectual property, but a carrier could not buy a patent portfolio and turn on a network.

The company therefore helped supply the whole chain. It partnered with Northern Telecom on base-station equipment and Sony on handsets. The Sony venture was majority owned by Qualcomm; David is less certain that the infrastructure venture had the same ownership split. The practical purpose was clear: when a carrier asked whether the rest of the system would be ready, Qualcomm could answer yes.

Silicon was the decisive fourth piece. These early chips were radios’ encoding, decoding, and power-management electronics, not the broad smartphone processors later sold as Snapdragon. The emergence of independent foundries allowed Qualcomm to design chips while outsourcing fabrication. It could keep the differentiated engineering without having to finance and operate a semiconductor factory.

A dependency map, not a flow of revenue. Qualcomm initially participated across the system to make adoption possible.

Networks went live in 1995. Revenue rose from $383 million that year to $814 million in 1996. Yet manufacturing and joint ventures absorbed capital and profits, and the stock did not reward that growth as the hosts might have expected. Building enough of an ecosystem to get started had created a burden on the business Qualcomm wanted to become.

In March 1999 it sold its infrastructure business to Ericsson as part of a settlement between the rivals. About a thousand of Qualcomm’s 9,500 employees transferred. Employees unhappy about losing Qualcomm stock options subsequently sued. In December, Kyocera bought the handset business. Qualcomm could now concentrate on chip sales and technology licensing.

The hosts describe an extraordinary dot-com-era stock surge and a subsequent collapse, warning that buying into the enthusiasm could still produce a large loss. Their spoken year and return figures sit awkwardly within the surrounding chronology, so this companion does not use them as a precise investment-return series. The strategic transition is the consequential point: temporary manufacturing had enabled adoption, and divesting it released the enduring businesses from its capital demands.

07 / 2000s–2015

A patent portfolio has to keep moving

A successful standard is valuable; its successor can make it obsolete.

Third-generation networks spread CDMA-based approaches much further, including through different technical families beneath the old GSM-versus-CDMA distinction. The move toward mobile data enlarged Qualcomm’s opportunity. It also made the business’s dependence on continuing invention harder to ignore. Patents expire, and standards change.

Paul Jacobs, an electrical engineering PhD who had worked his way through Qualcomm, succeeded his father as CEO in 2005. That year Qualcomm bought Flarion for $600 million. Its patents concerned technologies that would become important to fourth-generation networks, which used OFDMA rather than CDMA. The hosts describe an initial effort to defend CDMA’s position followed by a pivot toward the newer approach.

They give Paul’s era a mixed assessment. The early push into the internet of things arrived before the market developed as hoped. But Flarion helped renew the patent position, and Snapdragon expanded Qualcomm from radio electronics into mobile systems on a chip. More of the computing inside a phone became part of the company’s offering. By the episode’s 2022 perspective, Snapdragon was the familiar choice in high-end Android phones.

Expansion did not always reward Qualcomm. A reflective display technology called Mirasol led it to build a $2 billion fabrication facility, according to Ben, without finding the expected customer demand. A 2009 loss in its dispute with Broadcom required a $900 million payment. Steve Mollenkopf, previously chief operating officer, became CEO as the company continued to grow but faced mounting friction with its ecosystem.

In 2015, a licensing dispute in China and activist pressure from JANA Partners exposed the question beneath that friction: should licensing and chips remain together? One business generated unusually high-margin cash; the other required continuing investment. Qualcomm resisted a split. Ben returns to the argument in the closing analysis: predictable licensing income could fund research that produced both new chips and new patents.

08 / 2016–2019

The customer who wanted an exit

Technical leadership meets the limits of what customers will tolerate.

Qualcomm’s position invited conflict from several directions. Its proposed acquisition of NXP became caught in the tumult of 2016–2018 and did not complete. In 2018 Broadcom sought a hostile takeover at a $117 billion valuation. Ben describes financing involving $106 billion of debt and contrasts Broadcom’s profit-harvesting approach with Qualcomm’s continuing research investment.

The Trump administration blocked that deal on national-security grounds. The hosts regard Qualcomm’s independence as a major positive outcome. They also relay an interpretation from people they spoke with: Qualcomm’s government relationships and lobbying helped it make its case. That is their assessment of influence, not a finding establishing why every official acted.

Paul Jacobs also sought to take Qualcomm private during this upheaval and was removed from the board. The family no longer had a board member by the recording. The company’s next phase would be shaped as much by the demands of giant customers as by its founding lineage.

In 2017 Apple and the US Federal Trade Commission sued over Qualcomm’s use of its modem position and licensing terms. Ben frames the Apple conflict as a case of pushing value capture too far. An iPhone’s application processor was a separate component from its cellular modem. Designing its own main processor did not free Apple from modem purchases or the patents involved in wireless standards.

The episode’s fee reconstruction is deliberately uncertain. Ben discusses a $7.50-per-phone licensing amount, possible additional charges, and modem-price estimates ranging from $10 to $30, with $18 mentioned in litigation. These are different estimates and categories, not a single invoice to add up with confidence. At Apple’s scale, any of those modem prices represented an enormous relationship. Ben’s high-price scenario would make Apple as much as roughly a third of Qualcomm’s handset-chip revenue.

Other terms made the dependence more uncomfortable. Ben describes an agreement requiring a billion-dollar payment if Apple used a competing supplier and a request that Apple oppose WiMAX. Apple’s legal argument centered on obligations to license standard-essential patents on fair, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory terms. The hosts describe a dispute over what those obligations should mean; they do not supply a complete account of every antitrust ruling.

Intel offered an escape route, and iPhone XS and XR models used its modems. But Apple faced the possibility of falling behind on 5G. In 2019 it settled with Qualcomm, continued using its radios, and made a licensing payment Ben ultimately puts at approximately $4.5 billion for a six-year arrangement. Apple subsequently bought Intel’s modem operation to pursue its own solution.

That did not resolve the long-run contest. From November 2022, the hosts report that Qualcomm expected to supply the following year’s iPhone but was warning shareholders to anticipate little subsequent Apple chip revenue. This was an expectation at the time of recording. The episode’s point is the difficulty of replacing Qualcomm’s engineering and the strength of Apple’s incentive to try; it supplies no later outcome.

09 / 2019–2022

More bands, more computation

The radio becomes more complex while the processor needs to stand apart.

Fifth-generation networks were not one simple radio upgrade. The hosts contrast the remarkable speeds and limited reach of millimeter-wave service with other spectrum subsequently included under the 5G label. They distinguish genuine new capabilities from carrier rebranding and acknowledge the limits of their own technical explanations.

For Qualcomm, this variety created engineering work. A phone had to deal with different bands and conditions, with sophisticated electronics adapting the radio path. The RF front end became a substantial business. This companion treats that discussion as an explanation of complexity and opportunity, not as a physics model or a promise that every 5G connection offers a particular speed.

In 2021 Qualcomm acquired NUVIA for $1.4 billion. Founded by former Apple silicon engineers, including a former chief architect of the A-series chips, NUVIA offered a route to a custom CPU design. Ben distinguishes licensing an ARM instruction set and designing a processor from using an available ARM CPU design. Snapdragon’s other differentiated components did not make its then-standard CPU design unique.

The acquisition could help Qualcomm approach Apple’s processor performance and furnish an alternative for other device manufacturers. The hosts consider laptops and other devices, but Ben is wary: Qualcomm had repeatedly struggled when it ventured far from phones. Under CEO Cristiano Amon, management’s larger growth story emphasized automotive, the internet of things, and the RF front end.

The scale was already substantial in the figures presented in the episode: approximately $44 billion in annual revenue, comprising $37 billion in chips and $7 billion in licensing. Ben gives earnings-before-tax margins of about 34% and 69%, respectively, qualifying his identification of the margin measure. He reports revenue growth of 32% and earnings growth of 47% year over year. These are the recording’s snapshot, not current financial statements.

The valuation nevertheless prompted a question. At roughly $120 billion, Qualcomm was worth around the same amount as Broadcom’s earlier offer and, in the hosts’ comparison, its dot-com peak. It was larger by chip revenue than NVIDIA but valued much less highly. Ben saw greater potential in AI than in the “intelligent connected edge.” David came away with deeper appreciation for how much engineering each wireless advance demanded.

10 / The hosts’ closing analysis

How durable is the position?

A brilliant execution of the rules still leaves a difficult next act.

The most obvious power is a cornered resource: patents. The hosts also see a historical network effect between compatible handsets and infrastructure, each reinforcing the other’s adoption. They are less definite about how much of that advantage persists. Scale economies are clearer to Ben: the cost of designing a new Snapdragon can be spread across an enormous customer base, making a direct attack difficult.

David proposes a more unusual power in Qualcomm’s golden years: the working process of a particular group of people. He compares their collaboration to a creative brain trust. Many companies emerged from the San Diego ecosystem around Linkabit and Qualcomm, but he sees less of a comparable diaspora of giant successes than from Silicon Valley’s foundational semiconductor firms. He reads this as evidence that the original group and circumstances were unusually hard to reproduce. Ben finds the inference interesting; it remains David’s argument, not a demonstrated causal proof.

Why keep chips and licensing together?

  1. Licensing incomePredictable, high-margin cash
  2. Research and developmentFinanced experiments and engineering
  3. New chips and intellectual propertyProducts to sell; patents to license
  4. Renewed licensing baseCash available for another cycle

Ben’s account of Qualcomm’s stated rationale. This is a proposed reinforcing relationship, not a guarantee that every research investment succeeds.

The bear case begins below Qualcomm’s premium position. MediaTek supplied much cheaper modems and systems on a chip using standard ARM CPU designs; Ben thinks it had surpassed Qualcomm in units shipped. NUVIA could help distinguish Qualcomm’s CPU, but the competition made that distinction necessary. Meanwhile the advertised growth businesses were outside phones, where Qualcomm’s history gave the hosts reason for caution.

Customer resentment was the other danger. Apple, Samsung, and others had strong reasons to build alternatives after years of uncomfortable dependence. A competitor did not have to recreate every part of Qualcomm to erode its leverage in chip negotiations. Ben separates that threat from the licensing business, which he still regards as formidable. The continuing litigation in China, South Korea, the EU, Taiwan, and elsewhere added uncertainty.

Yet the lawsuits could also support the bull case: Qualcomm had repeatedly found ways to preserve very profitable economics despite challenges. And the new businesses were more than presentation slides. Ben cites about $2 billion in automotive revenue, $4 billion from the RF front end, and more than $7 billion in IoT. All are within the $37 billion chip segment; they are not extra revenue to add to the company total.

Management’s forecasts deserve a different status. Qualcomm framed opportunities around $100 billion and a much broader $700 billion connected-edge market. The hosts regard those addressable-market narratives as imprecise. Real growth in existing segments did not establish that Qualcomm would capture the much larger markets invoked in its pitch.

The early playbook had an apparent contradiction. Qualcomm needed to be the best supplier while creating credible alternative suppliers. A phone company would hesitate to depend on an entirely new standard served by only one vendor. Helping competitors become good enough reduced that adoption risk, while keeping some implementation knowledge private could preserve Qualcomm’s lead.

That balance extended to intellectual property. Ben estimates a portfolio of roughly 17,000 patents, but emphasizes that Qualcomm did not patent every invention. Disclosure and protection made licensing possible; trade secrets preserved advantages in consulting, implementation, and close engineering relationships. Access to the patent portfolio and access to the engineers’ accumulated know-how were different products.

Timing reinforced the model. David observes that generations of wireless technology advanced within a patent’s protected life. By the time core CDMA patents expired, the industry had moved on. Ben adds the converse: missing the early-1990s adoption window might have left Qualcomm unable to survive until the next major transition. The opportunity came in distinct industry cycles rather than as a permanently open market.

Keeping licensing and chips together made sense to Ben if licensing cash genuinely financed research that renewed both businesses. He also voices the less flattering possibility: a cash-rich division could subsidize comfort rather than productive invention, mentioning Qualcomm’s aircraft and its reputation as a pleasant place to work. The flywheel argument needed execution; it could not justify every expenditure by itself.

At the broadest level, Ben sees Qualcomm as an exceptional execution within the patent system’s rules. Those rules deliberately permit temporary exclusivity and substantial value capture. Whether the balance best serves innovation is a debate the hosts leave open. Their admiration for the company’s engineering and strategy does not dissolve the customers’ objections.

For David, the story also complicates an investor’s instincts. Qualcomm required a sequence of breakthroughs that could each have sounded like “and then a miracle happens.” This team had unusually informed confidence. But both hosts remember credible teams with similarly ambitious plans that failed. Qualcomm is an exception worth understanding, not a recipe that makes the risks disappear.

There is no numerical grade: the hosts say they have stopped grading for now. Ben’s final assessment is conditional. The previous decade had been exceptionally favorable for mobile monetization. Another successful decade would require Qualcomm to be right about IoT, automotive, and the connected edge; merely repeating the handset playbook could leave its best days behind it.

David offers the strongest version of that next act. Cloud computing creates enormous demand for moving data in and out, and much of the connection to the world is wireless. Supplying that connection could be an immense opportunity. Whether Qualcomm could capture value there as effectively as it had in phones remained an open question. The Lisbon recording ends with that uncertainty intact: extraordinary past execution, substantial existing businesses, and a future that still had to be earned.
