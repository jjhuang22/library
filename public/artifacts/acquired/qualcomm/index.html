Qualcomm

The invisible architecture

How a group of communications engineers turned a disputed radio idea into a business at the center of the mobile world.

A companion to Acquired: Qualcomm. The history and the hosts’ analysis through the November 2022 recording. Chapter navigation follows the reading, without inferred audio times.

01 · 1930s–1971

The long road to a radio company

An actress, an information theorist, and an unlikely engineering student.

Before Qualcomm existed, the ingredients of its business were scattered across film studios, military research, and university laboratories. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal begin with Hedy Lamarr and composer George Antheil, whose wartime frequency-hopping proposal sought to make radio-guided weapons harder to jam. The hosts describe synchronized player-piano rolls as the surprising model for coordinating transmitter and receiver. It was an early spread-spectrum idea; it was not yet a practical cellular network, and Qualcomm did not invent spread spectrum itself.

The next ingredient was Claude Shannon’s information theory: a way to reason about how much information a noisy communication channel can carry. The episode uses a crowded conversation to make the problem tangible. A receiver needs enough distinguishable signal to recover a message from noise. Improving a wireless system therefore means more than shouting louder. Encoding and decoding can change how efficiently the same scarce airwaves carry information.

Irwin Jacobs approached this world indirectly. Born in New Bedford, Massachusetts, in 1933, he grew up around his father’s restaurant business. A guidance counselor steered his mathematical talent toward Cornell’s hotel-management school, where he spent a year and a half before discovering electrical engineering. Jacobs later credited the business education with helping him become an entrepreneur. He finished his MIT doctorate in 1959 and stayed to teach digital communications.

A California sabbatical brought Jacobs into contact with Andrew Viterbi and the satellite-communications world. Jacobs moved to the new engineering school at UC San Diego, while consulting work drew him and fellow academics into NASA and defense projects. Linkabit began as a way to organize that work. By 1971, Jacobs was spending what was supposed to be a year’s sabbatical building the company; he never returned to his university post.

The limits of subcontracting pushed Linkabit toward commercial products. The hosts trace its satellite work to Walmart’s private network and its video-scrambling expertise to pay television. These were practical businesses built from deep communications knowledge. Linkabit sold to M/A-COM for $25 million in 1980. Five years later, following a leadership change, Jacobs and colleagues left with money, experience, and a view that wireless communications were only beginning.

02 · 1985–1988

A patent before a market

The grand ambition needed a business that could pay for it.

In July 1985, seven founders met at Jacobs’s house to start Qualcomm—“quality communications.” Analog cellular service already existed, but it was expensive and constrained. Powerful car phones competed for limited capacity, and customers waited for connections. The founders saw demand exceeding what the existing radio systems could efficiently serve. Knowing a better technical approach was only the beginning: they would have to persuade carriers, equipment makers, handset manufacturers, and standards organizations to move together.

Qualcomm initially returned to consulting. A Hughes project involving a proposed mobile satellite network gave the team an occasion to develop its approach to code division multiple access, or CDMA. The satellite proposal did not become the hoped-for business. The patent strategy survived it. The episode dates Qualcomm’s foundational terrestrial-cellular patent application to 1986; application and issuance are distinct events, even though the hosts briefly slide between them in conversation.

CDMA asked the receiver to do something that seemed improbable in the 1980s: recover many overlapping, digitally encoded conversations in real time. The processing had to work in base stations and, eventually, portable handsets. Qualcomm was betting that semiconductor progress would make the required processing practical by the time the product shipped. The technology was not a finished miracle waiting on a sales force. Its commercial feasibility depended on the timing of the chips.

Meanwhile, a customer named OmniNet wanted satellite communications for commercial trucks. Qualcomm won the development work, then discovered that customers wanted an operated service rather than a collection of components. Trucking businesses did not want to build dispatch and communications infrastructure themselves. Qualcomm and OmniNet merged in 1988, raising $3.5 million and diluting Qualcomm’s existing owners by roughly half. The hosts emphasize how painful that financing was, despite the opportunity ahead.

Omnitracs reached the market at the end of 1988. The episode reports $32 million of revenue in its first year, 1989, and rapid growth thereafter. Hardware and operating costs meant this was not software-like revenue. It nevertheless provided something the cellular ambition badly needed: cash flow, a real customer base, and a business against which Qualcomm could raise financing. The satellite-trucking product helped pay for a much larger terrestrial bet.

03 · The technical wager

Many conversations, one medium

Three ways to share the airwaves—and the obstacle that remained.

The hosts explain the progression using a dinner party. Frequency division gives each conversation its own room: users occupy separate frequencies. Time division lets conversations take turns in one room: digital information travels in alternating time slots. Code division allows multiple conversations to overlap, while giving a receiver a way to distinguish the intended message. Their different-language analogy is a teaching aid, not a literal description of the radio signal.

Qualcomm’s economic claim was that more calls could fit into the same licensed spectrum and infrastructure. That mattered to carriers far more than an elegant engineering demonstration. If a network could serve more paying subscribers with a similar fixed asset base, the carrier could earn more or cut prices. The hosts discuss a roughly three-to-fivefold advantage over the competing time-division approach, while distinguishing it from more aggressive early comparisons with analog systems.

The near–far problem made the proposal difficult. A nearby phone’s signal could overwhelm a distant one. CDMA required tightly managed transmission power so that receivers could distinguish many users without the closest drowning out the rest. Qualcomm also had to solve synchronization and other implementation problems. The patent portfolio expanded as engineers worked through these layers. The episode’s “magic tricks” are a way to convey their interdependence, not an assertion that a single patent solved everything.

04 · 1988–1993

A standard without permission

A narrow opening in the rules let the demonstrations matter.

The US industry’s requirements for the next digital cellular generation arrived in 1988. Europe was already moving toward GSM and time-division technology, backed by established suppliers. Qualcomm believed its code-division approach better fit the capacity ambitions of the US requirements. But a small company could not win merely by declaring the incumbent standard inadequate. It needed both a legal opening and evidence that its alternative actually worked.

Jacobs and Viterbi went to Washington to confirm a crucial distinction: the US industry standard was not a government mandate forcing every carrier to use the same technology. A carrier could choose another approach that met the requirements. The hosts see that freedom as the opening Qualcomm needed. The FCC’s allocation of spectrum and an industry association’s choice of technical standards were different functions.

PacTel supplied $1 million for a prototype. At the November 1989 demonstration in San Diego, Jacobs had to keep talking while engineers rebooted a GPS system. The test eventually worked, with vehicles moving around the city and a base station at headquarters. Skeptics then asked whether the technology could survive a harder urban environment. Qualcomm followed with a successful Manhattan demonstration in February 1990 and signed NYNEX; Ameritech followed that August.

South Korea offered a different route. There, a government-backed choice of CDMA helped create demand for an entire new network, rather than asking every participant to abandon an established system independently. The hosts describe South Korea becoming a very large share of Qualcomm’s revenue for a period. Both the American opening for competing systems and Korea’s coordinated commitment helped the company, through almost opposite institutional arrangements.

Qualcomm went public in December 1991, raising $68 million. US industry bodies formally adopted CDMA as a second standard in 1993, and the company raised more capital. Winning technical recognition had taken years. Building enough equipment to turn recognition into a functioning industry would demand still more money.

05 · 1990s–1999

Build the missing pieces

A complete solution was a bridge to a more focused company.

A carrier could accept the CDMA argument and still refuse to buy. Who would supply the base stations? What phones would its customers use? Where would the chips come from? Each supplier wanted confidence that the others would participate. Qualcomm therefore had to address four connected layers: intellectual property, infrastructure, handsets, and silicon. Selling permission to use its invention would not, by itself, resolve this coordination problem.

Qualcomm partnered with Nortel on infrastructure and Sony on handsets. These ventures made the carrier’s decision easier: there would be actual equipment to purchase. Manufacturing was expensive, however, and it was not the destination the company ultimately chose. On the chip side, the emerging foundry model gave Qualcomm another possibility. It could design the silicon that made CDMA practical while outsourcing fabrication, rather than financing a semiconductor factory of its own.

The hosts regard that combination as decisive. Qualcomm kept the underlying communications IP and the differentiated chip designs, while using equipment operations to get adoption started. The episode reports revenue rising from $383 million in 1995 to $814 million in 1996. Yet manufacturing and the joint ventures absorbed capital and profits, limiting the enthusiasm of public-market investors.

In March 1999, Qualcomm sold its infrastructure business to Ericsson in connection with a legal settlement. In December, Kyocera bought the handset business. Once there were enough other participants in the ecosystem, Qualcomm could withdraw from the businesses it had helped establish. The resulting model had two main engines: QCT, the semiconductor business, and QTL, technology licensing. It could sell chips and collect royalties for its patent portfolio, including where another supplier’s chips were used.

This was a difficult transition for some people inside the company. The episode recounts employees transferred to Ericsson challenging the loss of their Qualcomm stock options. The same restructuring celebrated as strategic focus by investors could look very different to workers moved out of the company. The hosts describe an extraordinary share-price surge around the dot-com peak and a subsequent collapse; this companion does not turn that volatile episode into an investment lesson or rely on their inconsistent year references.

06 · 2000s–2010s

The invention has to keep moving

A patent portfolio is valuable only while the industry needs it.

As mobile networks shifted toward data, code-division techniques became central to 3G, including the evolution of the GSM family. But Qualcomm could not assume its original technical approach would dominate every generation forever. In 2005, the company acquired Flarion for $600 million, gaining technology and patents relevant to the orthogonal-frequency approach used in 4G. The hosts describe an initial resistance to that transition followed by a practical pivot.

That was also the period when Irwin’s son Paul Jacobs became CEO and Qualcomm developed Snapdragon mobile systems on a chip. Its opportunity expanded beyond the modem that connected a phone to the network. High-end Android phones could buy a broader computing platform from Qualcomm. The hosts view this as a major success of an otherwise mixed era: ambitions in connected devices took longer than hoped, and the Mirasol display effort required a $2 billion fabrication investment without producing the expected customer demand.

Success intensified disputes over who should capture the value. Qualcomm paid $900 million following its Broadcom litigation in 2009. China challenged its licensing practices, and activist JANA Partners pressed in 2015 for separating the chip and licensing operations. Qualcomm kept them together. In 2018, Broadcom attempted a $117 billion takeover; the US government blocked it on national-security grounds. The hosts support Qualcomm’s independence, while presenting the role of its political relationships as their interpretation.

The other large confrontation was with Apple. Apple could design its own application processor and still need separate cellular technology and patent licenses. In 2017, Apple and the FTC challenged Qualcomm’s practices. The episode discusses allegations that Qualcomm used its modem position and standard-essential patents to demand excessive payments. Those are contested claims, not a finding that every charge discussed by the hosts was unlawful. Standard-essential licensing also involved the obligation to offer fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory terms.

07 · 2010s–2022

A customer tries to escape

Designing the main processor did not solve the radio problem.

Apple tried Intel modems in iPhones, but the move to 5G exposed how hard an alternative would be. The hosts explain that modern radio systems must handle multiple spectrum bands and changing network conditions, with complex processing and radio-frequency front-end components. Millimeter-wave performance could be extraordinary in the right location, while its propagation limits made coverage difficult. “5G” did not mean every connection delivered the same speed or used the same spectrum.

In 2019, Apple and Qualcomm settled. The hosts describe a multiyear license agreement and a payment of roughly $4–$4.5 billion, while acknowledging uncertainty in the detailed economics. Apple also bought Intel’s smartphone-modem operation and kept trying to build its own solution. The episode’s discussion of when Apple might stop buying Qualcomm chips is a November 2022 expectation. It is not a report about what ultimately happened.

The commercial stakes were large, but the transcript offers several estimates rather than a single transparent price list. The hosts discuss differing per-phone licensing and modem figures, negotiated discounts, and a number disclosed during litigation. This companion keeps chip sales and patent royalties separate and does not combine those estimates into a supposedly exact charge per iPhone. Apple replacing a purchased chip would also be a different event from escaping relevant patent licenses.

Qualcomm had its own response to the next computing frontier. In 2021, it bought NUVIA for $1.4 billion, bringing in former Apple chip designers. The opportunity was to build custom processors using the ARM instruction set rather than rely on a standard ARM CPU design. The hosts saw potential to differentiate Snapdragon, but treated expansion into laptops, servers, automotive, and connected devices as a question about execution. Past success in phones did not prove success in every adjacent market.

08 · The hosts’ November 2022 assessment

The next frontier is still a question

The same model that funds invention can provoke the customers who fund it.

At recording, the hosts describe roughly $44 billion of annual revenue: about $37 billion from chips and $7 billion from licensing. They cite approximately 34% and 69% pretax margins respectively, emphasizing the unusually profitable licensing stream. These are episode-time figures, not current financial statements. The proposed reason to keep the two operations together was that predictable royalty income could fund research, producing both new chips and new intellectual property.

Their Seven Powers discussion starts with patents as a cornered resource. They also identify historical network effects between compatible infrastructure and handsets, and scale economies in spreading chip-development costs across many customers. David argues that the original group of engineers had process power: a particular team and working environment that outsiders could not easily reproduce. He offers the more limited spread of comparable successor successes as suggestive evidence, rather than a conclusive demonstration.

The adoption story also contains a deliberate tension. Qualcomm needed credible alternative suppliers so carriers would trust CDMA, while preserving enough proprietary implementation knowledge to remain the best supplier itself. The hosts distinguish patents from trade secrets: some inventions were disclosed and licensed, while other know-how could support differentiated engineering services and implementation. Each new wireless generation renewed the opportunity, but a missed standards window could leave a company waiting years for another chance.

The bear case was that Qualcomm had pushed important customers hard enough to make alternatives worth pursuing. Apple and Samsung had incentives to develop their own technology; MediaTek offered cheaper competing modem and system-on-chip products. Repeated litigation could preserve the licensing model and still indicate that ecosystem relationships were strained. The hosts separate the durability of royalties from the competitive risks to the chip business.

The bull case rested on growth beyond the mature handset opportunity. The episode puts automotive revenue around $2 billion, radio-frequency front-end revenue around $4 billion, and IoT revenue above $7 billion, all within the chip segment. Management’s much larger addressable-market claims were ambitions, and Ben explicitly questioned how much precision those huge totals deserved. A connected world could be an enormous opportunity without guaranteeing that Qualcomm would capture it on the same terms as before.

Ben’s closing view is conditional: repeating the handset playbook might be less rewarding once customers understood it and sought ways around it. Another strong decade would require the new businesses to work. David makes the best version of the connected-edge case—data still has to travel into and out of the cloud—but leaves value capture unresolved. The episode ends with admiration for how many dependent technical and commercial steps the founders executed, alongside the acknowledgment that even exceptional teams often fail at bets that require so many things to go right.
