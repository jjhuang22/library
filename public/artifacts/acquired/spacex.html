Acquired · Spring 2020 · Season 6 Episode 7

SpaceX

A business on the way to Mars

A greenhouse becomes a rocket company. A launch company becomes a shipping service. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal follow the sequence that could finance something far larger.

Acquired · SpaceXReleased May 26, 2020Created October 15, 2026About 18 minutes to read

The episode’s May 2020 perspective, including its concluding analysis. Crew Dragon’s first flight with astronauts is still upcoming; Starlink and Starship remain developing ambitions.

01

2000 to 2002

A greenhouse before a rocket company

The first plan was to make people care about Mars.

Listening cue · 0:05:38

Elon Musk’s route into aerospace began with disappointment. After Zip2 and the company that became PayPal, he had money and relationships, but the hosts describe a man repeatedly removed from leadership. In October 2000, after another departure from the top job at PayPal, he turned toward Los Angeles and the space industry. Space was a longstanding interest: Ben and David recount him studying Soviet rocket manuals while his internet colleagues wondered what he was doing.

Musk initially wanted an inspirational demonstration. Through the Mars Society and gatherings of aerospace experts, a proposal took shape: send a plant and a robot to Mars, build a small greenhouse, and beam pictures of something growing there back to Earth. His available budget was then on the order of $10 million to $20 million. Most of his eventual PayPal wealth had not yet become cash.

The launch price threatened the entire idea. Jim Cantrell, an engineer with Russian connections, helped Musk investigate buying converted missiles. Mike Griffin joined the trips; his later role would become important. In the hosts’ account, negotiations ended without a purchase. On the flight home from Moscow in February 2002, Musk worked on a detailed spreadsheet and announced that they could build the rocket themselves.

Tom Mueller, an engine specialist, had helped him understand what the machinery required. The spreadsheet changed the question from how to buy an expensive launch to why it had to be so expensive. Ben cites an estimate that raw materials represented only about 2 percent of a rocket’s price. He immediately qualifies it: materials alone cannot produce a functioning rocket. The gap was an invitation to examine how the industry worked.

02

2002 to 2008

An engine, a factory, and four chances

A small rocket tests both the technology and the company’s ability to survive.

Listening cue · 0:35:07

Space Exploration Technologies was incorporated in 2002. Musk committed about $100 million of his own money over the early life of the company, according to the hosts. Having experienced removal from his previous companies, he initially resisted outside investors. SpaceX would start with Falcon 1, named after the Millennium Falcon, and a single Merlin engine designed around Mueller’s expertise.

The original manufacturing plan was less unusual than the company became: make the engine and assemble a vehicle using outside suppliers. High prices and slow schedules pushed more work inside. Ben and David describe layers of contractors adding margins and overhead, with government cost-plus procurement weakening the pressure to reduce the total bill. SpaceX moved toward making much more of the vehicle itself, while still relying on a substantial network of suppliers for materials and components.

Gwynne Shotwell supplied a different missing capability. She had worked in aerospace and understood the customers and their budgets. The early thesis centered on an emerging market for small satellites, but she saw that NASA, defense agencies, and established operators also wanted lower-cost launches. The hosts credit her with helping make SpaceX a transportation business that could fund the hardware ambitions. Taking a Falcon model to Washington was part sales effort, part public demonstration.

The first flights took place from Omelek Island in the Kwajalein Atoll. Getting there required flights across the Pacific and a boat journey. The first Falcon 1 launch failed in March 2006; the next attempt in March 2007 also failed to reach orbit after problems in the second stage. The hosts describe fuel sloshing as a central problem on that second attempt. Each failure consumed months as well as money.

A third failure followed in August 2008. By then Musk had also committed much of his remaining fortune to Tesla, and the financial crisis made outside money difficult to find. Founders Fund invested $20 million that summer. The fourth Falcon 1 flight reached orbit in September 2008, carrying a dummy payload. A Malaysian satellite followed on the fifth flight in 2009. The company had proved it could reach orbit, but its future business was already moving toward a larger vehicle.

03

2006 to 2012

NASA buys a delivery service

A change in procurement gives the larger rocket a customer.

Listening cue · 1:09:32

Mike Griffin, who had accompanied the earlier Russian trips, became NASA administrator. The hosts see that relationship as significant, but their larger argument concerns policy. NASA was expanding a commercial approach in which it could support transportation development and buy deliveries, rather than design and operate the entire vehicle itself. Ben describes the distinction through a shipping analogy: help build the trucks, then pay the operator to deliver the packages.

SpaceX needed Falcon 9 to carry enough cargo to the International Space Station. Musk had canceled the intermediate Falcon 5 and advanced the nine-engine design even while Falcon 1 remained unproven. The modular use of Merlin engines gave the company a route to more capacity, though it still had to develop the larger rocket and the Dragon spacecraft.

On December 23, 2008, NASA awarded SpaceX a $1.6 billion contract for 12 cargo missions. In the hosts’ telling, this came as Musk struggled to keep both SpaceX and Tesla alive. It changed the scale of the customer commitment: earlier Falcon 1 launches had sold for roughly ten million dollars each. The award was a contract to perform future missions, not evidence that all the money had already been earned.

Falcon 9 made a successful test flight in June 2010, followed by a Falcon 9 and Dragon test that December. Dragon reached the ISS in 2012. The hosts emphasize how unusual this was: a private company had built and operated not just a launch vehicle but a spacecraft capable of reaching the station.

They also distinguish government participation from a simple subsidy story. NASA supported development and became an important customer; SpaceX invested its own capital and retained vehicles it could use commercially. Ben cites roughly $400 million of NASA money and $450 million of SpaceX money for Falcon 9 and Dragon development, alongside a much higher hypothetical NASA development estimate. Those figures are the episode’s account, not a fresh cost audit. The hosts’ claim is that a new purchasing model could benefit both taxpayers and the commercial operator.

04

The launch market

The incumbent’s economics become the opening

Lower prices matter because the surrounding system was built differently.

Listening cue · 1:26:17

Ben contrasts SpaceX with United Launch Alliance, the combination of Boeing and Lockheed Martin’s launch operations. He acknowledges the incumbents’ extraordinary engineering history while arguing that their focus on government business left commercial opportunity underserved. Their vehicles and organizations reflected an environment that emphasized reliability and performance with less pressure on price.

He compares the emerging challenger to ARM in computing: an initially less impressive product can be built around a different set of requirements. Falcon 1 looked small beside the industry’s largest rockets. It was not obvious from that starting point that SpaceX would become an ISS supplier. Ben raises disruptive innovation as a possible interpretation, rather than treating the analogy as proof.

The hosts’ financial discussion remains tentative. David estimates substantial cumulative commercial revenue by multiplying launch counts by sticker prices. Ben pushes back on his total and suggests a lower figure. They agree that government and commercial customers both matter, but their disagreement should not be turned into a precise revenue split.

The competition was also responding. Ben notes leadership changes at ULA and its work with Blue Origin on the Vulcan program. The story is about a cost and operating advantage under pressure from capable rivals, not an industry in which everyone else simply disappears.

05

2011 to 2017

Landing is only the beginning of reuse

The valuable asset has to return in a condition worth flying again.

Listening cue · 1:41:44

The hosts introduce reusability with an aircraft comparison: air travel would be extraordinarily expensive if every trip ended by discarding the plane. SpaceX began working on landing technology through Grasshopper and then incorporated recovery attempts into operational launches. Failed landings made the difficulty visible.

A Falcon 9 first stage landed successfully on land in December 2015. A successful drone-ship landing followed in April 2016, and a previously flown first stage flew again in March 2017. These are distinct achievements: recovery does not by itself establish reuse, and reuse does not by itself establish economical turnaround.

Ben describes the landing burn as unusually unforgiving. A Merlin engine supplies so much thrust that the booster cannot simply hover indefinitely. Ignition and deceleration must be timed to reach the landing surface at the right speed. During this explanation David corrects Ben’s engine name: Falcon uses Merlin; Raptor belongs to the Starship discussion.

The economic debate remains open in the episode. Competitors questioned how much refurbishment cost and whether reuse really saved what SpaceX claimed. Ben’s bullish case depends on inspections becoming short and replacement work becoming infrequent. The hosts admire the engineering while recognizing that outsiders do not have an audited view of the cost structure.

06

May 2020

A crew waits at the next threshold

The conversation stops on the eve of a scheduled human launch.

Listening cue · 1:48:03

The episode was released May 26, 2020, ahead of a scheduled May 27 flight with Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley. The hosts are excited about an American company carrying astronauts to the ISS, but the mission is still ahead of them. This companion preserves that moment rather than narrating later events backward into their conversation.

They trace the crew effort to a $2.6 billion NASA contract in 2014 and an uncrewed Crew Dragon docking demonstration in 2019. Boeing had also received a crew contract. Ben discusses its Starliner test difficulties to explain why he sees SpaceX’s progress as consequential, while expressing hope that the upcoming launch goes well.

The company’s ambitions extend beyond Falcon and Dragon. Starship is presented as the future vehicle, and Mars remains the mission behind the enterprise. At this point those are development goals. The hosts’ admiration for what has already flown coexists with uncertainty about what comes next.

07

The next proposed business

SpaceX becomes its own launch customer

A constellation could turn a launch advantage into subscription revenue.

Listening cue · 1:58:10

When the hosts ask what might have happened without NASA’s procurement change, they return to timing. The small-satellite market had not arrived quickly enough to support the early company. Starlink offers a different response: build an internal customer for launches while pursuing a broadband business of its own.

Ben describes a proposed constellation of 12,000 satellites in low Earth orbit. Being closer to users than traditional geosynchronous systems could reduce latency. He discusses launches carrying batches of 60 satellites and early demonstrations, but his account of eventual global coverage and a connected constellation is a future vision. It is not a description of a mature operating network in May 2020.

The business appeal is recurring revenue. A launch customer pays for a mission; a broadband customer could keep paying for service from assets already deployed. Ben explicitly guesses at internal launch costs rather than claiming to know them. David sees a reinforcing loop: internal demand brings more launches, more experience, and potentially lower costs, which help the satellite business in turn.

Rideshare makes spare capacity available to other small payloads. Ben cites prices as low as $1 million and marvels that a customer can make a deposit online. He also highlights the published $62 million Falcon 9 price. In a market accustomed to bespoke negotiations, transparent pricing changes the buying experience as well as the competitive conversation.

The hosts’ proposed reinforcing businesses

Launch capability can serve two kinds of customer

Paid launchesGovernment and commercial customers buy transportation.
ReinvestmentManufacturing and reuse could reduce the cost of future missions.
Owned satellitesStarlink could create launch demand and recurring broadband revenue.

The Starlink revenue loop is a 2020 thesis. The episode does not establish a mature subscription business or audited launch margins.

08

The hosts’ playbook

A different structure can be difficult to copy

Ben and David ask which advantages endure beyond a technical lead.

Listening cue · 2:05:52

David applies Hamilton Helmer’s counter-positioning idea to SpaceX. Matching its vertical integration, operating approach, and price transparency could require incumbents to damage the business structures that already serve them. He is less certain about how broadly his explanation of vertical integration applies to other industries. The discussion is exploratory rather than a complete assignment of every possible competitive power.

Ben describes a sequence of reinvestment. Sell launches for less than competitors charge; use the margin to improve reuse and reduce costs; then use the resulting capability and funds to develop owned businesses such as Starlink. His suggestion that a first flight might already be gross-margin positive is explicitly an estimate. A profitable mission is also different from a profitable company investing heavily in new programs.

Their other examples keep the discussion concrete. Ben contrasts horizontal rocket assembly with expensive vertical workspaces. He points to the breadth of customers across civil government, defense, telecom, and media. David compares Musk’s early personal investment with the larger recurring capital commitments at Blue Origin, while Ben argues that the mission itself, rather than merely having money at risk, explains Musk’s persistence.

Private ownership enters the analysis through employee liquidity. David recounts Musk’s resistance to an IPO and the use of periodic share sales. He believes much of the fundraising was secondary; Ben treats that as an important distinction. They do not establish an exact primary-versus-secondary breakdown. Their broader point is that employee liquidity and funding a long development program are different needs.

09

Value creation and grading

Success can arrive before Mars

The final ambition is vast, but the intermediate businesses matter too.

Listening cue · 2:26:12

Ben and David are strongly positive about value creation. They see cheaper access to space as enabling activity that otherwise would not happen. They are more cautious about measuring value capture because SpaceX is private and its financial position is opaque. Their enthusiasm is not a substitute for knowing the accounts.

For grading, Ben proposes the grandest A-plus case: a society and economy on Mars. David responds that the usual five-year horizon still matters. Unlike a venture that only pays off in a remote final state, SpaceX has stepped through rockets, transportation, and potentially satellite broadband. Each intermediate stage can have value even if the ultimate goal remains distant.

Their speculative Starlink discussion illustrates why the distinction matters. They mention roughly $10 billion of expected capital needs, but neither establishes its eventual subscriber economics or valuation. Revenue, enterprise value, and invested capital appear as different ideas in the conversation; they cannot be substituted for one another to make a financial forecast.

The F cases include a damaging launch failure, loss of customer confidence, government restrictions, and other risks on a scale most companies do not face. Even the success case raises political questions. If a company carries a large population to Mars, who governs it? The hosts leave that question open. Technical accomplishment would not settle ownership, authority, or the rules of a new society.

The conversation closes with more ordinary recommendations: David chooses The Last Dance; Ben recommends Michael Mauboussin’s discussion of The Success Equation and the relationship between skill and luck. That last subject echoes the limits in the company story. The hosts can admire SpaceX’s decisions and accomplishments without showing that every uncertain step was destined to work.
