A business education before a computer education

Acquired reading companion · Spring 2024

Microsoft Volume IThe software inside every machine

From the Lakeside teletype to Windows 95: how Bill Gates, Paul Allen and Steve Ballmer built a business that could grow with every PC maker—and kept several futures alive until Windows became the franchise.

Released April 22, 2024 · Created October 19, 2026
About 32 minutes to read · Complete episode history and concluding analysis
Based on Acquired: Microsoft Volume I, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01

1955–1968 · 00:03:06

A business education before a computer education

Bill Gates grew up expecting to take part in consequential decisions.

Bill Gates III was born in Seattle in 1955, into a household unusually connected to the region’s business and civic life. His father, Bill Gates Sr., became a prominent attorney. His mother, Mary Maxwell Gates, moved from nonprofit leadership into corporate boards and university governance. Business was something discussed across the dinner table, not an inaccessible world somewhere else.

The hosts resist the image of young Bill as merely a shy computer enthusiast. He was intensely competitive, mathematically gifted and interested in leading a company before he knew which company it would be. His family supplied examples, access and support, including legal judgment that would later matter to a young software partnership.

At Lakeside School, that preparation met a remarkably rare opportunity. In 1968 the mothers’ club funded a teletype and rented access to a remote computer. A thirteen-year-old could type instructions, wait for a response over a phone line and see the result printed on paper.

Ben and David make the privilege explicit. Intelligence and effort mattered, but so did being born at the right time and attending one of the few schools offering this access. Bill and the older Paul Allen received an early view of a future that most people could not yet touch.

02

The late-1960s market · 00:13:48

The company that was the computer industry

IBM sold a complete institutional solution; smaller machines opened new territory.

IBM’s mainframe business combined hardware, operating software, applications, training and services. A customer was buying a system and an ongoing relationship, not a box that could simply be delivered and switched on. This was the world from which the later PC business would depart.

Antitrust pressure helped encourage IBM to separate parts of that bundle. The hosts see an important opening in the possibility of buying hardware from one company and software from another, even before Microsoft existed to exploit it.

Digital Equipment Corporation offered another opening. Its minicomputers were smaller and cheaper than the dominant mainframes, though still far from household appliances. Universities, branch offices and other customers could use computing capacity that had previously been out of reach. DEC founder Ken Olsen became a business hero to Bill.

Lakeside’s connection ran to a DEC machine, not an IBM mainframe. The distinction foreshadows the episode’s central pattern: a new generation of computing could serve new users before it threatened the incumbent’s established customers. The hosts later qualify the comparison—DEC never displaced IBM in the way the PC ecosystem would change control of the industry.

03

Late 1960s–early 1970s · 00:20:22

Learn the machine, then negotiate a royalty

The Lakeside group acquired scarce skills and an early business model.

Bill, Paul and their friends called themselves the Lakeside Programmers Group. Using a computer meant programming it; there was no familiar market of ordinary users selecting packaged applications from a graphical desktop.

A local timesharing startup, Computer Center Corporation, paid the students in computer time to find and document bugs. They moved beyond BASIC into systems languages and machine-level work. Steve Russell, known for Spacewar, was among the experienced people around that environment. The mentorship and access gave the teenagers practical experience well beyond a school exercise.

Another timesharing company wanted a payroll program. Bill negotiated with help from his father, choosing royalties rather than merely an hourly fee. The hosts cite more than ten thousand dollars earned from that work, a substantial sum for students at the time.

That contract did not yet invent the entire future of software. It did teach the group to separate the work of writing a program from the continuing value of letting someone use it. A recurring commercial idea appeared before a company named Microsoft or a mass market for personal computers existed.

04

1971–1973 · 00:25:51

The traffic machine that taught them emulation

A limited business success supplied an unusually valuable technical rehearsal.

Traf-O-Data was an attempt to process the traffic counts municipalities used in planning. Bill and Paul wanted to build a machine around an Intel microprocessor, putting a computer’s essential functions on a chip rather than relying on the much larger equipment they already knew.

They could not simply obtain the target processor and start working. Paul used a larger computer to emulate its instruction set from the manual. That let them write software before they had the actual hardware. If the manual and emulator were correct, their program could arrive before the machine did.

The hosts describe Traf-O-Data as a modest business, with revenue figures treated as approximate rather than a great entrepreneurial breakthrough. The more consequential result was experience with a microprocessor and an emulator. Paul would soon repeat that method under much greater pressure.

He also drew Bill’s attention to the implications of improving semiconductor economics. If computing kept becoming dramatically cheaper and more powerful, machines could reach every desk and home. That was an extraordinary expectation in a world of teletypes. The missing part of the familiar vision was just as consequential: those computers would run Microsoft software.

05

1973–1974 · 00:33:13

Waiting for a computer powerful enough to matter

The vision was firm; the product opportunity was still forming.

Bill went to Harvard and initially imagined a future in advanced mathematics. Encountering extraordinary mathematical talent changed that expectation. He made friends, played poker and met Steve Ballmer, whose social presence could obscure how analytically formidable he was.

Paul left Washington State and moved to Boston to stay close to Bill. Both worked at Honeywell for a period. They followed Intel’s newer processors and waited for a company to build the machine they believed had become possible.

The 8080 felt different from the earlier chip they had studied. It seemed powerful enough to support a real general-purpose computer and a commercial programming environment. Yet the established computer makers did not immediately flood into the market. The opportunity that looked obvious to Bill and Paul threatened other firms’ existing economics or simply did not fit their expectations.

Then Paul saw the January 1975 issue of Popular Electronics, featuring the Altair 8800. The story of him rushing the magazine to Bill’s room has the quality of a founding legend; the hosts’ larger point is firmer. The machine existed, and their preparation could finally be turned into a product.

06

1975 · 00:40:00

Sell the possibility, then build the interpreter

They offered software before they had written it.

Bill and Paul called Ed Roberts at MITS in Albuquerque and said they had a BASIC interpreter for his Altair. They did not. Roberts invited anyone who could demonstrate a working version to come and earn a contract, making the next step unambiguous.

The Altair was a very spare machine: a box with lights and switches, without the screen and keyboard a later buyer would expect. An interpreter would let someone use the more accessible BASIC language instead of working directly with the processor’s instructions. That made the software valuable to the machine’s manufacturer as well as its user.

Paul built an 8080 emulator on Harvard’s computer, repeating the Traf-O-Data approach. Bill wrote the interpreter. They could test against a model before ever running it on the target hardware.

Paul traveled to Albuquerque carrying the program. In the hosts’ account, he wrote the missing bootloader during the flight. The first attempt did not work; a subsequent one did. The anecdote is not evidence of an inevitable flawless launch. It captures both how much engineering they had already learned and how precarious it was to demonstrate a new program on unfamiliar hardware.

07

1975 · 00:45:45

An unlikely hardware company makes the market

The opening came from a struggling maker in Albuquerque.

MITS was far removed from IBM. Ed Roberts had worked in model rocketry, and the Altair was a bid to rescue a financially strained company. A magazine cover brought attention, while a negotiated Intel processor price made an affordable kit possible.

The hosts do not know why Intel gave Roberts such favorable terms. They offer possible explanations as speculation, not a documented negotiation. What mattered was the result: a machine priced at roughly four hundred dollars attracted thousands of prepaid orders.

Paul moved to Albuquerque and initially worked for MITS. Bill remained at Harvard for a time, continuing to improve BASIC, then came west. They formed Micro-Soft as a partnership, dated April 4, 1975 in the episode.

The name joined microcomputer and software. It also drew a boundary Bill would repeatedly defend when Paul was tempted by hardware. Their distinctive contribution was writing software that could be reproduced across machines. The first ownership split was sixty–forty, later becoming sixty-four percent Bill and thirty-six percent Paul. The partnership was already a negotiation between complementary founders, not a frictionless origin myth.

08

1975–1976 · 00:52:42

The first contract gave away too much control

An exclusive distributor had reasons not to serve the whole market.

MITS received an exclusive license to distribute the 8080 BASIC. Microsoft would receive thirty dollars per copy and a share of any sublicensing revenue, with total payments capped at a hundred eighty thousand dollars. Microsoft could not simply sign other computer makers as customers.

Those terms made sense to inexperienced founders needing their first route to market. They also created a conflict. MITS wanted to sell Altairs, not equip rival hardware companies with a useful programming environment. Its control over sublicensing could hold Microsoft back precisely when demand outside MITS began to grow.

The agreement did contain a crucial protection: MITS had to use its best efforts to commercialize BASIC broadly. Failure could justify terminating the arrangement. The hosts return to Bill’s legal support because a small clause would become decisive.

Meanwhile, sales of machines did not translate into comparable software sales. Microsoft earned roughly sixteen thousand dollars in 1975 and twenty-two thousand in 1976, even as enthusiasm for microcomputers rose. Users were copying BASIC. An apparently excellent product was attached to a weak method of collecting value.

09

1975–1980 legal context · 00:56:58

A license could travel with the machine

Bill’s anger at copying led toward a different customer.

Bill’s open letter to hobbyists argued that copying software without paying undermined the work of making it. Ben and David pause before projecting today’s legal framework backward. Copyright treatment of computer programs was still being developed, with a congressional change in 1980 helping establish the framework they discuss.

The essential distinction was between buying a physical object and receiving permission to use a copyrighted program. Microsoft could retain rights while licensing use under specified conditions. The hosts treat that distinction as a foundation of the software industry, rather than a settled assumption available to the founders from the beginning.

But a future legal framework could not fix the immediate business. Bill recognized a more practical answer: make the hardware manufacturer the customer. If BASIC made a computer useful, the manufacturer could include it in the purchase and pay for it as part of the machine’s economics.

The early deals would take several forms, including fixed payments as well as later royalties. The important change was who was responsible for buying the software. Microsoft no longer wanted its business to depend on persuading each hobbyist to make a separate optional purchase after acquiring the computer.

10

1977 · 01:02:43

Win the right to sell to everyone

The next generation of machines made exclusivity untenable.

The RadioShack TRS-80, Commodore PET and Apple II brought assembled computers and stronger distribution into the market. Larger firms also approached Microsoft about BASIC. MITS delayed or rejected opportunities, protecting its own hardware position while constraining its software partner.

MITS was sold to Pertec in 1977. The dispute continued under the new owner and went to arbitration. During that period Microsoft could not freely serve the customers it wanted, and cash became unusually tight. The hosts identify it as the company’s exceptional early financial squeeze.

Microsoft won the arbitration and regained the freedom to license BASIC directly. It immediately made deals across the emerging hardware market. Apple’s version needed floating-point capability, and buying Microsoft’s interpreter was simpler than completing every piece internally.

The thirty-one-thousand-dollar Apple arrangement, described as eight years of access, illustrates the pricing objective. Bill wanted the standard to spread, not the maximum possible payment from one customer. Low overhead and much of the technical work already completed made that patience financially possible. Other machines could differ, while the programming environment became familiar.

11

Late 1970s · 01:09:30

Make compatibility the easy choice

A modest lead could reinforce itself as the market expanded.

The hosts describe Bill’s positive spiral: a slightly better standard attracts use, greater use makes it more attractive to the next buyer, and inexpensive reproduction lets that advantage compound. BASIC helped Microsoft learn the pattern before DOS made it much larger.

They preserve an important qualification. A program written for Microsoft’s BASIC was not necessarily impossible to run elsewhere. The buyer simply knew it would run on the familiar implementation. If Microsoft’s license was inexpensive, why accept compatibility risk to save a small amount?

The economics depended on unusually low initial costs. A few technically strong people could create the interpreter, collect cash up front from hardware makers and spread their work across many machines. The hosts warn against assuming that every later market offers an equally affordable entry point.

By the end of 1977, revenue was roughly four hundred thousand dollars. The company began to think of itself as an enabler of the software industry, not only a programmer trying to win the next contract. More computers and more useful programs could expand the whole market, with Microsoft positioned inside that growth.

12

1978–1979 · 01:15:14

International before there was much company

A Japanese enthusiast became a distribution partner.

Kazuhiro Nishi shared Bill and Paul’s enthusiasm for microcomputers and brought Microsoft into Japan’s computer industry. He became an exclusive distribution partner there. By 1979, the hosts say, Japan accounted for about half of revenue.

This was an extraordinary choice for a small Albuquerque business. International activity became a basic condition of how Microsoft built products, rather than a secondary expansion once America was saturated. Later localization, training and in-country sales would inherit that expectation.

Revenue reached about 1.3 million dollars in 1978 and 2.4 million in 1979. Recruiting became a reason to leave Albuquerque, despite Bill’s appreciation for the focus and lack of distraction there. Seattle offered home, a strong university talent pool and relative distance from Silicon Valley’s employee circulation and rumor network.

The hosts credit the University of Washington’s continuing role and, in an official correction, make clear that Paul Allen and the Ballmer family also supported it. Bill was not the only Microsoft figure contributing to that institution. The company’s move positioned it for a request from the largest name in computing.

13

1980 · 01:29:39

IBM starts a garage inside the elephant

A one-year deadline made outside suppliers essential.

IBM could see microcomputers becoming a real market. It had missed part of the minicomputer expansion and did not want to watch another generation grow entirely outside its reach. Yet its normal processes were poorly suited to building an inexpensive PC quickly.

Project Chess, in Boca Raton, was an attempt to escape those constraints. A small team would use outside components and ship within roughly a year. The hosts give IBM substantial credit for making that possible. An incumbent capable of launching such a project was doing something difficult, even if it later captured less of the ecosystem’s value than expected.

Microsoft was the obvious source of programming languages. It was already supplying the microcomputer world and could offer BASIC and other tools. The first conversation was not a master plan in which IBM knowingly handed over control of an operating-system platform.

In June 1980 Steve Ballmer joined as employee thirty, bringing the person Bill wanted beside him for the business ahead. His roughly eight-and-a-half-percent stake reflected that importance. Bill absorbed additional dilution when Paul objected to the size of the grant. Their small team was now negotiating with a company whose scale dwarfed it.

14

1980 · 01:36:25

The operating system opportunity almost passed by

Microsoft first referred IBM to someone else.

Microsoft did not yet have the operating system IBM needed. Bill directed the company to Gary Kildall’s Digital Research, which supplied CP/M. It was a reasonable referral from a programming-language vendor, not evidence that Bill had already decided how every part of the platform would evolve.

What happened next is disputed. The hosts discuss versions involving Gary’s absence, Dorothy Kildall’s reluctance to sign an NDA, and a later disagreement about royalties. They do not resolve the conflicting accounts into a definitive story about someone casually missing the chance of a lifetime.

What is clear in their narrative is that IBM and Digital Research did not reach the arrangement IBM needed. The company returned to Microsoft and asked it to solve the operating-system problem.

This time Microsoft accepted. The opportunity was partly prepared judgment and partly circumstance: a critical requirement had landed back in the hands of a team able to find and adapt a solution. That is a different story from either pure luck or an omniscient founder executing a perfect plan.

15

1980–1981 · 01:42:00

Buy a beginning, then turn it into DOS

Tim Paterson’s work supplied a route to meeting IBM’s deadline.

At Seattle Computer Products, Tim Paterson had written an operating system for the new processor generation because the company needed software to work with its boards. The hosts describe QDOS as the starting point Microsoft could obtain and adapt.

Microsoft first paid twenty-five thousand dollars for rights associated with an unnamed OEM customer, then another fifty thousand for broader ownership and licensing rights before the PC shipped. Paterson joined Microsoft and helped develop the product further.

The seventy-five-thousand-dollar total is memorable, but the episode explicitly rejects the idea that Microsoft purchased all the later DOS product for that amount. Considerable development followed. The acquired beginning and the eventual system were not identical pieces of software.

The transaction also illustrates how fluid the industry remained. Microsoft had entered the IBM talks as a language supplier and nearly left the operating system to someone else. Once IBM required an integrated answer, the company assembled one quickly. The business terms of supplying it would matter more than the initial purchase price.

16

November 1980 · 01:47:02

Let IBM create demand; retain the right to serve it

The transformative term was permission to license beyond IBM.

In Paul Allen’s account quoted by the hosts, IBM paid seventy-five thousand dollars for testing and consultation, forty-five thousand for DOS, and three hundred ten thousand for languages: four hundred thirty thousand dollars in total. IBM had no ongoing per-copy royalty obligation under that deal.

What Microsoft retained was ownership and the ability to license its software to other manufacturers. Bill accepted a limited immediate payment while preserving participation in a market much larger than the one customer.

IBM expected its PC to become the standard, and it was right. Bill expected other manufacturers to build compatible machines around the same available components, and he was right too. Developers would target the operating environment with the largest audience; the other hardware makers would then need that environment to attract buyers.

The hosts consider this the pivotal transfer of the industry’s point of integration. IBM would create demand using its reputation and distribution, while Microsoft could collect from firms competing with IBM. They also acknowledge IBM’s antitrust concerns and uncertainty about how deliberately it accepted the tradeoff. The outcome was clearer than every negotiator’s original intention.

Explanatory interlude

The two sides of the IBM bargain

IBM’s immediate deal

A fixed payment buys the agreed software and work without an ongoing per-copy royalty.

Microsoft’s retained right

It keeps ownership and can license DOS and languages to other manufacturers.

The later ecosystem

IBM establishes demand; compatible makers need the standard and pay Microsoft per machine.

Conceptual explanation from the episode. The $430,000 IBM package and $75,000 Seattle Computer Products transaction are separate deals.

17

1981–1984 · 01:53:20

The standard escapes its original manufacturer

IBM’s success and Compaq’s compatibility made Microsoft’s retained rights valuable.

The IBM PC shipped in August 1981 and sold strongly. Buyers could choose among operating systems, but the prices favored PC DOS: sixty dollars against a hundred seventy-five for CP/M and four hundred fifty for the UCSD p-System. The official correction clarifies that p-System was the operating system’s name; Pascal was the programming language.

IBM still controlled a proprietary BIOS. Compaq’s clean-room approach separated analysis of required behavior from implementation, allowing a compatible alternative without simply copying the original code. Other manufacturers could then combine available components with software licensed from Microsoft.

For those manufacturers Microsoft charged per machine, unlike its fixed-fee arrangement with IBM. The operating system was essential enough to travel with the computer, connecting Microsoft’s revenue to the proliferation of hardware suppliers.

The hosts cite twenty-five million dollars of calendar-1982 revenue and ninety-eight million in fiscal 1984, carefully noting the change in reporting periods. They compare software’s low reproduction cost with the hardware makers’ substantial cost of goods. That does not mean Microsoft had no engineering, manufacturing, distribution or support costs; it means another license had very different economics from another computer.

18

1981–1986 · 02:01:55

Outside money arrived after the cash machine worked

The venture investment was not a rescue financing.

Microsoft converted the partnership into a corporation and accepted a million dollars from Technology Venture Investors, roughly five percent at the initial transaction. Dave Marquardt had earned the founders’ confidence by engaging with the business and helping recruit talent, rather than simply offering capital.

Ballmer screened prospective investors. Marquardt eventually talked strategy with Bill at a football game, then continued spending time in Seattle. The hosts see governance, relationships and the corporate conversion as more important motives than a need for cash. The business was already profitable.

Paul’s diagnosis of Hodgkin’s disease led to leave and then departure from full-time work in the early 1980s. His economic stake and the history of his contribution remained important even as the operating team changed.

Microsoft went public in 1986, the same period as its move to the Redmond campus. The hosts cite a roughly seven-hundred-fifty-million-dollar market value and discuss how little investors then seemed to appreciate software economics. They also preserve Bill’s later fear that technology rules could change abruptly. High margins alone did not make the future feel safe to the people building it.

19

Early 1980s · 02:12:26

Multiplan loses to a focused spreadsheet

Owning an operating-system position did not guarantee application success.

VisiCalc and WordStar showed that packaged programs could make a personal computer useful for business. Microsoft formed a consumer-products effort to compete, hiring Charles Simonyi from Xerox PARC. His experience carried ideas from graphical computing into Microsoft as directly as Xerox’s influence reached Apple.

The official episode correction adds an important predecessor: Doug Engelbart’s 1968 work at SRI demonstrated a mouse and graphical interaction before PARC. Xerox remained a major source of people and ideas, but it was not the beginning of every invention in that lineage.

Microsoft’s Word and Multiplan initially operated in a character-based world. Portability looked like an advantage: make a program available on many machines. Lotus instead concentrated 1-2-3 on the IBM PC and used that platform’s capabilities to make a faster, stronger spreadsheet.

Multiplan lost decisively. The hosts stress that Lotus was for a time larger than Microsoft in revenue and valuation. The application team’s response was to target the next generation, even when the best available next-generation platform belonged to another company. Microsoft would seek the graphical spreadsheet opportunity on Apple’s Macintosh.

20

1984–1985 · 02:24:02

The Mac needed Excel, and Excel needed a new platform

A rival’s machine gave Microsoft a way around Lotus’s lead.

Microsoft had worked closely with Apple during Macintosh development. The early Mac’s limited memory constrained what developers could do, but a more capable version opened room for applications that justified buying it. Excel and PageMaker became central examples in the hosts’ account.

Excel’s initial release was for the Mac, a surprising fact for anyone who identifies the program entirely with Windows. A graphical spreadsheet offered visual relationships and interactions that distinguished it from the incumbent character-based product. Apple was invested enough in its success to share marketing effort, according to the episode.

The official correction makes the internal decision less tidy. Excel, code-named Odyssey, had originally targeted the PC. Bill personally changed the target to the Mac, upsetting the team. Doug Klunder, its leader, decided to leave but was persuaded to stay through the first release. This was a disruptive leadership choice, not a harmonious conclusion that everyone reached at once.

Meanwhile Word had appeared for DOS, even bundled with a mouse. The boundaries between an application, an input device and a platform were still being worked out. Microsoft could sell software for Apple while simultaneously pursuing its own graphical environment.

21

Mid-to-late 1980s · 02:30:25

A suite starts as a box and a price

Office’s commercial bundle came before deep product integration.

The early Macintosh business bundle grouped Microsoft applications together. At first it was largely a matter of pricing, marketing and manufacturing: one package made several programs easier to sell and cheaper to buy together.

It did not immediately mean a single integrated product. The seamless movement of a spreadsheet table into a document or presentation was still ahead. The hosts distinguish the commercial idea of the suite from the later engineering work that made the applications cooperate.

PowerPoint arrived through the 1987 purchase of Forethought. By the end of the decade Word, Excel and PowerPoint could form a more familiar bundle. The episode does not turn this into a separate acquisition history; it uses the evolution to explain how Microsoft’s application business developed alongside its systems business.

Those divisions were following different immediate opportunities. One sold applications on whatever platform looked most promising; another worked on DOS, Windows and the IBM relationship. Their unifying idea was still broadly software for personal computers, not an already settled decision that every product existed only to serve Windows.

22

1983–1987 · 02:34:29

The graphical hedge was not the favored project

Windows began as an awkward environment above DOS.

Microsoft announced Windows in 1983, before the Mac shipped, despite its close application partnership with Apple. Development was difficult. Ballmer eventually managed the final push after the previous manager unexpectedly resigned—a correction to the recording’s statement that the person was fired.

Windows 1.0 arrived in November 1985. Its tiled windows lacked the overlapping behavior later users would expect. The hosts treat it as an awkward intermediate product, not the moment the eventual winner emerged fully formed.

Apple licensed elements of its interface to Microsoft. The scope of that agreement, including future Windows versions, would become important in the later copyright litigation. The episode’s broad account of the dispute does not mean Apple owned every idea associated with graphical computing.

Early Windows was an operating environment running above DOS. More importantly, it was a hedge. OS/2 and IBM remained the prestigious, strategically central work inside Microsoft. A retrospective history centered on Windows can hide how uncertain its prospects looked compared with the partnership that had created the company’s strongest position.

23

Late 1980s · 02:41:52

Staying on the bear while encouraging the rebels

Microsoft remained close to IBM while keeping alternatives alive.

Ballmer described the IBM relationship as riding a bear: remaining on its back seemed safer than falling underneath it. IBM wanted to recover more control of the PC ecosystem through the next operating-system generation, and Microsoft could not comfortably assume that effort would fail.

At the same time, hardware makers such as Compaq pushed more powerful Intel machines. Microsoft encouraged the 386 path while continuing to express commitment to IBM. Its Windows and Macintosh work gave it alternatives if the official plan disappointed.

The official corrections add engineering that the recording omitted. David Weise and Phil Barrett drove a skunkworks effort using the 386’s protected mode to give Windows applications access to far more memory. Windows/386 also provided a bridge by letting multiple DOS virtual machines stay active. These were concrete technical steps toward commercial viability, not merely a change in marketing or managerial conviction.

OS/2’s reception did not match IBM’s ambition. Microsoft’s revenue kept growing through DOS and applications, while Windows became more credible on the increasingly capable installed hardware. The company had not predicted one future perfectly. It had done enough work on several possibilities to change course when one became attractive.

24

1990–1993 · 02:47:15

Windows finally becomes the main bet

Customer enthusiasm changed the internal hierarchy.

Windows 3.0 arrived in 1990 with performance, graphical interaction and support for existing DOS applications that made the proposition much stronger. Earlier Windows versions had reached only a small share of DOS users. The response to 3.0 and then 3.1 gave Microsoft a chance to be independent of IBM.

Brad Silverberg’s account emphasizes the cultural gap. Microsoft’s younger teams wanted to ship what users liked and what improving hardware made possible. Working around IBM’s constraints pulled in another direction. A small, less prestigious Windows team could now be promoted from hedge to strategy.

The divorce became official by the end of 1990. Revenue reached 1.2 billion dollars in fiscal 1990, 1.8 billion in 1991 and 2.8 billion in 1992. The later Apple interface dispute was resolved in Microsoft’s favor across its stages, with the earlier license central to the hosts’ explanation.

In January 1993 Microsoft passed IBM in market capitalization. That was not the same as passing IBM in revenue. The hosts also recount an unconfirmed story that Bill was invited to run IBM; they explicitly could not corroborate it. The strategic change does not depend on believing that anecdote.

Explanatory interlude

Three routes through an uncertain future

OS/2

Remain with IBM if its next platform succeeds.

Mac applications

Build graphical applications on Apple’s promising environment.

Windows

Develop an independent graphical route that can become the main bet.

Concurrent strategies with changing emphasis, not equal budgets or a claim that management always expected Windows to win.

25

Early 1990s · 02:55:09

Build the applications, recruit the developers, win the OEMs

A platform required several constituencies to move together.

Windows needed users, developers and hardware manufacturers at once. Developer relations supplied documentation, APIs and support. Microsoft’s own applications helped demonstrate that the platform could support valuable work, while OEM teams pressed manufacturers to preinstall it.

The episode also describes the per-processor contracts used during 1988–1994. In exchange for a lower rate, a manufacturer paid Microsoft on all relevant machines it shipped, including machines carrying another operating system. That made choosing a competitor feel like paying twice.

Regulators objected, and Microsoft agreed to stop the practice. The hosts distinguish recognizing its exclusionary effect from claiming it alone created the company’s position. Their interpretation is that DOS and Windows were already winning, though they acknowledge that timing affects how one evaluates the mechanism.

This is the tension of the platform throughout the story. Microsoft could create opportunity for independent hardware and software firms while controlling a dependency they all needed. Supporting an ecosystem and capturing power within it were not separate eras with a clean dividing line.

26

Early 1990s · 03:00:24

Employees brought the opening; Ballmer built the sales motion

Selling a copy and serving an institution were different jobs.

People were using spreadsheets and word processors at work before Microsoft had a mature way to sell and support an enterprise-wide relationship. Office gave Ballmer an opening with executives: their employees already depended on the applications.

That did not mean a large company wanted to buy retail boxes one at a time. It needed support, deployment, partners and systems that fitted the organization. The hosts credit Ballmer with taking responsibility for building those capabilities, including direct and indirect sales, consulting relationships and customer service.

They also preserve why early IT departments were uneasy. A PC on someone’s desk could be useful to that employee while failing to connect cleanly with the institution’s existing systems. Making the company more productive required more than adding many independent spreadsheets.

This is the beginning of an enterprise transformation, not a claim that the entire later product lineup existed in the early 1990s. The episode looks forward to the organizations and tools that would develop, while reserving the fuller enterprise and cloud story for Volume II.

27

1988–1993 onward · 03:07:22

Two implementations of the Windows future

An enterprise foundation could not simply be rushed into the consumer product.

Dave Cutler joined Microsoft from DEC in 1988, bringing experience building an enterprise operating system. The official correction names that system VMS; VAX referred to the machine or wider system, not the operating system’s proper name.

His credibility mattered alongside the engineering. Microsoft could make a more serious case to IT departments with someone who understood their requirements, rather than merely extrapolating from a consumer graphical environment. Windows NT was a long-term bet with demanding development, testing and deployment needs.

In 1991 Bill summarized the direction as one evolving architecture, a couple of implementations and many applications from Microsoft and others. The hosts unpack the distinction: developers could target a common interface while enterprise NT and the consumer successor to Windows 3.1 followed different implementations.

NT shipped in 1993. It did not replace the need to make a compelling everyday consumer product. The seeds of later server and enterprise businesses were being planted while another team worked toward Windows 95. The breadth of the eventual Windows franchise emerged from concurrent efforts with different users, constraints and time horizons.

28

1993–1995 · 03:13:28

An ordinary destination rather than a distant promise

Chicago had to become a product people could actually reach.

Consumer PCs were becoming companions to daily life: games, multimedia and CD-ROMs expanded what people expected. Even before Windows 95, the hosts cite seventy-five million Windows users. The next version had to make that expanding world coherent for ordinary buyers.

Chicago was the code name for the practical consumer release. Its team contrasted a familiar, reachable destination with Cairo, the ambitious collection of future operating-system ideas elsewhere inside Microsoft. Cairo never shipped as the promised unified product in the episode’s account.

Windows 95 needed a clearer answer to what belonged in an operating system and what belonged in an application. The Start menu, networking and multimedia helped turn scattered capabilities into something a new user could understand and begin using.

The hosts admire the product without eliminating the tradeoff against Apple. Microsoft’s ecosystem reached across many independent hardware configurations, which created compatibility and driver problems. Apple’s more integrated experience had different strengths and, in this era, much smaller scale. Neither their preference as Mac users nor Windows’ market success alone explains the whole comparison.

29

August 24, 1995 · 03:16:03

Software becomes a mass-market event

The launch celebrated an operating system as though it were a cultural release.

Microsoft filled its Redmond campus with tents, invited press and beta testers, and put Jay Leno alongside Bill onstage. The Rolling Stones’ Start Me Up became the theme. Around the world, people lined up to buy software.

The hosts are struck by how openly joyful the event was. Only about 360 people had built the product, according to their account, yet the release felt enormous. Traditional broadcasting and print coverage mattered because internet access was still limited. The coordinated launch in eight languages also reflected the international habits established in the company’s earliest years.

Windows 95 sold a million copies in its first week and seven million in its first month in the figures the hosts cite. Fiscal-1995 revenue of 5.9 billion dollars ended in June, before the August launch; it should not be attributed to those sales. The following fiscal years reached 8.7 billion and then twelve billion dollars.

The hosts describe Windows 95 as cementing the franchise. It also moved beyond the simple description of a graphical layer over DOS, though legacy support remained. The reading companion ends its main history here, leaving the internet, later antitrust case and the costs of continuing to center everything on Windows for the next episode.

Before turning to analysis, the hosts add a brief coda about Microsoft Research. Nathan Myhrvold started the effort in 1991 with a different problem: many existing mainframe and minicomputer tasks had now been adapted to PCs. Research could help discover uses for future technologies and keep the ecosystem moving beyond those inherited applications.

30

The hosts’ concluding analysis · 03:28:26

Capital efficiency preserved unusually large ownership

The economics and the historical moment mattered together.

The hosts return to a striking capitalization table. At IPO, they cite forty-nine percent for Bill, twenty-eight for Paul and seven-and-a-half for Steve, alongside the venture investor and employee ownership. Some reconstruction of the earlier option pool is explicitly approximate.

The company raised outside money, so saying it literally never received investment would be wrong. Their argument is that operating cash funded the business without a continuing need to consume new investor capital. Even the forty-five million dollars raised in the IPO was small relative to the cash the company generated.

Employee shareholding helped motivate going public before the anticipated shareholder threshold forced the timing. The hosts also debate whether public status was necessary to become a trusted institution at this scale. One points to other large private companies; the other thinks Microsoft’s eventual role was different. The disagreement is part of the analysis.

The conditions were exceptional. Scarce programming skills, low initial fixed costs, cheap reproduction and the extraordinary expansion of PC shipments reinforced one another. The hosts cite shipments rising from roughly four thousand in 1975 to nine million in 1986. This was not merely a universal lesson to raise less money. They question whether an equivalent opening can be found again, and discuss later internet economics as a comparison.

31

The hosts’ playbook · 03:38:47

Conviction about software, flexibility about the route

The alternatives had to be real enough to become the main strategy.

Microsoft’s leaders believed software for personal computers would matter enormously. They were less certain which platform or institutional relationship would dominate. OS/2, the Mac application business and Windows represented different positions across those possibilities.

The hosts emphasize not only holding a hedge but developing it far enough that the company could move when circumstances changed. Turning the less prestigious Windows effort into the main direction required organizational commitment, not merely announcing that management had changed its mind.

New technology generations supplied opportunities to reorder a market. A direct attack on an incumbent within the same constraints was harder. Lotus beat Multiplan; Microsoft needed the graphical generation to turn Excel into a different proposition.

They also caution against equating a shift in perception with the death of the old business. Microsoft passed IBM in market value long before it passed IBM in revenue, which the episode dates to 2015. IBM’s revenue did not peak until 2012. Customers could continue finding value in an incumbent’s systems for decades after the story of industry leadership moved elsewhere.

32

The hosts’ operating analysis · 03:42:55

Talent, local markets and software that is never finished

The platform’s scale rested on work beyond writing the first version.

The hosts’ interviews convey intense commitment from early employees. People neglected other parts of life but remembered the work as meaningful. Brad Silverberg points to clear product principles, responsibility pushed toward developers and a sense that each person owned the result.

OEM distribution let a small Microsoft team benefit from the independent scaling of Compaq, Dell, HP and other manufacturers. International reach supplied both more customers and a discipline: products had to be localized seriously. Country managers could choose sales and marketing suited to their own markets while Redmond developed the common software.

Microsoft also copied useful ideas and made switching easier, including familiar spreadsheet shortcuts. The hosts qualify the claim that copying alone explained its wins. Learning from customers and being ready for a platform transition mattered more than simply reproducing the incumbent product.

Their final operating theme is that software is never done. A shipped version began another period of feedback, fixes and improvement. Weak early releases could become stronger third versions. That perspective differed from thinking of a product as a cabinet of equipment that had been delivered once. Continuous development is part of the episode’s explanation, not a claim that every release or deadline succeeded.

33

The hosts’ competitive framework · 03:53:23

Seven possible powers, with unequal strength

They make a broad case while doubting parts of it.

The hosts think a case can be made for all seven of Hamilton Helmer’s powers somewhere in Microsoft’s 1975–1995 history. Counter-positioning meant embracing microcomputer software without needing to preserve IBM’s hardware economics. Scale spread the cost of development over a huge base, making each improvement more valuable to Microsoft than to a small rival.

Switching costs came from the applications users depended on. Network economies joined developers, users and manufacturers; file formats added another effect, because a document was more useful when colleagues, clients and other institutions could open it.

Process power is their least certain candidate. They discuss the later ability to coordinate large interdependent software releases but are less sure that early Microsoft had a uniquely defensible production process. Brand existed in the enterprise and, especially after Windows 95, with consumers, yet they think structural advantages were more fundamental to the outcome.

DOS became a cornered resource after IBM’s distribution made it the standard others needed. The hosts connect that power to the wider value created around Microsoft: hardware makers and application businesses could grow on top of the platform. The company captured enormous value, but the episode’s conclusion does not reduce the ecosystem to value capture alone.

34

The final takeaway · 04:00:58

The place where the industry had to meet

The most important negotiation moved control to a new layer.

The episode gives only a brief bear-and-bull framing because the subsequent history is already known. The bull case was that operating systems would remain central and Microsoft would keep improving them. The bear case was that something else would become the important layer. Volume II begins from that tension rather than an unknown share-price outcome.

The hosts’ lasting fascination is the IBM deal. An established giant created a new market at remarkable speed, but Microsoft retained the rights that let it serve the rest of that market. The point of integration moved from the manufacturer of the mainframe to the software environment users knew and developers targeted.

They give IBM credit for Project Chess even while marveling at the negotiating outcome. Likewise, they admire the ambition of young Microsoft without pretending there was no ugly behavior or that every effect was beneficial. Bill’s belief in software as a way to make other people productive was sincere in their reading, alongside the determination to capture the resulting economics.

The final substantive reflection is about the team beginning to believe it could stand independently of IBM. That confidence arrived gradually, after years of uncertainty and hedging. Trivia and personal recommendations follow from 04:06:03 onward; the company analysis ends with that combination of unusual preparation, favorable timing and a negotiated right to participate in an industry’s growth.
