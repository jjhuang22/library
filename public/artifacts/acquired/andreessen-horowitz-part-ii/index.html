An illustrated companion to Acquired, with Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal
Released August 10, 2021 · Created Unpublished
Part II only · 2008–2021 · About 24 minutes to read

Chapter 01 · 2008–2009

Two operators enter the cathedral

A plan to change venture capital begins with a problem of access.

Marc Andreessen had long found venture capital intoxicating. As he recalled it, visiting Kleiner Perkins meant high ceilings, reminders of public companies, Larry Ellison walking through, and an enormous buffet at a time when he was eating at Subway. It felt like a cathedral for nerds. By late 2008, he and Ben Horowitz were no longer outsiders to technology investing. They had made 36 angel investments together in three years, generally writing checks no larger than about $200,000. But joining somebody else’s round and winning the right to lead one were different businesses.

The instant-message exchange that opens this episode belongs to 2008, the hosts correct themselves, before the firm launched in 2009. The financial crisis made this an unlikely moment to raise a fund. Existing investors were protecting portfolios, and Sequoia’s “RIP Good Times” presentation captured the mood. Marc and Ben saw a different future: Silicon Valley’s opportunities had not disappeared with the financial system’s trouble. They intended to build an institution around that conviction.

Their friend Andy Rachleff, a Benchmark cofounder who had moved into teaching and entrepreneurship, liked a correct bet that other people rejected. He also understood the entrance barrier. Benchmark had challenged Kleiner by promising a small, equal partnership: founders would get the particular partner they wanted on their board, without pressure to trade with other portfolio companies. A new firm needed an equally coherent reason to exist. Marc and Ben settled on being, in Horowitz’s later words, the anti-Benchmark.

That was a strategy rooted in their own experience as technical founders. They believed the innovator should remain in charge. A company could outgrow a young founder’s contacts and management experience much faster than the founder could acquire them. Rather than replace that person with a professional CEO, could a venture firm supply the network and skills that a professional CEO already possessed? This episode begins with that question, rather than with the assumption that the eventual institution was inevitable.

Chapter 02 · 2009

Put the fees back to work

The promise to founders requires a different cost structure.

The answer cost money. Marc and Ben wanted to raise $300 million and use its management fees to hire specialists. At an initial 2.5% fee, the arithmetic was $7.5 million a year. The hosts explain that higher fees early in a fund’s life can be balanced by lower fees later; “two and twenty” is shorthand, not a fixed annual schedule. Marc and Ben took no salary for at least their first two years. Having already made money as entrepreneurs gave them room to turn fees into services.

Their criticism of the existing arrangement was pointed. A lean venture partnership could take home substantial fee income while telling founders to live on $50,000 or $60,000 a year. Andreessen Horowitz would instead spend on recruiting, customer introductions, communications, financing and acquisitions. These functions became “networks”: a way to give an inexperienced technical CEO access to people the startup could not yet afford to hire.

The investment mandate broke convention too. They would invest at seed, Series A, later venture rounds and growth. Sometimes they would take a board seat; sometimes they would not. Conventional fund pitches specified stage, ownership, check size and governance, then repeated that pattern across a portfolio. This new pitch was unusually broad. It reflected both the founders’ angel success and their belief that growth investors and technology buyout firms were capturing large dollar returns without supplying the assistance they proposed to offer.

The hosts connect this to changes in startup financing. Seed money and cheaper infrastructure could remove some early risk before the Series A, while Series A investors still expected the large ownership stakes associated with the older, riskier model. Marc and Ben could compete on price. But their seed strategy exposed a flank: if they declined to follow on, would the market read that as inside information against the company? The hosts return to the signaling objection with an embarrassing counterexample. After Burbn became Instagram, a16z chose its competing investment PicPlz. Instagram still raised from Benchmark’s Matt Cohler. Here, the bad signal hurt the investor’s reputation more than the founder’s prospects.

Chapter 03 · Before the launch

The dream execution machine

Michael Ovitz supplies an institutional analogy; Margit Wennmachers supplies a voice.

Rachleff’s warning extended beyond strategy. There were only so many companies in a vintage that could make a venture fund. Winning them meant fighting established firms with lasting monuments: eBay, Cisco, Oracle, Google. Rivals could dismiss Netscape and Opsware as good stories that had not endured. In the hosts’ telling, the launch coverage carried some of those punches, including Steve Case’s comparison of Marc to a rock star whose first album had eclipsed the next ones. David interprets these press attacks as part of the competitive battle; he does not establish who planted each quote.

Michael Ovitz, their former board colleague, offered another model. At Creative Artists Agency, he had challenged a Hollywood system in which studios controlled the money and talent negotiated individually. CAA connected its clients and packaged projects before offering them to studios. The agency became the place where the package came together, giving artists more leverage. Its shared relationships mattered more than the isolated agent. The phrase the hosts seize on is “the dream execution machine.”

For a technical founder, a great product was only the beginning of a company. A16z would assemble the other pieces: executive and engineering recruiting, enterprise customers, future capital, potential acquirers and public relations. The hosts differ slightly on emphasis. David calls PR perhaps the most differentiated piece; Ben says the firm itself would likely emphasize its executive briefing center and ability to gather large corporate customers. They agree that marketing became integral to the institution.

Margit Wennmachers had cofounded OutCast, whose clients included major technology companies. A conflict involving a company Marc and Ben had backed brought her to Marc’s attention. An apparent conversation about that problem became a meeting at the Creamery in Palo Alto about their new firm. She disliked putting the founders’ names on the door of a business meant to transcend individual partners. They argued that Marc’s existing reputation would get them started, after which “a16z” could carry the franchise. The geeky abbreviation also suggested their ambition to invest from A to Z.

The public launch was staged over months. Marc appeared on Charlie Rose in February 2009, redirecting a question about the next big idea toward the proposition that innovation was alive and well. The fund occupied only a small part of the interview; the brand did not yet exist. In July, Fortune announced the $300 million fund with an Uncle Sam-style cover: “I want you to get the future.” The claim “by entrepreneurs, for entrepreneurs” challenged career investors’ legitimacy, while their optimism gave founders a public champion. The product and its publicity were being built together.

Chapter 04 · 2009–2010

One-sixth of a fund in Skype

A controversial large check makes the all-stage promise tangible.

The earliest investments drew on existing relationships. The hosts tentatively identify a small Digg check as the first, while acknowledging uncertainty about whether it began as a personal or warehoused investment. Apptio was their first substantial round leadership. Its founder Sonny was familiar through the Loudcloud orbit, and the company illustrated a network-driven approach before the firm had its later research machinery. Rockmelt, the social browser founded by former Opsware executive Eric Vishria, likewise combined a person they knew with a product category Marc understood.

Then, in September 2009, the firm invested $50 million alongside Silver Lake in the separation of Skype from eBay. The wider transaction bought 65% of Skype for about $2 billion. A16z’s check purchased only 1.8%, but it consumed one-sixth of a $300 million fund. Measured by a conventional ownership target it looked small; measured as dollars that could come back to this particular fund it was enormous.

Marc helped arrange Skype’s Facebook video-calling partnership and recruit Cisco executive Tony Bates as CEO. Bates’s recollection of entering the firm’s library, looking for William Gibson’s Neuromancer and using its absence to open a conversation became part of another Fortune story. A large transaction, operating assistance and the institution’s cultivated identity reinforced one another.

Microsoft’s purchase of Skype for $8.5 billion turned the stake into about $153 million in roughly eighteen months, according to the episode. This was approximately a threefold return, a modest multiple beside a classic early-stage home run but a consequential sum for Fund I. The hosts later return to the risk: losing that $50 million early could have changed the firm’s history. Its later resilience should not be projected backward onto this first test.

Chapter 05 · 2010–2011

Strength follows strength

Okta, a second fund, and the permission to keep trying.

Early in 2010, a16z led Okta’s Series A. Todd McKinnon believed cloud software created room for a new identity provider despite Microsoft’s established position through Active Directory. Marc and Ben also knew identity technology through Tim Howes, a Netscape veteran and Loudcloud cofounder who had invented LDAP. The thesis was not simply that identity was valuable. It was that a change in architecture could reopen a category that looked settled.

The hosts report a substantial stake at Okta’s public offering and estimate a very large outcome. Their spoken discussion mixes rough IPO valuation and later market-value figures; the dependable point for this companion is their estimate of $1.5–$2 billion attributable to the investment in the closing analysis, not a precise reconstructed cash distribution. They explicitly doubt that the first fund held every share through the recording date. That distinction becomes essential when the episode later totals its wins.

With half the first fund reserved for follow-ons and money moving quickly into new companies, another fund was already necessary. In December 2010, a16z raised $650 million. The pace and size shocked an industry still accustomed to stretching older funds after the dot-com collapse. Marc’s formulation was that strength leads to strength: success attracts entrepreneurs, capital and talent, making more success possible. The hosts distinguish the observed persistence of venture performance from a proven explanation for it.

More capital also enlarged the operating budget. Wennmachers joined full time after selling OutCast; Jeff Stump came to run talent, and former Opsware business-development leader John O’Farrell became a general partner. The latter was an exception to the simplified message that GPs must have been CEOs. In early 2011, the firm also bought private secondary shares in companies including Facebook, Twitter and Groupon. The hosts put the spending above $80 million but qualify both the total and its allocation; their discussion of the venture exemption’s 20% limit is an explanation of the constraint, not an audited account of these purchases.

Another 2011 investment tested the founder promise. A16z led Tiny Speck’s Series B, backing Stewart Butterfield’s game Glitch. Butterfield told his board that the money felt less like gas on a fire than good whiskey poured onto a drugstore heating pad: this was not product-market fit. Rather than take the money back, the investors let the team continue. Its internal communications tool became Slack. The hosts estimate an approximately $3 billion a16z outcome only if the firm held through the later Salesforce transaction. It is a conditional estimate, not evidence of realized proceeds.

Chapter 06 · 2011

The year of hits—and the Uber term sheet

The firm can see a larger future and still misprice an individual decision.

The portfolio was never a procession of winners. The hosts recall ShoeDazzle, Jawbone, Lytro and Fab alongside Airbnb, Pinterest, Stripe, Nicira and Tiny Speck. Jawbone’s ambitious technology and Fab’s appealing merchandise did not prevent losses. Their phrase is “slugging percentage, not batting average”: a handful of enormous successes could matter more than a long catalog of unsuccessful bets.

Jeff Jordan joined in 2011 with experience running businesses at eBay, PayPal and OpenTable. Bill Gurley had been his board member at OpenTable, a personal connection across the a16z–Benchmark rivalry. Jordan became central to a16z’s consumer investing, including Pinterest and Airbnb, and later Instacart and Affirm. By 2019 he was a managing partner alongside Marc, Ben and Scott Kupor, whom the hosts describe as the firm’s stewards.

That first year also brought the Uber miss. As the hosts recount the reporting, a16z and Travis Kalanick had a handshake understanding for a Series B at a little over $300 million post-money. Someone became uneasy; the account implies Marc, but the hosts retain that attribution rather than claiming certainty. At dinner, Marc proposed roughly $220 million instead. Travis supposedly remained willing. The formal term sheet then included a large new option-pool refresh, further diluting existing holders. That additional cost broke the deal. Menlo accepted the higher valuation and won the investment.

A16z later backed Lyft, whose peer-to-peer model was a different proposition from Uber’s black-car business at the time of the failed negotiation. The hosts estimate about a billion-dollar Lyft stake at the point it could become liquid, while stressing that Uber would have created a much larger result. A good subsequent investment did not erase the missed opportunity.

The irony is that Marc had just published “Software Is Eating the World” in August 2011. His argument was that cheaper computing let software companies enter industries far beyond their original domain, expanding possible outcomes. David connects this with the expectation that successive technology generations can become much larger. Ben pushes back: entry prices rise too, so larger companies do not automatically mean better fund returns. He also asks where ultimate saturation lies. They are discussing the 2021 horizon, not establishing an endless law of growth.

Chapter 07 · 2012–2013

Hunting bigger game

GitHub and Coinbase make the portfolio math visible.

In January 2012, the firm raised $1.5 billion for Fund III—7.5% of all venture capital raised globally that year, according to the episode. Part of the apparent shock came from labeling very different kinds of investment with venture terminology. A16z’s $100 million GitHub investment was called a Series A because the company had largely bootstrapped. Economically, the hosts argue, this resembled the later-stage technology deals that firms such as Summit or Silver Lake might have pursued.

A mature business could absorb a large check with less early product risk. A tenfold return on $100 million was highly consequential even if ten times would be an inadequate single home run for a portfolio of tiny seed bets. The hosts put the ultimate GitHub proceeds at about a billion dollars following Microsoft’s 2018 purchase. They are less certain about the original ownership and post-money valuation; the causal point is check size multiplied by a good outcome, not a precise cap-table reconstruction.

Chris Dixon joined later in 2012 after founding SiteAdvisor and Hunch and helping create Founder Collective. His move from New York to Silicon Valley was itself notable. In 2013, he led a $25 million Series B in Coinbase at about $150 million post-money. A16z subsequently bought shares from other investors as well. By Coinbase’s 2021 direct listing, the hosts value the combined stake at roughly $11 billion. They use this to demonstrate how a single company could overwhelm the objections to the scale of a $1.5 billion fund; the stake value is not a claim that $11 billion had been distributed in cash.

The breadth remained extraordinary: the hosts count 76 new companies in 2012 and 97 in 2013. Lyft, PagerDuty, Robinhood, Oculus and Databricks joined the story, alongside Zenefits and Clinkle. Databricks’s eventual ownership and return were still speculative at recording. The Google Glass Collective, meanwhile, made the hosts laugh: three firms announced they would share related deal flow without committing capital. To them it was a striking PR success around a thin investment proposition, and a reminder that calling something the future did not make that particular product succeed.

Chapter 08 · 2014–2015

The franchise becomes the message

Competitors begin buying the same expensive capabilities.

Marc’s Twitter activity changed abruptly in 2014. After scarcely posting despite having invested in the company, he became prolific. He described the platform as a tube with loudspeakers in reporters’ cubicles. Ideas, argument and publicity were now distributed continuously rather than only through an occasional magazine cover. Another $1.5 billion fund closed in March 2014, bringing assets under management to around $4 billion. Seed investing slowed for a period; the hosts wonder whether signaling concerns affected either the firm or entrepreneurs, rather than asserting a settled cause.

Tad Friend’s 2015 New Yorker profile crystallized the rivalry. A competing investor calculated that low average ownership meant a16z’s portfolio would need $240–$480 billion of combined value to deliver five to ten times across its first four funds. Marc dismissed the objection: they were hunting elephants. Looking back from 2021, the hosts add up the enormous market values of Coinbase, Roblox, Okta, Slack, Airbnb and Pinterest to show why the once-implausible scale no longer looked absurd. Combined company valuations still were not the firm’s return.

The same article captured incumbent adaptation. Sequoia’s Doug Leone said a16z had forced it to improve its marketing because young founders paid attention to media and Sequoia would not be depositioned. Hiring specialists meant the large firms could no longer run as cheaply. Ben separates two kinds of compression: more staffing reduced the fees partners could keep, while greater competition and higher valuations reduced ownership per invested dollar.

For founders, the hosts see a better product: more capital, less dilution and access to specialists before a startup could justify those capabilities internally. They acknowledge debate about the services’ value, but see a public champion and strong recruiting as potentially consequential. The institutional consequence was a thinner middle. Large franchises needed large cost structures, while individual investors could play narrower games with almost no staff. The hosts’ praise concerns this competitive change; it does not make every service effective in every portfolio company.

Chapter 09 · 2015–2021

A family of funds

Specialization changes the institution while exits take time to ripen.

The firm expanded through dedicated pools: a $200 million bio fund in 2015, another $1.5 billion core fund in 2016, and a $450 million second bio fund in 2017. The first $300 million crypto fund arrived in 2018, led by Dixon and Katie Haun. Haun had been a federal prosecutor and worked on the Mt. Gox case before joining Coinbase’s board. That path brought regulatory experience unusually well suited to the new category. The Cultural Leadership Fund also launched in 2018; the hosts mention criticism but do not unpack its structure in this episode.

In 2019, early and growth investing separated into a $750 million early fund and a $2 billion growth fund. In 2020, the firm raised about $6 billion across early, growth, bio and crypto pools. Another $2.2 billion crypto fund in 2021 brought total capital under management to just under $19 billion. The hosts count roughly a thousand investments in over five hundred companies, 160 exits, twenty public listings, twenty-two GPs and 220 employees. About 90% of the workforce were not GPs; eight network teams made the services promise a substantial organization.

The geography was changing as well. Covid-era hiring and investing extended beyond a firm that had strongly identified with the Bay Area. David Haber became its first New York-based GP. The hosts also acknowledge the growing media company but deliberately leave its detailed history and future for another venue. This companion follows their boundary rather than supplying a separate media-company narrative.

The cash-generating evidence took longer to appear than early failures. The episode invokes Michael Moritz’s phrase that apples take longer to ripen than lemons: Okta’s 2017 IPO was followed by Lyft, PagerDuty, Pinterest and Slack in 2019, Airbnb in 2020, then Affirm, Roblox and Coinbase in 2021. Their Roblox estimate is a particularly fast example of a large growth check becoming a large stake value. These public events finally gave outsiders enough information to see that the large-fund strategy belonged in the top-tier conversation, even without knowing actual LP distributions.

Chapter 10 · The 2021 bull and bear cases

Can the machine keep working?

The hosts distinguish an opportunity from an operating fact.

David’s bull case centers on crypto and the possibility of extending the brand internationally. A16z had been early, but not alone: USV and native crypto firms also mattered. The advantage he proposes combines accumulated success with the operational machinery to invest in tokens and navigate regulation. He qualifies his reconstruction of how separate crypto funds related to the venture exemption and the firm’s later registered-investment-adviser status. Ben adds that staying current across jurisdictions requires costly infrastructure.

Ben’s more speculative case is “HP 2.0”: could the firm become a platform on which people invent companies, sharing more fundamental operations than occasional help from specialists? He wonders about an idea-to-IPO dream machine, informed by his own startup-studio work. Neither host claims that this institution already exists. They connect the possibility to CAA’s packaging model while admitting that the precise shape remains unclear. David also thinks HP is an awkward contemporary brand for the ambition.

The bear case begins with timing. The firm had operated through an extraordinary technology bull market that began with its founding. Its combination of high prices, abundant capital and expensive services had not been tested in a sustained downturn. Ben asks what happens if follow-on money and LP demand dry up. Bigger technology markets had vindicated its underwriting so far, but that did not settle every future vintage.

Other risks came from the organization itself: overextension into a new model, the politics that can corrode a large partnership, and an underdog becoming the incumbent. David presents politics as a general organizational vulnerability, not an allegation about current misconduct. Tiger-style investors offered another challenge: cheap capital without the bundle. Experienced founders might prefer to build their own networks and capabilities. If a16z no longer offered the highest valuation, its services would have to earn the difference. The hosts remain long-term optimists, but their optimism does not erase these financing and execution questions.

Chapter 11 · Powers and playbook

From counter-positioning to brand

The institution changes what competitors must supply.

Ben identifies the early Power as counter-positioning in the stricter business-model sense. Incumbent partners accustomed to million-dollar salaries could not quickly agree to surrender those salaries and staff a large service organization. Marc and Ben’s wealth and operator habits let them make that commitment at the beginning. Over five to eight years, competitive pressure could force others to adapt; the original barrier was not permanent.

The more durable Power, the hosts argue, was brand. Capital’s central value remained capital, a commodity with many close substitutes, even though an exceptional board member or recruiter could change a company’s trajectory. Benchmark stood for craft and the individual partner; a16z stood for the franchise. Those brands could appeal to different founders. On the LP side, Ben cites a report of 30% carried interest as evidence of pricing power. On the founder side, brand could win entrance to a round and a meaningful allocation. The two audiences mattered differently.

Their playbook is a set of changed expectations: operators as investors, large expert teams, the language of partnership throughout the firm, transparency and content, PR, bigger checks at higher valuations, and a serious commitment to crypto. The hosts qualify the originality. Brad Feld and Fred Wilson already blogged; USV deserves credit in crypto; some practices existed in isolated form. Their argument is that a16z combined and repeated them until they became requirements for competing, rather than inventing every element from nothing.

They also examine the charge that the firm bought its brand by paying up. Ben argues that even an initially expensive investment could benefit continuing LPs if it helped create a leading franchise; the early funds’ eventual outcomes made the charge less persuasive to him. David finds it self-defeating for investors to publicly complain that valuations are too high when entrepreneurs are the customers. These are the hosts’ arguments about incentives and messaging, not a declaration that prices never matter.

Inside the partnership, Ben describes a non-consensus process in which an individual GP can champion an investment while a red team argues the bear case. Marc’s natural debates with Horowitz supplied the model. David likes the theory but questions how thoroughly it works when deals close in days or hours. The episode preserves that skepticism. A process described on podcasts is not proof of consistent practice.

Finally, they discuss Marc’s resistance to failing fast and constant pivoting. Persistent believers can create breakthroughs that early tests would have rejected, even at personal economic cost. David says persistence and testing can both be true; Ben distinguishes the benefits to society from what makes economic sense for the individual. Ben connects persistence to the time a brand needs to form. David argues that an established brand can survive losses that might have destroyed the first fund; he offers Clubhouse only as a hypothetical failure with its outcome still undecided. Ben is less relaxed about brand robustness in their own podcast: every poor episode could still cost a listener’s trust.

Chapter 12 · The closing grade

An A, with the books still closed

A powerful estimate is still an estimate.

For the final assessment, Ben explicitly says they are not a16z LPs and do not know its returns. They use financing data, public ownership disclosures and assumptions about liquidity. Their ten prominent liquid outcomes sum to roughly $25 billion of potential proceeds or stake value: Coinbase dominates at $11 billion, with estimates for Okta, Airbnb, Lyft, PagerDuty, Slack, Pinterest, Roblox, GitHub and Affirm. Some shares could have been sold earlier, distributed, or retained. The number is not audited fund performance, and an opportunity to sell is not a realized sale.

They then remove newer early, growth, bio and crypto capital that could not reasonably be judged by those exits. For easy arithmetic, Ben compares about $25 billion with an early pool of roughly $8 billion, describing a gross multiple around three times before numerous other holdings. The boundary is approximate rather than a complete fund-by-fund cash-flow model. Private Databricks and Instacart values and ownership remain guesses; possible Bitcoin or Ether exposure is even more tentative. None should be silently added as certain proceeds.

Their conclusion is narrower and stronger than a fabricated league table: a16z had successfully broken into a business with unusually persistent winners. They could not rank it reliably against Sequoia or Benchmark, but dismissing it as an unsuccessful strategy no longer fit the evidence. They interpret the negative 2016 returns narrative as premature because later successes, especially Coinbase, had not ripened. Both give an A. Ben withholds the plus pending clearer data; David contrasts it with a transformative outcome such as Facebook buying Instagram, and leaves open what future evidence could show.

The final emphasis is absolute dollars as well as multiples. A large result requires being right, having enough conviction to invest meaningfully, winning access, and first raising the capital to deploy. That is why a large institutional LP might care about a sizable gain even beside a smaller fund’s higher multiple. These remarks complete the business analysis. The conversation then turns to the hosts’ books, video and film recommendations and their appeal to share the episode personally. The story closes in 2021, with a franchise established and its next form still an open question.
