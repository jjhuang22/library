NvidiaPart I · The GPU Company

A new medium for stories. A crowded market for chips. Thirteen years of learning how to survive.

Released March 28, 2022Created UnpublishedAbout 18 minutes to read

1993–2006, with the founders’ earlier lives and the hosts’ March 2022 analysis.
Based on Acquired, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01 · 1963–1992

Before there was a GPU

A childhood across borders, then an education in how chips become products.

At nine, Jensen Huang arrived in eastern Kentucky with his older brother. Their parents, still unable to afford the move from Thailand to America, had found a school they could pay for: Oneida Baptist Institute. They had mistaken a reform school for a preparatory school. As David tells the story, Jensen’s roommate was a seventeen-year-old recently out of prison, recovering from seven stab wounds. The boys became friends. Jensen helped with mathematics; his roommate introduced him to weightlifting.

The hosts begin here because Huang’s later tolerance for discomfort had a history. Born in southern Taiwan in 1963, he had learned English from a dictionary with his mother, ten words a day, before ever seeing America. Once his parents could join their sons, the family settled first in Tacoma and then near Portland. Jensen played competitive table tennis, skipped two grades, and entered Oregon State at sixteen. Electrical engineering became both his intellectual home and the place he met Lori, his laboratory partner and future wife.

After graduating in 1984, he joined AMD in a role the hosts liken to a technically demanding chip-design product manager. He also began a Stanford master’s degree at night; finishing it took eight years. AMD taught him how hard the entire chip stack was. Designing and manufacturing everything internally had seemed exciting as a student. Working inside it made him imagine a different arrangement: tools and specialized companies that would let more people build chips.

LSI Logic offered a version of that world. It designed application-specific integrated circuits for customers building larger systems. Huang moved there and worked closely with Sun Microsystems, translating the requirements of people such as Andy Bechtolsheim and Vinod Khosla into chips that could actually be produced. By 1992 he was a respected bridge between ambitious engineers and the practical business of delivering their designs.

02 · 1992–1993

Three people and a new medium

The consumer PC looked like an opening; building the games was another question.

Around Thanksgiving 1992, two friends from Sun, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, asked Huang to discuss an idea. They met at Denny’s, where he had worked as a teenager. The two were formidable engineers. Huang understood how to turn engineering into a product. Together they imagined bringing the costly three-dimensional graphics of professional workstations to ordinary PCs.

The contrast was striking. Hollywood could afford Silicon Graphics machines; a child at home could not. Yet Wolfenstein 3D and, soon, Doom showed the appetite for immersive games on consumer computers. John Carmack’s unusual programming ability made those experiences possible. The proposed company would put specialized graphics hardware beside the CPU, taking graphics work away from the general-purpose processor and making richer worlds available to many more people.

Ben resists treating the opportunity as obvious. Would games become a large enough market? Would consumers choose PCs over dedicated consoles? Was there an ecosystem of developers ready to use this hardware? The company would need more than a chip: APIs, software-development kits and people willing to learn a new way of making games. Their long-term ambition was artistic storytelling, but their first customers still had to materialize.

When Huang resigned from LSI, CEO Wilf Corrigan asked the same developer question. He said he would keep Huang’s desk available. He also called Don Valentine at Sequoia. Corrigan’s endorsement mattered more than Huang’s nervous, unfinished pitch. Sequoia and Sutter Hill financed a $2 million round at a $6 million post-money valuation. David explicitly does not know the split between the firms.

The founders’ design files used “NV” for “next version.” Searching for a company name led them to invidia, Latin for envy, and then NVIDIA. But the opportunity attracted a crowd. In the hosts’ telling, roughly ninety companies pursued graphics chips. The idea could be easy to fund and still be a terrible place to earn durable profits.

03 · 1993–1996

The wrong shape of the future

A proprietary approach collided with the standard that developers actually adopted.

Nvidia initially tried to shape the industry around its own architecture. A Sega relationship promised a major customer, and the company chose quadrilaterals as the basic building blocks for its graphics. That decision was entangled with a wider attempt to supply the hardware, interfaces and development environment together.

Microsoft had different incentives. If games made PCs more desirable, Windows benefited. It wanted developers to reach many kinds of graphics hardware easily. Direct3D, part of the DirectX world, standardized around triangles. Competitors could adopt Microsoft’s interfaces rather than persuade developers to learn a separate system. Nvidia’s early start had put it on the wrong side of the emerging common language.

By 1996, Sega was moving away from the approach while Nvidia was already working on NV2, intended for Sega’s next console. Another early assumption had also gone wrong. Memory prices had fallen. The hosts describe Nvidia’s carefully memory-constrained design as carrying about $200 of memory costs against competitors’ roughly $50. Their point is not simply that the design was bad: it had optimized for a constraint that changed before the product could win.

Huang told his cofounders that the original plan had to be abandoned. Nvidia would use Microsoft’s standard and compete directly on performance. For engineers recruited to invent a new platform, this was a painful retreat. Ben frames it as the CEO admitting that strategy had failed and asking engineering to save the company. David adds the decisive requirement: they had to ship faster.

There were about nine months of cash left. Nvidia laid off roughly seventy percent of its staff, leaving about thirty-five people. The survivors had to design a new chip from scratch before the money ran out. An ordinary eighteen-to-twenty-four-month development cycle could not solve a nine-month problem.

04 · 1996–1997

One frame every thirty seconds

Survival required manufacturing a chip they had never tested as physical silicon.

Huang chose emulation. Nvidia spent about $1 million on hardware and software that could model the chip before it existed. Ben recalls that as roughly a third of the cash in the bank. It bought time by substituting painfully slow simulated tests for the usual cycle of ordering prototypes from a foundry, examining them and revising the design.

A game normally redraws the screen many times a second. The emulator produced about one frame every thirty seconds. Engineers could still compare the result against what it should have displayed, but the work demanded extraordinary patience. When they finished, Nvidia sent the design straight to production and ordered 100,000 units. It skipped the physical prototype stage.

The RIVA 128 arrived in 1997 with a formidable advantage: it embodied assumptions that competitors were still turning into chips they would ship much later. It was also imperfect. The hosts disagree about how many of Direct3D’s blend modes worked and how many failed. Both describe Nvidia asking developers to work within a limited reliable subset. The exact fraction is uncertain; the bargain is clear. Accept constraints, and get remarkable graphics performance now.

Developers accepted because consumers chose the graphics cards, whether installed by a computer manufacturer or added afterward. A developer wanted the game to run well on the hardware people would buy. A consumer wanted hardware that could run the most impressive games. Nvidia’s rushed campaign to work around defects became an education in those linked incentives. It sold one million RIVA 128 units within four months.

The emergency also left a repeatable capability. The hosts describe Nvidia delivering new generations on a six-month rhythm against rivals’ eighteen to twenty-four months. Their claim of roughly doubling delivered performance each generation describes this period’s competitive acceleration; it is not a promise of indefinite physical scaling. The company had escaped bankruptcy by changing the time required to turn a design into a sale.

05 · 1997–1999

A faster clock, a stronger partner

Manufacturing and branding supported the claim that graphics deserved its own processor.

Success made Nvidia credible to a manufacturer that had previously been out of reach. Huang wrote a physical letter to TSMC founder Morris Chang after struggling to reach the company through its salespeople. Chang read it and called. In the story the hosts recount, Huang answered amid the chaos of testing RIVA boards in the office, then shouted for everyone to be quiet: Morris Chang was on the phone.

The following year, Nvidia and TSMC signed a major multiyear agreement making TSMC its primary foundry. A better manufacturing partner reinforced the fast design process. In 1999, a naming contest produced “Geometry Force,” shortened to GeForce. The GeForce 256 carried the new identity and, in the hosts’ account, a large performance lead.

Nvidia marketed it as a GPU: a graphics processing unit. Ben notes that Sony had already used the term, so this is not a claim that Nvidia invented those letters. What mattered was the architectural assertion. A graphics chip was being presented as a processor with an independent future, rather than a temporary peripheral waiting to disappear inside Intel’s motherboard.

The threat was concrete. Sound and networking cards had once been separate businesses. Integrated versions became good enough for most users. Intel could let independent suppliers discover useful features and later absorb them. Integrated graphics already served customers who did not need a high-performance card. Why should Nvidia escape the same fate?

Graphics had one important property: much of the work could happen in parallel. Many pixels could be processed independently, unlike a chain of dependent instructions. The hosts use this to explain how Nvidia could pursue a different performance path from a CPU. But faster fixed-function graphics alone did not yet make a fully programmable processor. The next transformation would have to change what developers could ask the chip to do.

06 · 1999–2001

The GPU becomes programmable

Microsoft was a necessary partner—and a customer able to demand its share.

Nvidia went public at the beginning of 1999 at a market capitalization of about $600 million. Microsoft was meanwhile preparing the Xbox and wanted Nvidia’s graphics. The hosts describe a $500 million-a-year supply agreement with a $200 million advance. It was a very large commitment for a company still approaching a billion dollars in annual revenue.

The Xbox used a modified version of technology associated with the GeForce 3. Programmable shaders changed the meaning of the GPU. Earlier graphics accelerators performed a largely prescribed set of operations. Developers could now write small programs that affected lighting and other visual behavior on the graphics processor itself. A moving light could affect its surroundings dynamically, rather than relying only on a scene whose appearance had been prepared in advance.

Nvidia also developed Cg, extending C with graphics capabilities. Hardware and software together began to give developers a richer creative instrument. The hosts see this as the founders returning to their original platform ambition, this time working with Microsoft rather than against its standard. The investment was enormous. Ben recalls that the staffing required put the company under acute cash pressure again.

Nvidia partnered with AMD as well. At this point AMD was principally Intel’s CPU competitor; it had not yet acquired ATI. Intel tried discrete graphics and also built a successful integrated-graphics business, but the hosts argue that its integrated products did not satisfy users who demanded the highest graphical performance. Programmability made Nvidia harder to reduce to an interchangeable motherboard feature.

Revenue shows the scale of the change. For fiscal years ending January 31, the hosts give $158 million in 1999, $375 million in 2000, and $735 million in 2001. For the next year—largely calendar 2001, when Xbox launched—they give roughly $1.4 billion. Nvidia became, in their account, the fastest semiconductor company to reach a billion dollars in revenue and joined the S&P 500. Yet revenue growth and a powerful product did not settle who would keep the profits.

07 · 2001–2006

Another plateau, another wager

Scientific computing offered possibility before it offered an obvious market.

Competitors learned programmable graphics too. Revenue growth flattened after the early surge. Ben places revenue around $2.8 billion in 2005, but annual net income in these years was only a couple of hundred million dollars or less. Nvidia was not losing money; it was also not building an enormous cash surplus. Engineering leadership required continual reinvestment.

New users were beginning to treat graphics processors as something other than game hardware. The hosts repeat a story Huang tells about a Stanford quantum-chemistry researcher whose gamer son suggested buying GeForce cards at Fry’s. Porting a molecular model to Cg supposedly reduced weeks of supercomputer work to hours. David calls the story probably apocryphal; Ben suggests a composite of several experiences. It belongs here as an illustration of the opportunity they discuss, not as a verified benchmark or documented individual result.

The real transition was awkward enough without a perfect anecdote. Researchers were adapting consumer cards to parallel tasks they had not been designed to make easy. Tools still had to be built. Observers wondered whether Nvidia was neglecting gaming while ATI concentrated on it. Spending heavily on scientific computing could look like a distraction from the market that paid the bills.

AMD approached Nvidia about an acquisition before buying ATI. According to the hosts, Huang insisted on becoming CEO of the combined company; that condition ended the talks. AMD instead acquired ATI in 2006. Nvidia remained independent, facing a newly combined competitor and preparing another bet on what its parallel hardware might become.

The episode’s history stops at this threshold. The later machine-learning story belongs to the next installment. Here, the uncertainty still matters: Nvidia had proved people wanted its products, survived brutal competition, and helped establish a new medium. It had not yet proved that those achievements would yield enduring economic power.

08 · The 2006 crossroads, viewed in March 2022

Finding a market was not finding power

The hosts’ bull case, bear case and unsettled verdict on who captured the value.

Ben asks the listener to stand in 2006. The bear case begins with the income statement. Gross margin had been only 29% in 2004. That margin still had to pay engineers, overhead and the next generation of research. The 66% gross margin he contrasts with it is a figure from the hosts’ 2022 recording perspective, not a 2006 result.

Xbox explains part of the squeeze. David argues that the contract was strategically right: Microsoft helped Nvidia establish programmable graphics and resist Intel. But Microsoft could extract very favorable economics. As the original Xbox generation ended, Nvidia also faced the loss of a large revenue stream; it did not power the Xbox 360. Heavy spending on a speculative computing market therefore arrived just as a major gaming contract was rolling off.

Intel’s announced Larrabee effort added another threat. The supplier of integrated graphics was again aiming at a full GPU. Why assume scientific computing would be large enough to support an independent Nvidia? Ben’s bull case is the same bet viewed from the other side: parallel processing could address far more than scientific computing. In this episode that is a direction of travel, not a retrospective catalog of later AI achievements.

Product-market fit was one mountain; power was another

The hosts define power as the ability to earn persistent differential returns against a close competitor. Ben says Nvidia had found product-market fit but had not yet found that durable source of power. Customers could switch cards easily. Cg hinted at developer switching costs, but Microsoft wanted Windows applications to work across hardware suppliers; it had little incentive to build Nvidia a protected position.

David offers a narrower possibility: the six-month release process may have provided process power for a while. Rivals could not immediately copy the cadence. But the gap compressed over time. They do not turn early engineering excellence into an unqualified claim of permanent defensibility.

What the struggle left behind

Ben traces simulation back to the emulator that saved the company. Replacing a physical iteration with a modeled one changed development speed. David draws another thread from Huang’s AMD and LSI years: better tools let more people create. Without the graphics platform and its software, making a compelling game demanded the rare combination of engineering and storytelling represented by Carmack.

Keyhole supplies a small bridge beyond gaming. Nvidia invested in the company that became Google Earth, and Ben describes Huang wanting it to survive partly because it demonstrated Nvidia technology so well. A navigable model of the earth made the widening uses of computer graphics tangible.

The venture discussion is more unsettled. David argues that Sequoia and Sutter Hill’s original business thesis was wrong even though their investment was extraordinarily right: Huang kept remaking the business. Ben asks whether an investor should enter during the proliferation of competitors and back a founder, or wait for consolidation and pay more for an emerging winner. They leave that question open, noting that the staged financing choices available later scarcely existed in the same form at Nvidia’s founding.

An A, with Microsoft in the frame

For 1993–2006, David gives Nvidia an A rather than an A+. It survived, alongside ATI by a different route, and helped create and shepherd the industry. But Microsoft captured substantial value from both Nvidia’s escape from the crowded graphics market and its resistance to Intel. Windows’ position let Microsoft benefit while suppliers shouldered much of the hardware risk.

Ben challenges David’s suggestion that Microsoft did “basically nothing.” DirectX required real development, and the Xbox was a major project. David agrees that the characterization is unfair to Xbox. Their narrower argument is about position and capital allocation: Microsoft could reinforce its platform without accepting every burden of the graphics-chip business.

Ben also leaves a counter-question unresolved. Could Microsoft’s comfortable position have spared it the pressure to develop capabilities that Nvidia was forced to invent? He does not yet know enough about the next market to answer. At the 2006 stopping point, the judgment is simpler: Nvidia had repeatedly fought for its life and won, while Microsoft had used its existing position exceptionally well. The next act would test whether those hard-won capabilities could finally become lasting power.
