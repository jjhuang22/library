Part I

The learning machine

Before the holding company came a boy counting pennies, a partnership built for independence, and a series of costly changes of mind.

Acquired · Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal

Released April 21, 2021

Created Unpublished

Part I only · Early biography through the 1970 partnership wind-down, followed by the hosts’ analysis · About 22 minutes to read · Chapter navigation, without invented listening timestamps

Chapter 01 · 1867–1940

The family business

Independence begins as a concrete desire.

Long before Warren Buffett bought a textile company, the Buffett family was learning what it meant to work for someone else. In 1867 Sidney Buffett left his father’s Long Island farm, dissatisfied with his pay, and headed west to Omaha. The town was growing around the railroad. Sidney opened a grocery business; the next generation, represented by Ernest Buffett, carried on its austere habits. Ernest’s working creed combined long hours, low pay, firm opinions, and no foolishness.

Ernest wanted his children to become educated professionals. His son Howard studied journalism at the University of Nebraska, where he met Leila Stahl at the student newspaper. She was a promising student who was very good at mathematics, but left college when they married. Howard’s ambitions ran toward journalism and politics; Ernest steered him toward insurance. By 1927 Howard had moved again, this time into stockbroking. His customers wanted a share of the stock-market boom.

The crash made that career precarious. Warren was born on August 30, 1930; in 1931 Howard’s bank employer failed, taking the family’s savings with it. Ernest could not give him a paying job in the store. Howard instead opened his own brokerage, offering the people who still had money conservative utility securities and municipal bonds. The hosts describe a family that recovered surprisingly well from the Depression, even though Warren’s earliest circumstances had included genuine insecurity.

Young Warren counted whatever he could: bottle caps, letters in newspaper articles, passing license plates. A belt-mounted coin changer became an object of fascination. He bought gum in quantity and sold it door to door, then sold soda. Money was both a measurable score and a way to acquire more money. On a New York trip with Howard at age ten, he met Goldman Sachs’s Sidney Weinberg and visited the stock exchange. The elaborate service there impressed him less as luxury to consume than as evidence of how much wealth existed.

What he wanted from that wealth was freedom to choose his own work. He announced that he would become a millionaire by thirty-five. The hosts place that ambition in the Omaha of 1940, when it sounded far less ordinary than it might to a modern startup founder. The desire for independence would later reach beyond choosing investments: he would also try to choose the terms on which anybody could influence him.

Chapter 02 · 1940–1950

Pennies that buy more pennies

A boy discovers the arithmetic, then the people.

A library book, One Thousand Ways to Make $1000, supplied a thought experiment. A public weighing machine could collect pennies until it had earned a thousand dollars. Warren saw another possibility: use the early proceeds to buy a second machine, then a third, so that each new purchase accelerated the next. He wrote out the compounding calculations. He never actually bought those weighing machines. The distinction matters: the first empire was an imagined one, built in a notebook.

He did buy used pinball machines with a friend and place them in barbershops. They pretended to be employees of a larger business when customers complained, allowing an invented boss to absorb the pressure. Ben says they left when Warren learned that growing in the trade could mean dealing with the mafia. This was a practical education in collecting cash, maintaining a small operation, and deciding which complications to avoid.

Stocks brought a different complication. Warren persuaded his sister Doris to pool their money and bought six preferred shares of Cities Service at $38 apiece. They fell to $27. Doris worried; Warren felt responsible. When the price reached $40, he sold, relieved to return her money. It subsequently went above $200. He later named three lessons: disregard the purchase price when judging the future, resist a quick small gain, and manage the emotional demands of other people whose money you hold. David argues that the third lesson stuck much sooner than the first two.

Howard’s election to Congress moved the family to Washington, which Warren disliked. He briefly returned to Omaha expecting a partnership with Ernest, and instead got low-paid shelf-stocking work. Ernest even withheld a little pay to demonstrate taxes. Charlie Munger had once worked in the same store, but the two future partners would not meet until 1959. Back in Washington, Warren found a more congenial enterprise delivering the Washington Post. His route also gave him customers for magazines and calendars; discarded subscription labels helped him learn when renewals were due.

In high school he was making about $175 a month and had saved more than $2,000. At fifteen he spent $1,200 on a Nebraska tenant farm. The tenant did the farming and split crop profits equally with the owner. Capital could earn while somebody else performed the work. After graduating high school in 1947, Warren reluctantly went to Wharton, transferred to Nebraska when the family returned home, and accelerated his degree. While studying, he managed rural circulation for the Lincoln Journal, with fifty paperboys reporting to him. By nineteen, the reluctant student was ready for a new kind of education.

Chapter 03 · 1950–1952

Finding a teacher—and GEICO

Fundamental analysis offers a language for the obsession.

Harvard Business School rejected him. Looking through other schools’ catalogs, Warren discovered that Benjamin Graham and David Dodd were teaching at Columbia. He had devoured Graham’s The Intelligent Investor and wanted direct access to the people behind this systematic way of thinking. He wrote after the application deadline. Dodd, who chaired admissions, admitted him without the usual process. Warren arrived in New York in fall 1950 with about $10,000 and rented a YMCA room for a dollar a day.

Graham and Dodd treated a share as ownership of a business, distinguished its price from its value, and insisted on a margin of safety. The hosts embrace those principles while disputing Graham’s narrow implementation. His favored bargains were companies whose realizable assets appeared worth more than their market prices: businesses worth more dead than alive. A discarded cigar might still offer a free puff. In a market with fewer analysts and less available information, these cigar butts could be profitable.

Before Graham’s seminar began, Warren noticed something intriguing in a stock manual. Graham-Newman owned 55 percent of the Government Employees Insurance Company, and Graham chaired its board. On a Saturday he took a train to Washington and persuaded a guard to let him speak to somebody. Lorimer Davidson, GEICO’s finance leader and investment manager, expected a brief encounter. The conversation lasted four hours.

GEICO sold directly, avoiding the agent expense borne by other insurers, and initially selected government employees as relatively responsible drivers. Davidson explained the money between premium collection and claim payment: float. Premiums arrived before the corresponding cash outflows, sometimes long before. An insurer could invest that money in the meantime. The hosts are excited by the distributed nature of those liabilities, but also discuss catastrophes, underwriting economics, and the need to meet claims. Float is money held against obligations; its attractiveness depends on how those obligations and investments behave.

Warren returned and put roughly 75 percent of his portfolio into GEICO. Graham was unimpressed by the concentration and by an investment that did not fit his usual bargain template. Davidson’s business explanation and Graham’s price discipline were pulling the student in different directions. By early 1952, Warren had sold his GEICO position for $15,259. The hosts report an annualized return above 50 percent, then emphasize the much larger gain he forfeited by selling.

David’s criticism of cigar butts has two parts. Book values may fail when unwanted factories or equipment must actually be sold. And even a successful bargain usually ends: another idea, transaction, and tax bill must replace it. Ben compares this repeated labor to a business that must continually make and sell another product. A company capable of increasing its own value could spare its owner that cycle. They also give Graham his historical context: after the Depression and decades of damaged market confidence, insisting on a tangible margin of safety was understandable.

Chapter 04 · 1951–1956

The prescriptionist becomes a principal

The job he wanted is followed by the freedom he wanted more.

Warren excelled in Graham’s class, receiving the A+ the hosts describe as unique. He also met Bill Ruane, a broker auditing the course. But Graham-Newman initially refused to employ him. The partners reserved their small firm’s jobs for Jewish applicants who faced discrimination elsewhere. The hosts explain the decision in that context; Warren remained disappointed and persistent.

Graham and Howard discouraged him from immediately opening his own partnership. He went home to work at Buffett-Falk instead. Stockbroking exposed an incentive problem he could not enjoy: pay followed selling securities and generating commissions, rather than the eventual results for clients. He later likened the role to a medical prescriptionist paid for the quantity and type of pills prescribed. His enthusiasm for GEICO did not make customers especially eager to buy an insurer without familiar agents.

The Omaha interval nevertheless changed his life. He married Susie Thompson, whose father had worked on Howard’s political campaigns, and persuaded Howard to establish Buffett and Buffett, combining some of their money for Warren to invest. He was beginning to act as a principal. His intensity also had a personal cost. The hosts describe a man who might leave a dinner party to read annual reports upstairs, with little interest in socializing for its own sake.

He kept writing Graham and Newman, discussing investment ideas and visiting New York. In 1954 they made an exception and hired him. He accepted immediately and appeared at the office a month before his paid start. Susie and their young daughter moved with him to an inexpensive apartment in White Plains. At Graham-Newman, he became central to investment ideas and decisions. When the older partners prepared to retire, they offered him the chance to continue the firm.

He declined. He had gone to New York to work with them; he did not want to remain there to run their institution. In 1956 the family returned to Omaha. Warren had about $175,000 and proposed retirement on a $12,000 annual household budget. He could invest his own capital, spend time at home, and live well. The proposal lasted only until friends and relatives began asking what he might do with their money.

Chapter 05 · 1956–1962

A partnership on his terms

The contract protects both the capital and the decision maker.

Warren began organizing small partnerships for friends and family. David describes the early arrangement as a four percent annual hurdle, with Warren retaining half of returns above it, no management fee, and personal reimbursement of a quarter of partners’ capital losses. Ben initially remembers a 25 percent share of upside instead of half; David attributes his version to The Snowball. David also qualifies his understanding of the neutral band between zero and four percent. These are the episode’s account of early terms, rather than a claim that every later Buffett vehicle used the same contract.

He initially contributed only $100 to each pool, keeping his own investments separate while making similar investments personally. The loss promise was additional reimbursement, not merely the risk of losing a large contribution of his own. He intended to retain his earned interest as ownership in the pools. As performance accumulated, his share could grow without regularly withdrawing cash.

Costs were almost comically small. Working without employees or outside services, Warren handled investments, accounts, and taxes himself. David gives total 1956 expenses as $22.71. By year-end the capital under management exceeded half a million dollars. The Davis family invested $100,000 and remarked on his resemblance to a brilliant former Omaha neighbor, Charlie Munger. Their eventual introduction in 1959 began a relationship whose fuller story the hosts reserve for another episode.

Another term gave the manager room to think. Partners could add or withdraw money on December 31, while the holdings themselves remained undisclosed. The Cities Service experience had become a structure: investors would receive results and explanations without being able to demand an emotionally driven trade whenever prices moved. The hosts later connect this secrecy to the avoidance of a different temptation, publicizing an idea merely to look clever before the investment was complete.

With close to a million dollars across seven pools, Warren could do more than wait for quoted prices to improve. At Sanborn Map he put 35 percent of partnership capital into a position, gained control, and forced a separation that produced a quick 50 percent profit. This was the activist side of Graham’s method: buy assets cheaply, then make the value distributable. The hosts explicitly compare it to corporate raiding.

Results drew more capital. Ben gives cumulative gains of 141 percent across the first four years against 43 percent for the Dow, and 46 percent for the partnerships in 1961 against 22.4 percent for the Dow. By then assets exceeded $7 million. In 1962 Warren rented an office, hired help, consolidated the vehicles as Buffett Partnership Limited, and committed his family’s own wealth. His ground rules promised value rather than popularity, a focus on permanent capital loss rather than fluctuating quotations, and virtually his entire family net worth alongside the partners. At thirty-one he crossed a million dollars, four years ahead of the childhood deadline. His letters repeatedly warned that the advantage over the market could not last.

Chapter 06 · 1963–1965

A scandal outside the customer’s mind

American Express makes an intangible asset visible.

American Express was already a trusted financial name when a small warehouse-receipts subsidiary became entangled in the salad-oil scandal. Travelers’ checks worked because people could pay AmEx in advance, carry its promises while traveling, replace lost checks, and obtain local currency through a widely trusted institution. That business held substantial cash before customers redeemed the paper. A warehouse receipt applied a related promise to stored commodities: an inspection certified goods against which somebody could borrow.

Anthony “Tino” De Angelis exploited that certification by filling tanks largely with seawater instead of the soybean oil they were supposed to contain. The episode describes more than $150 million of fraud, with associated speculation going wrong. Whether AmEx was legally responsible for the full loss was debatable. Its chief executive nevertheless chose to settle with creditors. The share price fell more than half, and Wall Street questioned the company’s survival.

Warren and his staff investigated a different question from the market’s immediate fear. They interviewed consumers and banks in Omaha, New York, and elsewhere. Were people abandoning the checks or cards? Many had not heard of the scandal. The operating trust seemed intact. Buffett judged that AmEx could absorb the damage, pointing to more than $200 million of cash and over $500 million of travelers’ check float. That reasoning depended on the absence of a run: liabilities dispersed among customers were manageable if those customers continued to trust the institution.

BPL had over $17 million when he initially invested $3 million. The position later grew to $13 million, representing five percent of AmEx. Those figures describe stages of the investment, not an assertion that thirteen million went into it from the original seventeen-million pool. AmEx settled for $60 million the next year, the stock recovered, and the partnership made about two and a half times its invested money.

Then he sold. The hosts see both progress and repetition in the outcome. Buffett had looked beyond machines and balance-sheet assets to a brand whose value could be tested in customers’ behavior. But he still sold when the immediate bargain had worked, as he had with GEICO. The lesson that a good business could keep increasing in value had not yet displaced the habit of collecting the available puff and searching again.

Chapter 07 · 1962–1965

The twelve-and-a-half-cent grievance

A successful tender offer leaves him with the wrong prize.

The next apparent bargain was a New England textile manufacturer. The hosts place its asset book value around $20 a share against a stock price of $7.50. Berkshire Hathaway descended from a collection of mills, including Hathaway Manufacturing, founded in 1888 as New Bedford sought an industry beyond whaling. Its physical plant carried the weight of that history. Cotton had to travel north, labor was costly, and industrial air conditioning later helped mills operate nearer southern cotton production.

Seabury Stanton, a descendant of the old mill leadership, viewed his task as preserving the industry. He spent heavily on modernization. Buffett saw capital being poured into an operation with poor economics, but initially did not plan to run it himself. He bought shares expecting a profitable exit. Stanton bought shares too, and a company tender offer seemed a way for Warren to sell.

According to Buffett’s telling, they shook hands on $11.50 a share. The formal offer arrived at $11⅜, a difference of twelve and a half cents. Buffett became furious. The hosts cannot establish exactly why this relatively small gap provoked such an uncharacteristic reaction. They suggest the importance he placed on keeping one’s word, and David speculates that Howard’s illness and death around this period may have affected him. These are interpretations, not a proven account of his state of mind.

Instead of selling anyway or simply refusing, he canvassed shareholders for enough stock to take control. By April 1965 he had a board seat; the next month he forced Stanton out and became chairman. The reward was a business he had never wanted to operate. Liquidation value ceased to look dependable when the question became who would actually buy unwanted mills and equipment. Closing the operation also meant throwing people out of work and damaging the community. The hosts discuss roughly 15,000 mill jobs when framing that dilemma.

Buffett curtailed the lavish reinvestment while keeping the operation running. Reputation and responsibility complicated the easy image of an asset sale. His later cigar-butt description was bleak: Berkshire had no puff left. In a 2010 counterfactual cited by the hosts, he estimated that putting the original money directly into insurance instead would have produced another $200 billion by then. It is Buffett’s compounded opportunity-cost estimate, not an observed loss or a calculation independently established here. David nevertheless wonders whether the bad purchase created the circumstances for the next insight.

Chapter 08 · 1965–1968

Too much success, and a boundary around technology

The circle of competence also excludes a remarkable opportunity.

American Express helped bring partnership assets to $37 million by the end of 1965, with Warren’s net worth around $7 million. Another $6.8 million arrived from partners at year-end. For the first time he struggled to deploy everything. He held reserves and closed the partnership to new capital. Ben compares the decision to a seed investor refusing to enlarge a fund beyond the opportunities its strategy can handle.

In 1967 Buffett wrote that the partnership would avoid businesses whose investment case required understanding technology. That boundary ran straight through an extraordinary connection. Susie’s civil-rights work helped bring him to Grinnell College, where hearing Martin Luther King Jr. moved him. He became a trustee and chaired its investment committee. Fellow trustee Robert Noyce later brought the college an opportunity to invest $100,000 in Intel’s founding financing. Buffett approved Grinnell’s investment, yet made none for himself or the partnership.

David is astonished. Buffett had learned industries such as insurance and textiles after first encountering them, so why should technology be categorically different? He imagines an enormous missed possibility. Ben asks harder questions about what Warren would have owned and how long he would have kept it. The partnership’s experience was chiefly with established quoted businesses; Intel was something earlier and less familiar.

The closing discussion revisits the issue rather than leaving David’s first reaction as the final verdict. A startup needs more than one independent believer: later financiers, recruits, partners, and customers must come along. Ben and David contrast that need with evaluating an existing asset independently of popular opinion. David concedes that this may be why technology investing would not have worked for Buffett then. The omission remains striking, but the hosts do not agree that simply inserting Intel into the old strategy would have reproduced a venture firm’s results.

Chapter 09 · 1967

The mill acquires an insurance company

Separate businesses can meet at the capital account.

Berkshire’s factories were still losing money. Stopping the reinvestment did not make their products competitive, and the company’s cash reserves would eventually disappear if nothing changed. Warren began looking for another business to buy with the money inside Berkshire. In Omaha he had been watching National Indemnity, run by Jack Ringwalt.

National was unlike GEICO. Where GEICO preferred a broad pool of relatively safe drivers, National priced unusual and difficult risks. Ringwalt’s principle was that the problem was a bad rate rather than an inherently bad risk. He personally investigated situations to understand what he was promising. These policies could leave premiums available for long periods, sometimes because the covered event never occurred. The attraction depended on disciplined pricing and the actual pattern of future claims.

Ringwalt occasionally wanted to sell and then changed his mind. In February 1967 Buffett caught the opportunity. They agreed terms in about fifteen minutes: a one-page deal, Ringwalt’s price, no audited financial statements, a promise to stay in Omaha, and a promise not to fire employees. Ringwalt remained to run the company. Buffett bought it for Berkshire rather than BPL. An insurer had become part of the textile company.

The hosts regard what followed as the crucial capital-allocation insight, while leaving open how much Buffett planned in advance and how much he discovered while solving Berkshire’s problem. Insurance provided investable float. Other operating businesses could generate cash that supported the insurance operation if claims or underwriting turned unfavorable. Berkshire could therefore use capital more aggressively than an isolated insurer with only its own resources.

The connection was financial rather than operational. Managers did not need to cross-sell one another’s products or merge their organizations. Cash needs and surplus cash could meet at the central office, where Buffett chose their use. The hosts quote him explaining that additional resources in Berkshire could supply National if underwriting soured. Their excited language about free capital and a stacked deck describes an advantage, not the disappearance of obligations. The logic relies on sufficient operating cash, available support, and sound underwriting; claims still have to be paid.

Ben joins three capacities in this purchase: independent judgment, probabilistic thinking, and capital allocation. The AmEx wager had required estimating the risk of a run rather than treating any scandal as fatal. National applied the same temperament to pricing liabilities and deciding what to do with the cash. For David, this was the beginning of a holding company with a purpose, where the pieces reinforced one another through their financing.

Chapter 10 · 1968–1970

Stopping after the best year

The new structure exists before he can see his next career.

The partnership returned 36 percent in 1967 and 59 percent in 1968, its best year. Yet rising prices made Buffett increasingly unhappy: the purchase prices on which his method depended were harder to find. His 1967 letter refused to abandon an approach he understood for apparently easy gains from one he did not. The hosts describe somebody with more to lose, not somebody liberated by his success.

Even National Indemnity did not immediately settle the problem in his mind. In 1968 he tried to sell Berkshire outright to Munger and David Gottesman. They declined. David reads Charlie’s response as an expression of respect: why would he want to buy something Buffett himself wanted to sell? The architecture of the next five decades was beginning to form, but Buffett still felt trapped by the scale and expectations of the old partnership.

On Memorial Day 1969 he told partners that public participation made him inescapably competitive. To stop pacing an investment rabbit, he would have to stop. He planned to retire at year-end, wind down the partnership, and distribute its holdings in early 1970. Susie also had reason to ask why their many millions had not brought more of his time home. He was still a comparatively private investor, not yet the national public figure later readers might imagine.

Partners asked where to go. Munger did not want an influx of investors either. Buffett directed larger clients to Gottesman and smaller ones to Bill Ruane, the former Columbia classmate now establishing the Sequoia Fund, a different institution from Sequoia Capital. In January 1970 public holdings were liquidated and BPL unwound. Buffett owned about 26 percent of the partnership by then.

His distribution included $16 million in cash, 18 percent of Berkshire, 20 percent of Diversified Retail Company, and two percent of Blue Chip Stamps. He also owned the Omaha Sun. The episode discusses these retained interests as its private-company holdings and does not resolve every legal-status shorthand used in the conversation. Partners could keep their stakes or sell them, with Buffett willing to buy. Asked whether they should hold, he said he intended to hold his own and buy more. The retirement announcement ended the partnership without ending his interest in the game.

The hosts disagree about this stop. Ben sees discipline in refusing to raise more money in a boom when bargains are scarce. David thinks closing was probably wrong in isolation, even if it enabled the later Berkshire. He also interprets it as a psychological transition: the extraordinarily successful Graham disciple might have needed to end that identity before embracing a different one. That is David’s reading of the change, not a plan Buffett is shown to have articulated.

Chapter 11 · The hosts’ closing analysis

Exceptional returns, unresolved value

The grade depends on what the achievement is being asked to prove.

Ben returns to compounding through the tax illustration discussed on the show: a dollar compounding at 15 percent for twenty-five years, with a 25 percent tax assumption and realizations every five years versus a long hold. He quotes roughly $24.90 for the uninterrupted path and $16.80 for repeated realizations, calling the difference about fifty percent. The spoken presentation does not clearly reconcile the same terminal tax treatment in both figures. These are the episode’s illustrative numbers, not a new precise after-tax comparison. Its point is the friction from repeatedly realizing gains, paying taxes, and finding another investment.

That point also makes GEICO and AmEx painful. David believes those two early sales prevented a much larger result despite everything Buffett achieved. The hosts do not treat his later principles as a strategy he possessed fully formed at ten. He had to discover the strengths of good businesses while repeatedly abandoning them for the next cheap security.

Independence of thought is another recurring strength, with limits. Other people’s agreement does not make an analysis right, any more than their disagreement makes it wrong. But the hosts acknowledge that venture businesses often need shared belief to keep building. They also contrast Buffett’s advice about broad diversification with his own concentrated holdings: concentration made sense to him when he believed he had an analytical edge and excellent managers, while index-like breadth served people without that conviction. The episode presents his choices as those of an exceptional investor, not an automatic prescription for its audience.

Ben suggests that secrecy counterpositioned the partnership against stock pickers paid to appear smart in the short term. Warren could keep an idea quiet and retain its economic benefit rather than moving the market through publicity. The incentive design mattered: commission sales had rewarded transactions, while this structure rewarded longer-term results. This is the hosts’ specific counterpositioning argument, not a complete Seven Powers scorecard.

They connect the record to singular focus and communication as well as raw ability. Buffett spent hours reading reports and discussing ideas, and had trained himself in speaking and writing. Ben explicitly insists that the life was costly in other dimensions. Someone seeking a rounded personal life would not get it merely by copying such absorption. Susie and the dinner-party absences belong in the account of the achievement, too.

Then they separate money made for investors from value created for the world. A cigar-butt activist can close a going concern, remove jobs, and stop serving customers: value destruction is a real possibility. Graham’s school also helped replace speculation with fundamental analysis, benefiting the institutions and pension savers able to invest on that basis. Those broader contributions do not automatically prove that every Buffett transaction enlarged the economic pie.

Through the story’s 1970 boundary, Ben and David struggle to name new products or additional productive activity caused by BPL itself. David judges Buffett’s intervention in the textile business probably neutral because the mills were already in decline; they still ask whether he accelerated that decline. They regard the early partnerships principally as value capture and groundwork for something else. Later examples they mention are deferred to the next episode, not imported here as evidence of what the partnership already accomplished.

On investment performance, however, both give an A+. They describe about 29.5 percent annual compounding over twelve years and roughly 28 times money, with a positive result every year and performance above the Dow. These are the hosts’ rounded partnership-level figures; the episode does not establish them as every investor’s net return. As a fund record, with periodic withdrawal opportunities rather than venture-style locked capital, the result is extraordinary. David qualifies the comparison: an individual investment can multiply much more, especially in the 2021 technology and crypto environment from which they are speaking. Ben adds that the era and the amount of capital matter.

The episode ends without pretending that this resolves Buffett. His financial grade is exceptional; his contribution beyond investors is debated; the wisdom of retirement is contested; his next strategy is visible before he fully embraces it. In their outro the hosts recommend Balaji Srinivasan’s Tim Ferriss conversation and Packy McCormick’s first-year Not Boring essay, then look forward to the May 1, 2021 Berkshire meeting and a later installment. Part I leaves Warren holding Berkshire, ready to buy more, while announcing that he is done.
