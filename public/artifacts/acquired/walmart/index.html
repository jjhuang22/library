Acquired · Spring 2022

Walmart

The machinery of low prices

A merchant learned to sell for less. Then he built the roads, routines, and information systems that made the promise possible.

Released July 19, 2022Created UnpublishedAbout 21 minutes to read

A companion to Acquired’s Walmart episode with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.
The story and analysis follow the July 2022 recording.

Chapter 01 · 1918–1941

Learning to sell

Before the discount store, there was a newspaper route.

Samuel Moore Walton was born in Kingfisher, Oklahoma, in March 1918. His father, Tom, moved from farming into financing farms, and the family eventually settled in Columbia, Missouri. The Depression exposed the children to both sides of that business. As crops failed and farmers lost their homes, Tom traveled to foreclose on properties, sometimes taking Sam and his younger brother Bud along. Sam remembered parents who shared a simple approach to money: they did not spend it.

The household found work wherever it could. His mother started a milk business; the boys helped with cows and deliveries. Sam sold magazine subscriptions, raised rabbits and pigeons, and delivered the Columbia Missourian. At the University of Missouri, where an ROTC scholarship helped him attend, he hired others to make newspaper deliveries while he concentrated on selling subscriptions. By the end of college, the operation was earning him roughly $4,000–$5,000 a year, according to the episode.

He was also a class president, an unusually young Eagle Scout, and the quarterback of a high-school team that, in his telling, never lost a game. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal put those experiences beside the foreclosures: deprivation taught him thrift, while winning taught him to expect another victory. Neither alone explains the merchant he became. His later stores would combine ambitious goals with small experiments that could fail without sinking the company.

Sam did not describe his first retail job as a carefully planned apprenticeship. He had hoped to attend Wharton but could not afford it. Tired of the work required to support himself, he accepted a job at JCPenney in Des Moines rather than the competing offer from Sears. He proved an effective floor salesman. James Cash Penney himself showed him how to wrap merchandise attractively with as little paper and twine as possible. Eighteen months later, Pearl Harbor interrupted the career.

Chapter 02 · 1941–1945

Helen sets the boundaries

A family decision determined the geography of an empire.

Sam expected to serve overseas, but a heart irregularity kept him out of combat duty. Bud became a Navy bomber pilot; Sam returned to Oklahoma while awaiting his Army assignment. In Claremore he met Helen Robson at a bowling alley. Her father, L. S. Robson, was a successful businessman and financier, and Helen had studied finance herself. Marriage brought Sam a partner in the business and access to a source of capital his own family could not provide.

After wartime postings around the United States, Sam wanted to return to retail as an owner. He and a friend proposed buying a department-store franchise in St. Louis. Helen vetoed it. She wanted to raise their children in a small town, and her family had seen outside partnerships go wrong. Their next business would be controlled by the family, in a town of no more than 10,000 people. The hosts emphasize the contingency: the celebrated small-town strategy began with Helen rejecting Sam’s preferred plan.

The Robsons also supplied an ownership model. Helen’s father had organized his ranch and businesses as a family partnership. Sam and Helen adopted that structure in 1953, creating the predecessor of Walton Enterprises. Family members could not simply sell a slice of the partnership on their own; the family had to decide together. The hosts connect this arrangement to the family’s continued control of just over half of Walmart at the time of the recording. Sam later warned his descendants, in his autobiography, against breaking it apart.

Chapter 03 · 1945–1950

The store he could not keep

A bad purchase became a good business—and a painful lesson in leases.

The Butler Brothers franchise organization could not offer Sam a department store in such a small market. It could offer a Ben Franklin variety store in Newport, Arkansas, a town of about 7,000. Sam and Helen bought it for $25,000: $5,000 of savings and a $20,000 loan from her father. Only afterward did Sam appreciate why the previous owner wanted out. Annual sales were about $72,000, rent consumed 5% of sales, and John Dunham’s competing Sterling Store across the street sold more than twice as much.

These variety stores were a different shopping experience from the one Walmart later made familiar. Customers often asked a clerk to fetch merchandise from behind a counter. The franchisor supplied inventory and marked it up on its way from manufacturers to merchants. Sam had bought a place in that system, but set himself an improbable goal: within five years, his store would be the most profitable variety store in Arkansas.

He began across the street, studying Dunham. Competitor visits became a lifelong habit: first a yellow legal pad, later a tape recorder, questions to clerks, and even searches for discarded business records. Promotions and lower prices drew customers. To make those prices possible, he found local manufacturers and arranged direct purchases outside the usual franchise supply chain. With a trailer behind his pickup, he could buy, load the goods, drive back to Newport, and put them on sale.

The central arithmetic was simple, though the operational work was not. Sam’s example was an item bought for 80 cents: at $1.20 he earned 40 cents per unit; at $1 he earned only 20 cents, but could sell three times as many. In that example, total gross profit rose from 40 cents to 60 cents for every original unit of demand. The hosts distinguish this broad discounting logic from loss leaders, where selected items could carry little or no margin to bring shoppers through the door. Sam was learning how to combine both.

By the fifth year, the Newport store was doing about $250,000 in sales and $30,000–$40,000 in annual profit in the hosts’ account. He had achieved his goal. Then his lease expired. It contained no renewal option, and the landlord wanted the successful store for his own family. Newport offered no easy substitute storefront. The landlord paid $50,000 for the franchise, inventory, fixtures, and other assets, but Sam had lost the business he had built. In 1950, the Waltons went looking for another town.

Chapter 04 · 1950–late 1950s

Let the customers browse

A bus trip brought a different kind of store to Bentonville.

Northwest Arkansas put them nearer Helen’s family in Oklahoma and, as Sam freely admitted, good quail hunting. Bentonville had only about 3,000 people and already supported three variety stores. The Ben Franklin franchise they bought had sold roughly $32,000 the previous year. After Newport, it was a small base from which to start again.

Sam heard that two Ben Franklin stores in Minnesota were experimenting with self-service. He took an overnight bus to see them. Customers entered the selling floor, chose their own goods, and paid at checkout counters instead of having clerks retrieve everything. He liked what he saw and adopted it. On July 29, 1950, the Bentonville store reopened as Walton’s Five and Dime, still a Ben Franklin franchise but bearing the family name. The hosts describe it as the country’s third self-service variety store.

David believes the first year produced about $90,000 in sales, while explicitly saying he could not pin that figure down exactly. The hosts speculate that novelty and better shopping brought people from surrounding towns; they do not establish what happened to the other Bentonville stores. What mattered to Sam was that rural shoppers wanted the same attractive retail experiences they knew existed in cities. Their address did not make them unsophisticated customers.

In 1952, expansion to Fayetteville required someone other than Sam to run a store. He recruited Willard Walker from Tulsa by offering a share of that store’s profits. Each new store became its own partnership. Existing managers could also invest their money in subsequent stores, giving them a reason to share useful ideas rather than protect them. This ownership opportunity initially belonged to managers, not hourly employees. After the company went public, the hosts say, Sam extended profit sharing and an employee stock purchase program to associates; they recount examples of long-serving hourly workers building substantial wealth.

Chapter 05 · Late 1950s–1962

Leaving the franchise behind

Bigger stores were possible. A different supply chain was harder.

Sam and Bud tried a Ben Franklin in the Ruskin Heights shopping development outside Kansas City. Its success convinced Sam that shopping centers were the future. But persuading town planners and assembling developments with multiple tenants was slow. He turned instead toward larger stores he could organize himself. These “family centers” could do $1 million or $2 million in sales, yet remained tied to the franchise supply system and its prices.

Other merchants were testing a more radical approach. Sam visited Ann & Hope in New England and Sol Price’s FedMart in California. Discounters combined lower markups across the store with eye-catching bargains, buying directly from manufacturers and operating the back end themselves. In the hosts’ comparison, the older retail model often marked goods up around 45%, after the intermediary’s own markup. Cutting those layers offered room to lower prices considerably.

Sam first asked Butler Brothers to help him bring discounting to Arkansas. He wanted its scale and distribution capability behind the new stores. It refused. Ben and David view this as counter positioning: eliminating the markup for Sam would threaten the economics and relationships of the franchisor’s existing business. It was understandable for Butler Brothers and dangerous for its future. Sam believed someone would bring discounting to small towns even if he did not.

He and Bud secured a site in Rogers. Store manager Bob Bogle proposed a short name that would cost less to put in neon: Walmart. Sam liked the economy and the echo of FedMart. On July 2, 1962, at age 44, he opened the first Walmart. The birthday can obscure the apprenticeship. Almost two decades of contracts, merchandising, experiments, and store ownership lay behind the new sign.

Chapter 06 · 1962–1970

Cheap goods, expensive problems

The promise was low prices on everything; the back end began in chaos.

The early stores were designed to make an event of shopping. The hosts compare their arrival in a small town to a UFO landing. Donkey rides and other promotions attracted attention. At the Harrison opening—the second Walmart, not the first—watermelons sat outside in sweltering heat and burst. Behind the spectacle, the company was trying to source merchandise and arrange deliveries without the franchise organization that had previously done that work.

The substantive promise was much more disciplined than the openings looked. Charlie Cate, manager of the Rogers store, remembered Sam insisting that this was not Ben Franklin with a few cheap items. Everything was to be discounted. Rogers sold about $1 million in its first year. Harrison tested whether the concept could win a smaller town; Springdale tested a larger market and quickly became the highest-selling store in the Walton group. Customers responded to both.

Sam initially imagined perhaps 10 or 15 stores because he wanted to visit and understand every one. There was little middle management between the store managers, Sam, and Bud. Mandatory Saturday meetings gathered managers to compare profit-and-loss statements and exchange what worked. Their stakes in one another’s stores helped make that openness economically sensible. Sam’s obsession was the speed with which sales information could reach him, reach the other managers, and change what happened on the selling floor.

The competition was formidable. Kmart, Target, and Woolco also began in 1962, each backed by an established retailer. By roughly 1967, Kmart had about 250 stores and $800 million in sales while Walmart revenue was around $10 million. David resists the easy explanation that Kmart simply refused to accept discount margins: it was well managed and its parent was willing to invest. Its inherited distribution network made rapid national expansion possible. Walmart had to construct one from scratch.

That handicap eventually mattered in the other direction. Kmart’s infrastructure had been built for the older retail system. Walmart’s was shaped around thin margins, fast inventory turnover, and cash needed to fund more stores. The hosts see its early capital constraint as an important source of discipline, while noting that Sam himself had wanted the easier route of a corporate partner. The result was not his original master plan.

Chapter 07 · 1960s–1970s

Flying over the next town

Expansion needed both local knowledge and new capital.

Bud’s wartime flying experience helped introduce a practical tool for expansion. The brothers used propeller planes to inspect towns and potential store sites from the air, then tracked down landowners to negotiate. Sam learned to fly rather than simply hiring a scouting organization. An airplane let him cover territory while remaining close to the stores. The episode treats these flights as working equipment in the same frugal culture as his old pickup and bird dogs.

Walmart reached 24 stores by 1968 and filed to go public with 32 stores and around 1,000 employees. A weak market delayed the offering, but on October 1, 1970, it went public. The hosts describe 300,000 shares sold at $15, raising $4.5 million, with only about 800 shareholders participating. It was still a regional company that most investors and consumers did not know well.

Growth soon made the small offering look consequential. Revenue rose 77% in the first year after the IPO. David calculates compound annual revenue growth of about 40.1% through the 1970s and 32.4% through the 1980s. The starting base was small, and these are the hosts’ historical calculations, not a promise about what followed. By 1977, the company had roughly half a billion dollars in sales while its market capitalization was still only about $135 million.

Chapter 08 · 1966–early 1990s

Build the machinery of low prices

Computers, trucks, and dense routes changed what a store could charge.

In 1966, Sam enrolled in an IBM seminar in Poughkeepsie on using computers in business. He did not become a technologist. Instead, he made room for technically capable people, then demanded that they justify expensive proposals. Ben sees an important distinction between understanding a technology’s benefit and selecting it himself. Sam could insist on the economics while someone else chose how to deliver them.

Ron Mayer and the team around him helped build the computerized operation. Mayer would briefly become CEO before Sam realized he was not ready to retire; in his book Sam accepted responsibility for that failed handoff. Information systems made it possible to take daily orders for each store’s particular assortment and quantities. Large shipments came into one side of a distribution center, were broken down and reassembled into store-specific loads, and went out the other side. Goods moved through a coordinated system rather than merely waiting in a warehouse.

The company added its own trucking operation. Expansion followed the reach of the distribution centers: establish an outer store within roughly a truck’s day of travel, then fill in the territory back toward the hub. Denser routes and greater purchasing volume let the same infrastructure serve more stores. When Walmart met Kmart in a market, the hosts argue, it could price lower and still earn money because its cost to put goods on the shelf was lower.

Fast communication extended the system. After correcting their initial uncertainty about the date, the hosts place Walmart’s $24 million private satellite network in 1987. It carried two-way voice and data and one-way video from Bentonville. Sales information traveled quickly, and Sam could broadcast to stores he could no longer personally visit every week. Technology expanded the reach of a management style originally built around direct contact.

By 1990, Walmart had passed Sears as America’s largest retailer. Its map still had gaps: the episode dates its first California store to 1990, Oregon to 1992, and Washington to 1993. Meanwhile Kmart bought businesses including Sports Authority, OfficeMax, Builder’s Square, Waldenbooks, and Borders, then sold assets as it struggled. It filed for bankruptcy in 2002, merged with Sears in 2004, and the combined company went bankrupt in 2018. The hosts stress Walmart’s role in this collapse rather than crediting it all to Amazon.

Chapter 09 · Late 1980s–2000s

The second business inside the store

Grocery turned the discount store into a weekly destination.

On a trip to Brazil, Sam saw Carrefour hypermarkets combining groceries and general merchandise. He returned with another idea to copy. Walmart’s Hypermart USA experiment was enormous, but the format proved too large. The hosts describe the smaller Supercenter as the successful adaptation, crediting David Glass and the organization beyond Sam with turning it into the next engine of growth. Sam died in 1992 after battling cancer; their point is that the decisive grocery expansion was not simply another achievement to assign to the founder.

Grocery required more than adding shelves. Fresh food spoiled, cold supply chains mattered, and margins were thinner. The logistics advantage became more valuable. The hosts estimate Walmart grocery prices averaged about 15% below comparable conventional stores, a meaningful saving for families. They describe a rise from effectively no grocery share at the start of the 1990s to America’s largest grocer by the decade’s end.

By the July 2022 recording, the hosts put Walmart above 20% of US grocery sales, with Kroger below 10% and Albertsons and Costco around 5% apiece. Grocery represented about 55% of Walmart revenue—over $300 billion in their account. These are the episode’s contemporary figures. They explain why Ben calls it an iPhone-scale reinvention: a category absent for the company’s first three decades had become its majority business.

The same episode also shows the limits of expansion. Sam’s Club, launched in 1983, was substantial at roughly $75 billion of annual sales, but far behind Costco’s roughly $217 billion. The hosts frame Costco’s lower gross margins and stripped-down warehouse experience as another turn of the discounting logic. The question was uncomfortable: why had Walmart, with so many advantages, not become dominant in its own warehouse-club business?

Chapter 10 · 2011–2022

The opportunity it was late to see

A technology pioneer struggled with the next technology wave.

Walmart had understood computers and satellites early, yet the hosts find its slow response to the internet puzzling. This was a company accustomed to pursuing opportunities, not merely reacting to threats. Ben suspects nobody made a compelling enough internal case for the scale of investment needed until the opportunity had grown difficult to catch.

In 2011 it bought Kosmix for about $300 million; that team became Walmart Labs. In 2016 it paid $3.3 billion for Jet.com, founded by Marc Lore after his earlier company sold to Amazon. Jet’s own storefront ultimately closed. The explanation offered by Lore and Walmart, which Ben identifies as their account, was that the purchase assembled the ecommerce talent and product organization Walmart needed. Doug McMillon credited Lore with jumpstarting the business; Lore left in 2021.

The hosts cite roughly 37% ecommerce growth in 2019, before the pandemic distorted comparisons, against much slower growth in the company overall. At recording, ecommerce was about 13% of sales, or roughly $75 billion, still far behind Amazon. Walmart’s answer was to blend stores and online shopping: delivery from nearby stores, pickup, and a consistent customer relationship across channels. Walmart+ added a membership and features such as scanning purchases on a phone in the store. Whether these assets would beat Amazon’s approach remained an open question.

Ben’s own online orders felt broadly comparable to Amazon, though he thought selection was smaller. He cites a former Amazon director’s account suggesting Walmart’s search used less metadata, while David notes that Amazon’s enormous marketplace made sophisticated search especially necessary. These are observations and attributed assessments, not a technical audit. Walmart also had third-party sellers and physical store space operated by vendors, including integrated merchandise displays.

International operations supplied another mixed result. The hosts describe success in Mexico and Canada, withdrawal from Germany, and an unexciting outcome from owning and then selling Asda in Britain. They are explicitly less studied and uncertain about Flipkart: David recalls a roughly 77% stake bought in 2018 for over $16 billion; Ben says 75%. Their discussion questions whether that investment would open India as hoped, without resolving the stake discrepancy or establishing a definitive verdict on the business.

Chapter 11 · Closing analysis

What still protects the business?

The hosts separate the takeoff from the mature company.

At recording, Walmart operated about 10,500 stores in 24 countries and was approaching $600 billion in annual revenue. The hosts cite roughly $25 billion of operating income, around a 4% operating margin, and gross margins near 24%. Those figures describe a huge business earning a small share of each sales dollar. Revenue growth had settled around the low single digits after decades of much faster expansion. Scale helped explain that slowdown, but the hosts did not treat it as a complete excuse for missed opportunities in ecommerce, international markets, or clubs.

Applying Hamilton Helmer’s Seven Powers, Ben starts with counter positioning in the takeoff period. The established chains had not built their distribution systems or organizations around the rural customers Walmart served. As Walmart grew, both hosts identify scale economies as its clearest enduring power: purchasing volumes, dense distribution, owned logistics, and high utilization reduced costs. Amazon’s investment in its own planes, vans, and facilities looked to them like a related logic.

Branding, in Helmer’s specific sense of willingness to pay a premium, did not fit Walmart in Ben’s view: the appeal was price. Walmart+ might create some switching costs, but they make no strong claim for them. David raises process power in the accumulated operating routines and the culture of passing savings to customers. Ben questions whether the second part still holds as growth slows and management seeks earnings. They leave process power debatable rather than awarding it as a settled fact.

The bull case rests on nearby Supercenters, grocery execution, and the possibility of a seamless online and physical experience. Same-day grocery delivery should fit those assets well. Ben also sees resilience in a downturn because shoppers become more price sensitive. The bear case is crowded with strong competitors: Costco on price, Amazon on convenience and ecommerce, established grocers, and dollar stores serving different occasions and locations.

Ecommerce profitability remains a qualification inside that bear case. Ben initially calls the segment unprofitable, then says Walmart does not break out enough information for him to know exactly what profit measure is at issue or how much reinvestment explains it. David wonders whether promotional prices also play a role. The companion preserves that uncertainty. Closing Sam’s Clubs and converting some buildings to ecommerce facilities also raised labor questions, as workers could lose jobs and have to apply for different ones.

Chapter 12 · Closing analysis & verdict

Who receives the savings—and the costs?

The shareholder score is only one part of the episode’s ending.

The hosts return to the habits that built the company: studying what competitors do well, recognizing the discounting wave, refusing to buy a supplier’s inefficiency, and constructing infrastructure when good infrastructure for the task does not exist. Their qualification matters. Price pressure is central to this kind of retail; it does not follow that every software or media business should organize itself around the lowest possible price. Walmart’s logistics made its offering better, but building everything internally is not their general prescription.

They then separate business success from social impact. Lower everyday prices can make a large difference to middle- and lower-income households. Stores also supply local jobs and, for some workers, the opportunity to own appreciating stock. Yet independent merchants can disappear, distinctive merchandise and personal relationships can be lost, and the equity created by local transactions can accrue to outside shareholders and the Walton family. Ben and David do not pretend those effects can be settled with a single anecdote.

David regards supplier pressure as a particularly troubling part of the story. Relentless demands for lower prices can push manufacturing offshore and encourage cheaper materials and lower product quality. He connects Walmart to the movement of American production abroad, while acknowledging that whether offshoring is good or bad can itself be debated. The hosts discuss the tension between an individual customer’s saving today and the industrial and labor consequences of producing that saving.

Environmental costs belonged in the same account. They credit management from the mid-2000s onward with recognizing that sustainability could also improve efficiency: solar installations, changes to truck design, and fuel savings could reduce both costs and environmental impact. Their estimates and praise are recording-era assessments. The discussion also reaches gun sales. David points to restrictions Walmart adopted over time, including ending assault-rifle sales in 2015, and describes its approach as a middle ground that different listeners could consider insufficient or excessive. The point is Walmart’s entanglement with national life, not a declaration that the controversy is resolved.

The final grade remains disputed, and they explicitly grade the business for shareholders rather than all stakeholders. Both are effusive about the early decades. David initially wants an A+ for the Supercenter era too; Ben counters that revenue growth fell sharply through the 1990s, even with a later rebound. He begins harshly on the later company, then moves toward a B because it remained the world’s largest retailer through tremendous upheaval. David still finds the 2000s and 2010s uninspiring. They never agree on one letter for the whole history.

They do agree that Sam’s autobiography, Made in America, coauthored with John Huey while Sam was dying of cancer, is an exceptional business story. The episode closes with their reading and listening recommendations and, fittingly, a delivery notification for Ben’s Walmart order. The company that learned how to bring a better store to an overlooked town was still trying to learn how to bring the store to the customer.
