Acquired · Fall 2021

TSMC

The factory behind the designers

A displaced child becomes an American semiconductor executive, loses the career he expected, and starts the Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company at fifty-six. Its customers will change what a chip company can be.

Acquired · TSMCReleased · September 7, 2021Created · October 14, 2026About 23 minutes to read

The full 2021 story and concluding analysis, with the supplied 2025 remaster introduction kept distinct.

01

1931 to the 1950s

A life interrupted and a discipline discovered

Long before Taiwan, Morris Chang was looking for a stable place to build a life.

Listening cue · 0:05:02

Morris Chang was born in Ningbo, south of Shanghai, in July 1931. His father worked first as a county official and later as a bank manager. The family was reasonably comfortable, but the world around it was becoming dangerous. When the Second Sino-Japanese War broke out, Morris and his mother fled to Hong Kong. The Japanese invasion of Hong Kong in December 1941 forced another move, this time to Shanghai. By 1948, the Chinese Civil War had sent them back to Hong Kong again.

Before turning eighteen, Morris had lived through three wars. The hosts begin here because his later career depended on something that had been scarce in childhood: stability. With help from an uncle in Boston, he was admitted to Harvard in 1949 and moved to the United States. He described the experience with astonishment. Ben emphasizes the ability to imagine that the country and its institutions would still be there ten years later. For someone trying to learn and invent, that was a profound change.

Harvard did not resolve his practical problem. His parents were not coming with him; he needed a profession that could support him. In the episode, Chang recalls the narrow range of occupations open to Chinese people in America at the time. Engineering looked more attainable than law or politics. He transferred to MIT to study mechanical engineering, completed his undergraduate and master’s work, and hoped to continue into a doctorate. Then he failed the qualifying examination twice.

The hosts delight in Chang’s dry account of the second failure. It forced him to look for a job rather than remain a student. They recount a story in which Ford offered him $479 a month and Sylvania’s semiconductor division offered $480. Supposedly, Ford would not match the extra dollar. Ben and David treat this as an appealing legend, not a securely established explanation of the decision. The consequential fact in their narrative is that the mechanical engineer entered semiconductors.

Sylvania posted him to its plant in Ipswich, Massachusetts. Chang knew little electrical engineering, so he studied William Shockley’s textbook after work in his hotel room. When he needed help, he brought his questions downstairs to a more experienced engineer who frequented the bar. That colleague became his informal teacher. The picture is remarkably modest: a young employee, a difficult book, and repeated evening conversations.

For three years, he learned both the technology and the limits of his employer. A senior manager’s admission that Sylvania could neither make what it could sell nor sell what it could make stayed with him. Chang decided to leave. His destination was not the emerging Silicon Valley, but Dallas. Texas Instruments was a major semiconductor force, and the hosts want its importance restored to a history usually told through California.

02

1958 to the early 1970s

Learning to make the factory work

Yield and pricing become two sides of the same manufacturing problem.

Listening cue · 0:25:30

Chang joined Texas Instruments in 1958, the year Jack Kilby developed the integrated circuit there. The episode pauses to distinguish three ideas that otherwise blur together. A transistor is a controllable switch. A semiconductor is a material whose electrical behavior can be manipulated to make such a switch. An integrated circuit puts many components together on one piece, instead of wiring separate transistors to one another. Manufacturing those circuits would become its own formidable technology.

Chang’s first important assignment involved IBM’s transistor-based 7090 mainframe. IBM needed more manufacturing capacity and supplied TI with the designs. This was an early glimpse of the work a foundry would eventually do: make another company’s devices. The trouble was yield, the fraction of output that actually worked. The hosts describe IBM’s line producing about ten usable transistors out of every hundred, while TI’s line produced almost none.

Chang approached the failure as a set of mechanical and chemical processes that could be tested and improved. After roughly four months, he brought TI’s yield to about twenty percent, twice IBM’s rate in the episode’s account. IBM initially suspected luck. When its engineers visited, he could explain the experiments and why they worked. That explanation mattered: this was a repeatable method, not one fortunate batch.

The achievement made Chang visible to TI’s president and earned him a management role with more than twenty engineers. TI then paid for his doctorate at Stanford while continuing his salary. He completed it in two and a half years and returned to Texas by 1964, as the industry’s use of silicon was advancing. The hosts stress how much knowledge was already embedded in manufacturing. Knowing a circuit’s design did not tell you how to produce it reliably.

As a general manager in 1967, Chang confronted a second problem. A new fabrication plant, or fab, required a large investment before producing anything. The industry’s usual response was to charge high prices for its new products. That helped recover the investment on each sale, but it discouraged volume precisely when the manufacturing process most needed practice. Low initial yields became harder to improve if the line stayed underused.

With Boston Consulting Group, Chang developed learning curve pricing. TI would begin with lower prices and keep reducing them, even when competitors or customers did not force a cut. Volume could rise sooner; repeated production could improve yield; better yield and fuller use of the plant could lower costs. The hosts describe the strategy as controversial because it sacrificed an immediately available price for a larger, more productive operation.

Chang credited this pricing policy, together with other strategies, with making TI’s integrated circuits business the largest and most profitable in the world. By 1972 he was a vice president running the semiconductor business and a leading candidate to become CEO. He had learned that manufacturing advantage could be built through both experiments on the line and decisions about which business the line should serve. The next part of his career would show how badly that expertise could fit elsewhere.

03

The 1970s to the mid 1980s

The expected CEO takes an unexpected exit

Two prestigious jobs leave Chang without the American career he had imagined.

Listening cue · 0:36:40

Chang never became CEO of Texas Instruments. David offers several possible explanations and does not settle them into one neat verdict. He raises racial discrimination as a possibility while explicitly saying he has no evidence for it. He also discusses a much more concrete mismatch: TI’s belief that a good manager could manage any business.

In 1978, TI moved Chang from semiconductors to consumer products. Calculators and other consumer devices required different customers, different marketing, and a feel for consumer appeal. Chang’s experience had centered on technology and cost. He later said the assumption that he could simply transfer those skills was wrong. The consumer business remained troubled, and he was moved into a diminished staff role concerned with quality and people effectiveness.

Meanwhile, TI’s semiconductor position weakened as the industry moved toward metal oxide processes. The hosts describe engineers leaving for competitors and new ventures, including L. J. Sevin’s Mostek. They connect the loss of talent to a culture that expected people to remain company employees. TI then missed the processor opportunity for IBM’s personal computer, which helped move the next major computing market toward Intel and x86. David is careful not to assign that entire failure to Chang, who had already moved out of the semiconductor division.

Chang resigned and considered two paths: a senior operating job at General Instrument or a move into venture capital. He chose General Instrument, where becoming COO seemed a route to the CEO position he still wanted. But its acquisition-and-sale approach to semiconductor businesses was a poor match for someone who wanted to develop technology. He stayed only about a year.

After these two setbacks, Chang concluded that running a major American company was probably no longer in the cards. He still had industry standing and enough money to live modestly without another salary. The founding story that follows does not begin with a young entrepreneur chasing an obvious market. It begins with an experienced executive whose intended career had come apart.

04

1985 and the industrial research institute

A foreigner arrives for a quiet retirement

Taiwan wants to move beyond low margin manufacturing; Chang expects a softer landing.

Listening cue · 0:45:43

Chang had visited Taiwan while discussing a TI manufacturing plant, but he was not returning to his homeland. The hosts insist on that distinction. He had been born in mainland China and built his adult life in America. Taiwan was a new place, with new institutions and a very different working culture.

K. T. Li, a government minister he had met during those earlier visits, recruited him to lead the Industrial Technology Research Institute, or ITRI. Taiwan had established the institute to transfer technology and develop businesses of its own. The hosts describe the aspiration as a Taiwanese Bell Labs. The economic problem was plain: ordinary local manufacturers could have gross margins of only four or five percent, before paying overhead. The government wanted technology that would allow more value to remain in Taiwan.

This was not an entirely blank slate. A technology transfer from RCA had already provided semiconductor capability, although the hosts describe it as well behind the leading edge by the time it reached Taiwan. ITRI had also helped create United Microelectronics Corporation, UMC. UMC initially combined manufacturing with chip design; its design business later became MediaTek. That lineage belongs to UMC, rather than TSMC.

Against the advice of people around him, Chang accepted the ITRI job in 1985. He saw it partly as retirement and a change of scene. The employees saw something else: a foreign executive from a demanding American industry suddenly placed in charge of government researchers. He brought an intensity that did not fit their expectations of stable public employment.

Then Li changed the assignment. He wanted Chang himself to start a semiconductor company and turn it into a global leader. The episode presents the compressed planning deadline as a story: first a week, then supposedly only three days. Chang compared the request to an offer he could not refuse. Li had specified the ambition, but the business model was left for Chang to invent.

05

1987

A factory for customers who barely exist

The pure play foundry fits Taiwan’s capabilities, but its market remains uncertain.

Listening cue · 0:54:54

Chang began with what Taiwan could plausibly do. In his assessment, it lacked strong circuit design, marketing, research, and intellectual property. Semiconductor manufacturing was a potential strength. A pure play foundry would concentrate on that capability and avoid competing in the areas where it was weakest. It would manufacture customers’ chips without designing and marketing rival products of its own.

The established semiconductor industry did not see this as an obvious future. Integrated device manufacturers, or IDMs, owned the design and the fab. AMD’s Jerry Sanders supplied the era’s memorable expression: real men had fabs. Owning manufacturing was part of what made a chip company credible. Outsourcing it could sound like surrendering the core technology.

Chang nevertheless remembered designers at TI and General Instrument who wanted to leave and form companies. The obstacle was the capital required to build a wafer fab. If manufacturing became a service, those designers could concentrate on the chip itself. But this was hope informed by experience, rather than an existing large customer base. The prospective fabless companies had mostly not been created yet.

The Taiwanese government offered half the capital, provided Chang could raise the other half from industry. He approached major manufacturers, including Intel and TI, hoping for an investor that would also become a customer. They declined. Philips, the Dutch electronics company, was interested. In the hosts’ account, it supplied twenty-eight percent of the $220 million total. The government supplied fifty percent, and other Taiwanese investors supplied the remaining twenty-two percent after government pressure to participate.

The financing was even less like a Silicon Valley startup than it first appeared. The hosts say Chang received no founder equity: the investors owned the company, and he continued as a salaried employee. As TSMC began to succeed, he used his own money to buy shares. TSMC listed in Taiwan in 1994 and in New York in 1997. David does not establish exactly when or through which transactions Chang accumulated all of his ownership; the striking point is that it was purchased rather than granted at founding.

Philips connects this story to another Dutch company. ASML had begun in 1984 as a joint venture involving Philips and ASM. It made equipment that would become essential to advanced chip fabrication. Ben introduces that relationship before describing the machinery because TSMC’s success would depend on specialized partners as well as its own factories. The foundry was a focused company inside an increasingly interdependent industry.

06

The late 1980s to the early 2000s

The customers arrive and the factory compounds

Overflow orders keep the foundry alive until fabless companies become its enduring market.

Listening cue · 1:13:41

The first business was not the business Chang wanted. Established manufacturers supplied work when their own lines were full, then withdrew it as soon as capacity became available. They also passed along older products they no longer wanted to make, sometimes because those products were losing money. TSMC inherited unstable demand and unattractive economics. The existence of a factory did not, by itself, create a healthy market.

Chang later acknowledged that he had hoped for the rise of the fabless industry rather than confidently foreseen it. He began telling designers that they could leave their employers and start companies without owning a fab. His reputation mattered. The man who had nearly run TI could get meetings, and engineers understood that he knew what it took to produce their designs.

Over time, the customers included Qualcomm, Broadcom, Marvell, and NVIDIA. Ben singles out NVIDIA, founded in 1993, which he says raised only $20 million and never built a fab. David initially describes its manufacturing relationship too absolutely, then allows that NVIDIA could also use other foundries. Their central point survives that qualification: access to outside fabrication made an ambitious chip design company possible with far less capital.

The hosts compare TSMC to AWS and to a platform backing an entire generation of entrepreneurs. Chang had considered becoming a venture capitalist. Instead of buying stakes in promising companies, the foundry could earn revenue by supplying the infrastructure many of them needed. A startup that began without manufacturing had little reason to bring that enormous responsibility in-house later. It was a much more durable customer than an IDM renting temporary overflow capacity.

The growing customer base created the episode’s central flywheel. More orders supported more revenue and profit. TSMC could invest in better equipment and manufacturing processes. Better chips let its customers enter more markets and improve their products, bringing more orders back to the foundry. This did not turn instantly. TSMC began behind the leaders and needed years of work to approach them.

The hosts describe a narrowing field at the leading edge: twenty-two manufacturers in the early 2000s, fourteen later that decade, six in the mid-2010s, and two at the five-nanometer generation discussed in 2021. Those last two were TSMC and Samsung. Their expectation that TSMC might stand alone at the next generation is a forecast from the recording, not a claim about every later process node.

Ben connects this narrowing to the speed and complexity of the industry. Chip architecture, design software, manufacturing equipment, chip design, and fabrication had each become demanding specialties. The best product could emerge from several companies working together, rather than one organization doing everything. That collaboration still required deep technical integration. Specialization did not make the handoffs simple.

07

The hosts’ technical interlude

The machinery is only the beginning

ASML can sell a machine; productive manufacturing still depends on accumulated know how.

Listening cue · 1:23:55

The episode’s lasers-and-molten-tin passage makes an abstract barrier to entry tangible. Advanced manufacturing was not simply a matter of ordering another production line. Ben describes an ASML machine costing around $200 million, with later versions expected to cost more. Transporting one required four 747s in his account, followed by specialists who helped assemble and operate it at the customer’s site.

The hosts explain extreme ultraviolet lithography, or EUV, through its light source. Molten tin droplets are struck by a precisely timed laser, creating a plasma that emits extreme ultraviolet light. They describe that happening fifty thousand times a second. Producing the light is only one part of the difficulty. Specialized mirrors, lasers, and the rest of the optical system must work together to use it in patterning the wafer.

ASML itself depends on specialized suppliers. The hosts point to a German laser manufacturer and to the difficulty of developing suitable mirrors. They describe only about fifty of these machines being made annually and equipment ordered well ahead. This is an account of the constraints discussed in the episode, rather than a current production estimate or a complete technical specification.

The node names require a qualification that Ben supplies later in the conversation. Five nanometers is a generation label, not a literal description of every dimension or spacing on the chip. The diagram here therefore shows the relationship among the light source, optics, and patterned wafer without pretending to be a dimensionally accurate machine drawing. The hosts’ argument does not depend on treating a marketing label as a ruler.

Nor does access to the equipment remove the operational challenge. Ben imagines receiving the machines and having no idea how to produce useful chips with them. The people, experiments, relationships, and accumulated process knowledge matter as much as the purchase. David’s hypothetical government with a vast budget cannot buy its way to parity next year. His claim is about time and learning, not an assertion that capital is irrelevant.

The hosts also describe restrictions preventing China’s SMIC from obtaining the most advanced ASML equipment, and discuss reported American pressure on the Netherlands. That adds an access constraint to the learning constraint. Both reinforce their broader point: the foundry’s capability is a system built through years of work, rather than a catalog of machines that a rival can immediately duplicate.

08

2005 to 2018

Chang returns for the smartphone era

A planned retirement gives way to a manufacturing commitment big enough to change the company.

Listening cue · 1:32:20

By 2005, the foundry model had worked far better than Chang originally hoped. At seventy-four, he handed the CEO role to longtime colleague Rick Tsai. The customers were growing, but their businesses did not yet have the scale associated with the later smartphone and AI eras. NVIDIA’s fortunes, for example, still depended heavily on games and console contracts.

The financial crisis disrupted that comfortable transition. In the summer of 2009, TSMC announced that Chang would return as CEO at seventy-eight. The hosts discuss whether the handover to Tsai had been mishandled, but do not claim to have resolved the succession question. Chang’s stated reason for coming back looked forward: he saw golden opportunities in the next phase of computing.

The first was mobile. Apple’s early iPhones had used Samsung processors, and smartphone demand was beginning to reveal its potential. ARM-based designs placed a premium on low power, while the companies designing chips for that market were generally fabless. The second opportunity was the cloud. Intel initially benefited from server demand, but more specialized workloads created room for GPUs and custom chips supplied through foundries.

Ben qualifies David’s enthusiasm about the cloud: most workloads still ran on CPUs. The opportunity was not that general-purpose computing had disappeared. It was that the range of important chips was expanding. A foundry serving many designers could participate in that expansion without needing to choose one architecture or one winning end product.

Apple’s developing design capability made it especially important. The hosts trace part of that engineering lineage through ARM, DEC’s StrongARM work, Intel, and P.A. Semi, which Apple acquired. At the same time, Samsung was both a chip supplier and a smartphone competitor. TSMC’s commitment to manufacturing for customers rather than selling competing devices gave Apple a different kind of partner.

The manufacturing agreement required a serious gamble. The hosts recount a reported $9 billion initial investment by TSMC and six thousand employees building a dedicated plant in eleven months, followed by years before chip production began. For Apple, a heavy commitment meant depending on a supplier without an easy backup plan. For TSMC, it meant committing enormous capacity to one customer. Ben and David see Chang’s standing as important to getting that bet made.

They are uncertain about precisely which early Apple chip generations moved to TSMC and when manufacturing became exclusive. They emphasize the size and significance of the commitment rather than a settled chip-by-chip chronology. Apple’s growth helped turn the foundry into a much larger platform. Chang relinquished the CEO role again in 2013, remained chairman, and finally stepped down from that position in June 2018 at eighty-six.

09

The 2020 results and 2021 plans

Profit buys the next generation

The spending requirement that deters rivals becomes a use for TSMC’s earnings.

Listening cue · 1:42:13

At the time of the original recording, the hosts describe TSMC as the world’s ninth most valuable company, with roughly $550 billion in market capitalization. They cite annual revenue growth compounded at 17.4 percent across the twenty-seven years since its 1994 Taiwan listing. The market capitalization had risen from approximately $4 billion at that listing. These are the hosts’ 2021 measures of the company’s scale, not current market figures.

For 2020, David cites about $48 billion in revenue and $20 billion in operating profit. Capital expenditures were about $17 billion. Ben separately cites adjusted net income of $17 billion. Those two $17 billion figures describe different things: one is spending on long-lived assets, while the other is a profit measure. The hosts use the comparison to emphasize the intensity of reinvestment, not to supply a complete cash flow statement.

In January 2021, TSMC forecast $25 billion to $28 billion of capital spending for that year. By April, the plan had risen to $30 billion for 2021 and $100 billion over three years. Ben also cites $28 billion of cash and cash equivalents. David expects the company to generate substantial funding internally but says he does not know the financing details. The three-year plan should not be read as already-completed expenditure.

Then came a reversal of the learning curve pricing pattern. The hosts discuss a decision first to stop cutting prices and then to raise them, with Ben citing an increase of twenty percent. They see this as evidence of pricing power after decades of manufacturing progress. Lower prices had helped build volume; scarce capability now gave the foundry more room to charge for it.

Their market-share discussion separates several denominators. They put TSMC above fifty percent of the overall foundry market and around ninety percent or more of leading-edge five-nanometer production. David also claims more than ninety-five percent of foundry profits. Those are different markets and measures. The point of the comparison is that the most technically demanding work carries a disproportionate share of the economic value.

The hosts consider the argument that chips might become good enough and customers could stop needing the leading edge. David is skeptical; Ben says software tends to use the capability available on the newest hardware. They also recognize that progress cannot indefinitely mean shrinking the same physical structures. New architectures and other engineering improvements matter. The episode leaves the future route open while expecting advanced production to remain expensive.

Ben invokes Rock’s Law, the proposition that fab costs double roughly every four years, but does not insist the rule holds exactly. His comparison with Moore’s Law captures two different effects: greater capability and lower cost per function at scale can coexist with rising upfront factory costs. The hosts believe the manufacturer with the greatest profitable volume can keep paying those costs, while competitors struggle to remain on the treadmill.

10

The hosts’ Seven Powers discussion

Scale is powerful because the process is difficult

A large factory budget and decades of learning reinforce each other.

Listening cue · 1:53:27

The hosts identify scale economies and process power as TSMC’s two strongest advantages. Scale spreads an enormous fixed investment across more production and provides earnings for the next generation. Process power is the hard-to-copy operating capability built through repeated manufacturing, experienced people, intellectual property, and relationships with equipment suppliers. Neither explanation is complete on its own. Money buys equipment, but knowledge makes it productive; productive equipment generates money for further investment.

Counter-positioning mattered at the beginning. The IDMs earned money from combining their designs with their manufacturing. Some also demanded rights to market a customer’s product under their own brand when providing capacity. A foundry that did neither could offer a materially different relationship. Ben wonders whether the margin tradeoff played out exactly as the theoretical account suggests. The hosts agree on the model’s strategic difference without establishing a precise historical margin comparison.

Switching costs are also substantial in their analysis. Moving a chip from TSMC to another foundry is not like changing the address on an order. Designs depend on the manufacturing process, and adapting them can take years. For a customer seeking the most advanced available process, the limited set of alternatives adds another constraint. The difficulty is technical integration as well as availability.

They decline to call the customer base a conventional network effect. One customer does not automatically benefit simply because another joins; Apple’s large demand may even consume capacity others want. David does see an ecosystem advantage in TSMC’s integration with design tools and intellectual property providers, but distinguishes that from the standard network economies category.

They similarly do not make branding a central power. The consumer sees Apple or another customer’s name, which suits TSMC’s role. They set aside cornered resources as a main explanation in this part of the discussion. The table below retains those distinctions instead of awarding the company every possible category.

Their proposed technological bear case is a discontinuity large enough to reset the field. If a different way to compute made advanced fabrication dramatically cheaper or obsolete, TSMC’s accumulated advantages might matter less. The examples they discuss, including quantum computing, are hypothetical. Ben thinks smaller shifts, even changes in substrate, might still favor the existing expertise. The argument is that an ordinary competitor is constrained by the current system; a new system could change the competition.

11

The hosts’ geopolitical discussion

An indispensable capability in a vulnerable place

Business defensibility cannot remove the political risk surrounding Taiwan.

Listening cue · 2:02:03

Most of the manufacturing capability discussed in the episode sits in Taiwan. The hosts consider a conflict over the island as a risk distinct from normal competition. They acknowledge the human danger and then ask what interruption of leading-edge semiconductor supply would mean for technology, industry, and defense. The chip shortage affecting vehicles in 2021 already made dependence visible at a much smaller scale.

Their thought experiment is deliberately unresolved. Could TSMC’s process power be moved out in an emergency if the people and equipment could be relocated? David does not know. The question arises because that power is more than a building: it also lives in experience, coordination, supplier relationships, and the surrounding industrial ecosystem. The episode does not demonstrate that an evacuation could preserve it.

They discuss geographic expansion, including a proposed Arizona fab, plans in Japan, and operations in China. Ben mentions roughly $12 billion for the Arizona project. He is uncertain about the plant’s process generation and the details of subsidies. Their argument is that opening plants abroad is not the same as recreating the full leading-edge ecosystem.

Chang’s comments, as described by Ben, favor keeping the most advanced manufacturing close to that Taiwanese ecosystem. The hosts see a tension between what makes commercial sense for TSMC and what other governments want for strategic security. Ben speculates that subsidies help explain overseas expansion; that remains his interpretation of the incentives, not a confirmed explanation for every project.

They close this discussion with the hope that international interdependence continues to make cooperation worthwhile. They do not forecast a war or claim to be geopolitical experts. Their strong view of the company’s moat therefore comes with a substantial condition: the operating system can remain intact and connected to its global customers and suppliers.

12

The hosts’ playbook and final assessment

A successful outcome does not prove an inevitable strategy

The closing discussion returns to the opportunities missed and the uncertainty that remains.

Listening cue · 2:07:27

The episode revisits an opportunity Intel missed. The hosts recount Steve Jobs asking Intel’s Paul Otellini to supply chips for the first iPhone, and Intel turning down pricing it considered too low. Apple went to Samsung, later developed its own designs, and ultimately relied on TSMC for manufacturing. David initially proposes a broad rule against making strategic decisions based on economics. Ben narrows it: the issue is rejecting a strategically valuable arrangement on price when its structure otherwise lets the company participate in the upside.

Their criticism of Intel is not a claim that it had become irrelevant overnight. Ben points to its large PC processor share and even greater server presence in 2021. Many important workloads did not require the newest process. A company could lose its position in the next computing wave while retaining a very substantial business in the existing one.

They also discuss Intel’s strategic reversals, from considering outsourced fabrication under Bob Swan to pursuing a foundry business under Pat Gelsinger. Ben thinks serving other designers would require genuine independence from Intel’s own chip business. AMD’s earlier manufacturing separation illustrates another possible organizational response, although the hosts do not treat GlobalFoundries as having reproduced TSMC’s leading-edge success.

Intel’s early commitment of more than $4 billion to EUV in 2012 supplies a further example. The hosts describe a company that invested in the new technology but was slower to adopt it, preferring improvements to established methods. They compare that reluctance to the innovator’s dilemma. Recognizing a potential future and rearranging a profitable existing operation around it are different tasks.

Ben then applies the same skepticism to the winner. TSMC’s success does not prove that building a platform before its customers exist was a high-probability strategy. Chang had reasons to hope for fabless companies, but the episode has already shown how weak the first market was. Ben’s illustrative probabilities are a warning about reading inevitability backward from an outcome, not a measured success rate for foundries.

David ends the playbook by returning to outsourcing. When many companies perform an expensive activity that does not distinguish their own products, a specialist platform may have an opportunity to serve them. This is the hosts’ extension of the AWS comparison: chip designers could focus on design because someone else made fabrication a business in its own right.

For grading, they ask where TSMC belongs among the great technology companies rather than simply giving Taiwan’s founding investment an A+. Ben calls it the most successful B2B hardware company, while both settle on the top ten technology companies rather than the top five. They debate how much value to assign to TSMC versus ASML and its customers. Importance across a supply chain is harder to attribute than the success of a branded consumer product.

David sees a formidable moat; Ben reminds him that unforeseen changes will eventually occur. They acknowledge that their immersion in the story may make them overenthusiastic. Their final admiration therefore contains the same uncertainty as the founding: a system that has become extraordinarily hard to compete with is still subject to technology, politics, and events its operators cannot control.

The supplied transcript adds a distinct 2025 remaster introduction. In it, the hosts describe TSMC’s market capitalization moving from roughly $550 billion to more than a trillion dollars and its importance to the AI era. That introduction looks back on the 2021 episode; it does not revise every forecast within it. The original conversation ends with recommendations for Ted Lasso’s first season, Greek, and Who Is Michael Ovitz?, followed by an invitation to share the show.
