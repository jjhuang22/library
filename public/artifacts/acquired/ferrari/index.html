Ferrari

The workshop and the world.
A racing dream becomes a scarce object of desire, while the crowd remains welcome.

Chapter 01 · 1898–1929

A driver discovers his other talent

A racing ambition survives loss, rejection and a failed first business.

Listen from 00:06:11 · episode chapter marker

Enzo Ferrari grew up in Modena, the younger son of a successful metalworker. Ben and David introduce a boy who was far less interested in school than in the possibilities of automobile racing. His older brother was expected to continue the family business. Enzo imagined another future: becoming a driver. The First World War destroyed the security around that ambition. His father and brother died of pneumonia; Enzo contracted the disease during his own military service and survived. Back home, he had a mother to support and no obvious livelihood.

His first plan was to sell the family house and buy a racing car. His mother steered him toward employment instead. Fiat rejected his application, but in 1919 he found work at CMN and pledged future salary to acquire a car he could race. The hosts describe a sporting world divided between wealthy private entrants and resourceful young men finding a foothold through the industry. Enzo belonged to the second group. His results attracted Alfa Romeo, whose racing operation gave him cars, mechanics and a place to compete.

Success behind the wheel did not make his first business succeed. He started a coachbuilding company in 1920, expecting his reputation as a driver to attract customers. It failed. Nor did he become one of the great racing champions. In the hosts' account, deaths among drivers close to him made it difficult to commit himself to the absolute limit. He turned toward a dealership and organizing racing instead.

When Alfa reduced its racing activity, Enzo proposed taking on races the company would no longer enter. He would supply the drivers, mechanics and expense while retaining an association with Alfa. That became Scuderia Ferrari. The hosts emphasize that the team came before Ferrari-built cars: Enzo assembled an organization, relationships and a name around racing machinery supplied by another company.

Chapter 02 · 1920s–1943

A stable, a horse, a national identity

The hosts challenge the story of a racer indifferent to business.

Listen from 00:25:08 · episode chapter marker

The prancing horse belonged first to Francesco Baracca, the Italian wartime aviator whose parents became supporters of Enzo. The episode recounts the countess encouraging Enzo to put her son's emblem on his car. He combined the horse with a yellow shield associated with Modena and the Italian colors above it. Ben and David read this as a deliberate connection between a racing stable, a national hero and an ambition to represent Italy.

Their reading of Enzo matters to everything that follows. David describes asking Luca di Montezemolo whether the familiar story was true: Enzo cared only about racing and built road cars merely to pay for it. Montezemolo rejected that characterization. The hosts present Enzo as a businessman and marketer with a strong appreciation for engineering, rather than an engineer himself. They compare his role to Steve Jobs: shaping a vision, recognizing the technical work it required and persuading people to deliver it.

Red also became part of that vision. It was Italy's assigned racing color, but the hosts argue that Ferrari gave it an emotional association of its own. The color came to suggest passion, danger and desire rather than merely a country's entry in a race. It helped make Ferrari recognizable well beyond the people who could purchase one.

Alfa eventually made Scuderia its racing operation, then brought it back inside the company. German teams supported by their state dominated the later 1930s, and war overtook European racing. Enzo's separation from Alfa in 1939 barred him from using his name for a racing team or carmaker for four years. His new Auto Avio Costruzioni made machine tools during the war. Even then, the hosts say, the tools carried the horse and Scuderia identity: Enzo's intention to return to racing remained visible.

Chapter 03 · 1943–1950

An engine finds its customer

Racing, manufacturing and service become one proposition.

Listen from 00:35:41 · episode chapter marker

Enzo moved his operation to Maranello in 1943, hoping the countryside would offer protection from bombing. The factory was still struck. After the war he had a damaged plant and a business that had spent years making tools. An old Alfa acquaintance, Luigi Chinetti, returned from America with a view of what wealthy customers there might buy. He had maintained their European cars during the war and understood their attraction to an exceptional Italian machine.

The hosts reject another tidy origin story: Chinetti did not have to persuade Enzo to abandon machine tools for cars. Enzo already intended to return. Chinetti's essential contribution was the American market. Ben and David connect it to Enzo's cultivated public image: a remote creator in a small Italian town, apparently indifferent to commerce, whose work the buyer was fortunate to obtain. They even describe the dark sunglasses as part of that performance.

In the episode's chronology, Ferrari built racing cars in 1947 but did not sell a car to a customer until 1948. The 166 MM Barchetta joined beautiful bodywork to a machine capable of competition. Early customers included wealthy Americans brought through Chinetti and Gianni Agnelli, whose family would later become Ferrari's industrial backer. In 1949, Chinetti entered a privately owned Ferrari at Le Mans despite Enzo's reservations about readiness. He and Lord Selsdon won. A Ferrari's victory created demand even when Ferrari's own team had not entered it.

Ben and David pause to explain the business already taking shape. Ferrari was a racing team, a constructor selling machines to clients, and the support organization that prepared and maintained those machines. The customer could buy a connection to the same people and work that produced racing results. Reliability and everyday convenience were secondary to that connection. The hosts describe buyers knowingly accepting the compromises of owning a racing machine rather than treating it as ordinary transport.

Chapter 04 · 1950s–1963

Beauty, tragedy and an uncertain succession

The 250's design partnership sits beside losses that shape Enzo's future.

Listen from 00:51:31 · episode chapter marker

Formula 1 gave Ferrari another international stage. The episode follows Enzo's relationship with Alberto Ascari, son of one of his old racing mentors, and the devastation of Ascari's death while practicing in a Ferrari. Enzo resolved not to become emotionally close to another driver. Meanwhile, the road-car business developed a more substantial production model in the 250 series.

Its beauty was not incidental bodywork placed around an engine. The hosts describe Enzo's long partnership with Pininfarina as a collaboration between mechanical purpose and form. The coachbuilder participated from the beginning of the project. They compare the relationship to Jobs and Jony Ive, David contrasts Ferrari's forceful, passionate expression of Italian luxury with the quieter royal associations he sees in French luxury. Ben questions how sharply that distinction holds, while agreeing that Italy evokes a different emotional experience for him.

Dino, Enzo's son and intended successor, died at twenty-four from muscular dystrophy in 1956. The hosts emphasize the limits of Enzo's efforts to keep him away from racing danger: the loss came through an illness he could not control. Enzo stopped traveling to races. His grief was also bound up with the future of the company he had expected Dino to inherit.

There was another son, Piero, from Enzo's relationship with Lina Lardi. The episode describes the social and legal constraints that kept him outside the acknowledged succession at that time, even as Enzo loved him and he later worked in the company.

The 1957 Mille Miglia brought a Ferrari crash into a crowd, deaths and a manslaughter case against Enzo. The hosts discuss the Vatican's condemnation, Enzo's eventual acquittal and racing's disturbing attraction as a confrontation with mortality. They argue that the public drama made the cars more famous and desired, even while it weighed heavily on Enzo. By the early 1960s, the company was selling hundreds of cars annually, but the question of what happened after its founder remained unresolved.

Chapter 05 · 1963–1971

The contract is about control

Ford's challenge precedes the Fiat agreement and the plan for Piero.

Listen from 01:14:03 · episode chapter marker

Henry Ford II wanted racing to give his company a new image. Buying Ferrari appeared to offer an immediate route. The episode describes negotiations that reduced an initial $18 million asking price to $10 million and a proposal for two entities: one predominantly Ford-owned for road cars, the other predominantly Ferrari-owned for racing. The apparent separation did not settle who controlled the racing budget. Enzo demanded final authority to race; Ford expected the final decision to belong to the buyer. The transaction collapsed.

Ford responded by building a program with Carroll Shelby, Ken Miles and an American racing team. In 1966 its cars took the first three places at Le Mans. Ben and David regard that famous sporting contest as only part of the business story. In their interpretation, the negotiations and publicity also reinforced Ferrari's Italian identity and signaled that Enzo was prepared to sell to an acceptable partner. That assessment of his intentions is the hosts' argument, rather than a newly established fact about a private negotiation.

The urgency changed when Enzo developed kidney disease in 1968 and believed he was nearing death. Fiat and Gianni Agnelli became the chosen partner. In 1969, Fiat acquired 50 percent. The episode describes an agreement for Fiat to acquire another 40 percent after Enzo's death, leaving 10 percent to Piero. Fiat would control road cars; Enzo would keep the racing authority he had refused to surrender to Ford.

The hosts calculate a value of roughly $6.8 million for the company in the initial Fiat deal. They explicitly interpret the low price as reflecting Enzo's circumstances and narrow choice of acceptable buyers. Factory investment, expensive racing, labor problems and Lamborghini's competition compounded the pressure. The arrangement proved timely: Enzo was hospitalized for months after organ failure in 1970. He recovered and returned in 1971. A plan made in expectation of imminent death had to govern a company whose founder would live for almost another two decades.

Chapter 06 · 1971–1991

A new generation learns to organize winning

Luca changes a racing culture, then returns to a damaged road-car business.

Listen from 01:29:10 · episode chapter marker

Luca di Montezemolo enters the story through a radio program. A young rally driver with a connection to the Agnelli family, he passionately defended motorsport against a caller's criticism. Enzo happened to be listening. The episode recounts him tracking down Luca and hiring him as an assistant who could discover what was happening around the organization while its founder recovered from illness.

Luca first investigated road-car sales, then the Formula 1 team. His diagnosis was that Ferrari remained preoccupied with engines while the sport had moved toward chassis and aerodynamics. Enzo gave the young assistant authority to manage the team and change that approach. Luca brought in expertise and Niki Lauda. In 1975, Ferrari won the drivers' and constructors' championships.

The hosts return repeatedly to Montezemolo's explanation of racing's commercial role. A win does not translate mechanically into a particular number of car sales. But an identity built around competition cannot survive indefinite failure. They describe Enzo as needing someone who could change a culture he was himself too closely identified with to change easily.

Lauda's fiery 1976 crash and attempted comeback created tension. David interprets Enzo as losing confidence in a driver who had confronted death so directly, while Luca continued to support him. Luca left day-to-day Ferrari work for the wider Fiat group, though he remained on the board. The episode presents respect between them alongside the difficulty of sharing authority.

The F40 became the last dramatic expression of Enzo's approach. The hosts describe its stripped cabin, minimal door handles and raw focus on performance, rather than comfortable luxury. Introduced for Ferrari's fortieth anniversary in 1987, it put a halo car in the hands of selected clients while retaining the compromises of a racing machine. They later return to it as the beginning of a profit model whose importance may not have been understood at the time.

Enzo lived to introduce the F40, then died in 1988. Fiat acquired the additional stake and Piero received his 10 percent. Ferrari's difficulties continued. The hosts argue that producing more road cars and introducing shared Fiat parts had weakened rarity and the connection to racing. By 1991, unsold cars and a factory shutdown made the crisis undeniable. Agnelli called Luca back, now with wider management experience, to take charge of Ferrari.

Chapter 07 · 1991–2000s

Prestige meets an uncomfortable comparison

The team, the technology and the myth become Luca's three priorities.

Listen from 01:52:40 · episode chapter marker

Before returning, Montezemolo bought a 348 to experience Ferrari's current product himself. He disliked it intensely. He then had test drivers compare it with a Honda NSX. The NSX won the comparison decisively in the hosts' telling. Ferrari could not rely on its reputation while another company made a better sporting machine.

Luca's response had three parts: the team, the technology and the myth. He rebuilt Formula 1 around Jean Todt, Ross Brawn and Michael Schumacher. Their five consecutive drivers' championships from 2000 through 2004 made Ferrari dominant. On the road-car side, the 348 gave way to the 355, which the episode describes as both recognizably Ferrari and much more usable. It became the workhorse of the recovery. Luca also cut output from about 4,500 cars in 1991 to about 2,300 in 1993; the earlier peak would not be exceeded until 2006.

The hosts distinguish Luca's understanding of luxury from Enzo's original racing proposition. Waitlists, delivery ceremonies and fitted leather luggage became deliberate parts of the customer experience. Licensing generated urgently needed income, but they criticize indiscriminate extensions, using the Ferrari-branded Acer netbook as an extreme example. Their argument includes an important qualification: clothing and merchandise can serve sporting fans without making car owners resent their presence. That does not make every licensed product appropriate.

The episode also describes Ferrari managing Maserati as a way to answer demand for a more practical family car under a separate name. The hosts compare the relationship to Rolex and Tudor, while stressing that the operations were separate rather than a shared Maranello production system. Piero's minority ownership helped preserve a connection to Enzo through the Fiat period.

Manufacturing became another central part of the proposition. Ben describes production beginning only after a customer orders and personalizes a car. The factory combines extensive in-house work, including engine casting, with flexible, largely manual assembly. Ferrari does not build its road range on another car brand's shared platforms. The hosts contrast this with the tooling and supplier arrangements of much larger manufacturers.

They do not treat inefficiency as an accidental flaw to be eliminated. Ferrari can change models and transfer ideas from racing relatively quickly because it has kept manufacturing flexible. The margin supports that flexibility, and the craftsmanship becomes part of what owners value. Geographic expansion also offers a way to sell more without making Ferraris common in an existing market. The episode later uses these capabilities to explain the wider modern model range.

Chapter 08 · 2000s–post-IPO

Independence changes the question investors ask

The episode connects Ferrari's flotation to a crisis in the Agnelli empire.

Listen from 02:27:41 · episode chapter marker

The Fiat crisis brought Montezemolo, John Elkann and Sergio Marchionne together. The hosts describe Marchionne arriving as an experienced turnaround operator from SGS. A successful new Fiat 500 and a negotiated exit from Fiat's arrangement with GM helped the group survive. Chrysler's later acquisition created another financial challenge: the combined company had debt to repay, and Ferrari offered a valuable asset to separate and sell to public investors.

This set Luca and Sergio on a collision course. The hosts contrast a Ferrari leader whose commercial decisions began with the cars with an operator focused on the wider industrial empire and its finances. Luca wanted freedom to change production and product decisions over time. A public-market commitment to predictable growth implied a different timetable. The episode recounts his removal as Ferrari chairman in September 2014 and treats weak recent Formula 1 performance as the stated reason rather than the whole explanation.

Ferrari's 2015 IPO valued it at roughly $9.8 billion. The episode discusses nearly $1 billion in proceeds and an additional transfer of about $3.2 billion of Fiat Chrysler debt to Ferrari. Ben and David see both the painful personal ending and the financial logic: Ferrari had become capable of servicing the debt, while separation allowed investors to recognize a business whose value had been obscured inside the parent.

Marchionne's unexpected death in 2018 brought another period of leadership changes. The hosts discuss subsequent leadership, including Benedetto Vigna's semiconductor background, and changes in the business. They also stress the continuity beneath those changes. Ferrari became more independent from Fiat's car platforms and operations, while Piero and Exor retained substantial influence. They distinguish the family's economic ownership from the voting rights that allow the two principal holders to remain powerful together.

Post-IPO merchandise strategy changed too. The hosts describe narrowing licensing to selected partners, then creating Ferrari's own lifestyle products. They see a possible way to reach affluent people who want participation beyond cars, but express reservations about the fashion strategy. Theme parks offer another form of engagement. These are examples they discuss with mixed reactions, rather than automatic endorsements of every extension.

The model range broadened after the IPO. Alongside the core sports and grand-touring cars and occasional halo supercars, Ferrari added more expensive special versions and the historically inspired Icona series. The hosts argue that the very top of the range matters disproportionately to profits. Their F80 calculation uses assumed selling prices, dealer margins and delivery timing; it is an estimate, not a disclosed result. They interpret Icona as a way to sustain similar economics between occasional supercar launches.

Chapter 09 · The business at recording

More occasions, carefully bounded

Allocation, resale, ownership experiences and an electric Ferrari extend the story.

Listen from 02:48:24 · episode chapter marker

The Purosangue answers demand for a four-door Ferrari. The hosts admire the company's decision to limit it to about 20 percent of production, contrasting that restraint with Porsche and Lamborghini's far greater dependence on utility vehicles. Its naturally aspirated V12 and its name both insist on continuity with Ferrari. The point, in their interpretation, is that encountering one on the street should still feel unusual.

They describe a company sold out roughly two years ahead, with about 80 percent of new cars going to existing owners. New-car allocations now come centrally from Ferrari; dealers provide delivery, operations and service rather than independently owning the customer relationship. Used cars are a major route for new entrants. Dealers retain the economics of those secondary-market transactions, while Ferrari participates through servicing, parts and Classiche certification. The episode explains how keeping a car eligible for certification supports an ecosystem of original specifications, authorized work and meticulous records.

The hosts' organizing image is a pyramid surrounded by a broad collection of experiences. A used-car buyer can progress to a new car, a collector can seek an invitation to a rarer model, and an enthusiast can attend events or racing programs. Some clients can even acquire a former Formula 1 car with the support needed to run it. Growth requires both a wider base and new levels above existing owners. Simply making a few objects without the accompanying programs would not reproduce the system they describe.

Ferrari Luce, its first electric model, remained only partly revealed when they recorded. They discuss the interior collaboration with Jony Ive and Marc Newson's LoveFrom, including its tactile controls, while explicitly not knowing the exterior or the final customer response. Their concern is that speed alone no longer makes a Ferrari exceptional when electric alternatives can accelerate faster. The optimistic case is a different, compelling experience that attracts customers uninterested in conventional Ferraris. They also consider the importance of participating in a future increasingly shaped by electric cars outside America.

Before formal analysis, the hosts discuss 2025 revenue of about $8.2 billion and EBITDA of about $3.2 billion, a 38.8 percent EBITDA margin. They distinguish gross margin from EBITDA and emphasize the unusually large gross profit per car. Formula 1 is now described as both marketing and a profit center. They also identify slower expected growth and possible limits to repeated price increases as concerns, while suggesting slower expansion could help preserve the brand.

Chapter 10 · The hosts’ concluding analysis

The crowd and the collector

Seven Powers leads into the episode's two expressions of Ferrari's essence.

Listen from 03:29:39 · episode chapter marker

Ben and David begin Seven Powers with branding, but they do not reduce Ferrari to a badge. Its cars still have to provide a distinctive experience and advanced engineering. They compare Ferrari with Lamborghini to discuss pricing power, then qualify the comparison with other luxury categories. A watch communicates identity; a Ferrari can also deliver a readily experienced difference when driven. For the hosts, that makes the physical product an unusually important part of the luxury promise.

They see scale economies in a company large enough to support technology, a broad model program and extensive owner experiences, yet small enough to keep flexibility and rarity. It occupies a useful position between tiny hypercar makers and mass manufacturers. The hosts connect this to the operating choices already described: an artisan-like factory can introduce new models without first building an enormous dedicated production system, while Ferrari has resources that very small firms lack.

They also identify network economies in the owner community and the tifosi. Sharing a sport, meeting other enthusiasts and participating in programs make the brand more engaging. Their point is not that everyone must become a buyer. The vast majority of supporters will never own the object at the center of the story, yet they can belong to its sporting world.

That leads to Ben's account of the company's essence: Ferrari joins the limited access of a luxury brand to the broad participation of a sports team. The two do not have to undermine each other. The fans reinforce a shared identity that owners value, while owning a car remains exceptional. He relates the combination to Acquired's own selectivity about subjects and sponsors alongside its desire for an accessible community.

The hosts are less convinced by counter-positioning and discuss independence from shared platforms as a possible present-day distinction. They reject meaningful switching costs: collectors can own other marques as well. They regard Enzo's history and the continuing myth as a resource competitors cannot simply acquire. Although the factory has accumulated knowledge, they do not identify a clear, separate case for process power.

Why have rivals failed to reproduce the whole strategy? Their answer emphasizes continuity. Ferrari remained connected to racing and its own identity through changes in ownership, management and product quality. They discuss different shortcomings at Lamborghini, Aston Martin and McLaren rather than claiming that Ferrari has never had bad periods. The story itself has already shown substantial reversals.

David's closing answer to what an owner buys centers on passion and the possibility of feeling intensely alive. Ben adds the idea of a functional alibi: engineering, racing history and craftsmanship give a buyer convincing reasons to desire the car even when status or collecting also motivates the purchase. These are the hosts' interpretations of the appeal, not universal descriptions of every customer's motives.

The episode then revisits the legend that a confrontation with Enzo inspired Ferruccio Lamborghini to make cars. The hosts contrast it with a mechanic discovering similarities between Ferrari and tractor parts, then acknowledge that the surviving stories may contain elements of both. They also speculate that Ferrari could know every owner and serial number through its sales, service and certification relationships. They explicitly leave that as an unconfirmed idea. Another closing anecdote identifies Pierre Louis-Dreyfus as a Ferrari entrant at the 1949 Le Mans race.

The personal recommendations include Ford v Ferrari, Maison Wheat sweaters, Craighill scissors, Amazon's grocery service and the Travelpro Altitude backpack. The closing thank-yous credit the people who helped the hosts assemble the history and understand the owner community.
