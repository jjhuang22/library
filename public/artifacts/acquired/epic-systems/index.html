Epic Systems (MyChart)One patient, one long story

How Judy Faulkner’s patient-centered database became a hospital operating system—and why its success leaves clinicians, patients and buyers with different views of the same screens.

Released April 21, 2025 · Created October 19, 2026
About 26 minutes to read · Complete episode history and concluding analysis
Based on Acquired: Epic Systems (MyChart), with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01

1943–1960s · 00:06:23

A mathematician discovers a material she can shape

Programming combined the precision and creativity that Judy Greenfield loved.

Judy Greenfield grew up near Cherry Hill, New Jersey, in a household with two different models of initiative. Her father, Lou, ran a pharmacy and soda fountain. Her mother, Del, became a peace organizer, later leading Oregon Physicians for Social Responsibility. The hosts connect that organization to the international movement awarded the 1985 Nobel Peace Prize; they do not mean that Del personally received an individual prize.

Mathematics caught Judy early. A seventh-grade exercise about divisibility made numbers feel discoverable rather than merely procedural. At Dickinson College she pursued math, then spent a summer at the University of Rochester working around particle physics. Asked to learn Fortran, she taught herself quickly and fell in love with programming.

She described the work as a combination of mathematics, language and art. That mixture helps explain the later company: rigorous systems would coexist with whimsical names, theatrical buildings and a founder who cared deeply about how things were made.

Stanford and Wisconsin were opening computer-science departments as she applied to graduate school. Both redirected her mathematics application toward the new field. She chose Madison. She would not finish the doctorate, but the university’s medical environment would give her a problem that could occupy a lifetime.

02

1960s–1970s · 00:14:07

The doctor who needed a programmer

A small scheduling assignment opened a much larger problem.

At Wisconsin, physician Warner Slack wanted to use computers in medicine. Judy took a part-time programming job for five dollars an hour. She was effective enough that the rate doubled, but what mattered more was the expanding range of requests. Departments wanted programs, schedules and ways to retrieve information that lived elsewhere in the hospital.

A patient did not experience cardiology, surgery and a laboratory as separate lives. The institution’s records often behaved as though they did. One department needed to know what another had done, while paper had to move physically before anyone could read it. Even carefully standardized forms could not solve that transport problem.

The hosts step back to the early twentieth century, when medical records were often a physician’s personal account rather than a shared institutional resource. Standardization developed slowly, through hospitals and professional organizations. Doctors had reasons to resist: their judgment was central to the work, and a mandatory form could feel like someone else prescribing how to practice medicine.

Computers offered a different possibility. Instead of simply replacing a sheet of paper with a screen, a system could organize a patient’s history so that several departments could work from it. Judy’s opportunity grew out of those concrete requests, not a fully formed plan to build a giant company.

03

1942–1965 policy background · 00:20:39

Why the record also had to produce a bill

The person receiving care was often not the institution paying for it.

The episode’s policy detour begins with wartime wage controls. Employers could compete for workers through health benefits, while the tax treatment of those benefits helped employer-sponsored insurance spread. Medicare and Medicaid, created in 1965, made government payment another central part of American healthcare.

The resulting structure separated receiving care from paying for it. A physician could not simply tell a patient what an appointment cost and collect the money. Hospitals had to document procedures for insurers and public programs, using standardized information that a payer could audit and challenge.

That changed the purpose of the record. It was clinical memory, but it was also evidence in a financial negotiation. Payers pooled enormous numbers of people and had leverage that an individual practice did not. A hospital needed dependable flows from what happened in a room to what appeared on a claim.

Ben and David frame the complexity of this system as essential context for Epic’s business. Their criticism of American healthcare is not a claim that software alone created its costs or can repair them. It explains why hospital executives would value integrated billing and documentation, even when clinicians might prefer a simpler working day.

04

1966–1970s · 00:28:45

Put the patient at the center of the database

The architectural decision preceded the familiar applications.

At Massachusetts General Hospital, computerized medical-record work produced COSTAR and the MUMPS language and database environment. Concurrent access mattered: several clinicians and administrators could be working with the same patient at once. Losing an update was a different problem from calculating a result on an isolated machine.

Warner Slack’s connections to that world helped bring those ideas to Wisconsin. Judy wrote what became Chronicles, organizing information around the patient. Departments could build screens that retrieved information from that shared underlying structure.

This was still the era of text terminals. Chronicles was not a modern graphical application suite waiting to be unveiled. A hospital ran the database on its computing infrastructure, and users queried it through comparatively spare screens. The future products would accumulate over decades.

The hosts return repeatedly to this architecture because it makes Epic’s later breadth intelligible. Clinical applications, scheduling and billing could draw on a consistent foundation rather than require reconciliation between separately acquired products. A single logical database within a customer’s system does not mean every Epic hospital shares one worldwide database. Nor does Epic own the entire language ecosystem: the episode later distinguishes its application code from the InterSystems technology it uses.

05

1979 · 00:37:02

A reluctant company and a very small financing

Other hospitals wanted what Wisconsin’s doctors were describing.

Wisconsin physicians talked about the system at academic conferences. Other institutions called, asking whether Judy could provide something similar. In the story the hosts heard, she repeatedly resisted starting a business before finally agreeing to a small, part-time undertaking.

Human Services Computing began in 1979. It needed its own computer to develop software outside the university. A seventy-thousand-dollar bank loan helped purchase a Data General Eclipse, and friends and family supplied another seventy thousand dollars of equity. The episode describes a seventy-thousand-dollar pre-money valuation and a hundred-forty-thousand-dollar post-money valuation.

Those numbers are striking beside the eventual enterprise, but they do not mean Epic never borrowed or never issued equity. The more precise story is that this was the company’s primary equity financing. Some early shares were later repurchased; others circulated in secondary transactions. The hosts even mention shares reaching Sequoia without describing a conventional venture round into Epic.

The initial office was a basement in Madison. Judy’s programming ability limited how much outside engineering capacity the business needed at first. What followed was not a rapid venture-funded land grab. The market and the product were both too early for that.

06

Early 1980s · 00:46:08

Learn a company from another programmer

An established medical-software founder helped a potential competitor.

Judy knew how to write software; she did not yet know how to run a company. Warner Slack arranged time with Neil Pappalardo at Meditech. Neil shared practical methods and company materials rather than treating the young founder only as a threat.

The hosts emphasize his example of a programmer-led business. Hiring bright graduates and teaching them a coherent way to work could create an organization around technical competence. Judy also absorbed a skepticism of venture financing and the possibility that outside capital could remove founders from their own companies.

Epic initially concentrated on complex academic institutions with computing infrastructure. Meditech was serving a different part of the hospital market. This was partly a strategic distinction and partly a constraint: a small clinic without a substantial computer could not readily buy what Human Services Computing offered.

In 1983 the company became Epic Systems, a reference to the long story contained in a patient’s life. It had only nine customers. By the end of 1988 it had twenty-four and roughly one and a half million dollars in revenue. That was a viable small software business, but it was still a long way from becoming a national standard.

07

1987–2001 · 00:53:35

The record becomes the operating system

Billing, scheduling and clinical work finally belonged to one product family.

Resolute, launched in 1987, brought billing onto the Chronicles foundation. Cadence supplied scheduling. EpicCare added a graphical clinical application in 1992, initially for ambulatory care: appointments and treatment without an overnight hospital stay.

These were more compelling together than separately. A hospital administrator could see a route from an appointment to the clinical record and then to payment without treating every handoff as an integration project. A mistake could threaten both revenue and patient safety, so reliability carried unusual weight.

Revenue reached eighteen million dollars in 1995. The hosts deliberately compare that with Microsoft’s six billion dollars in the same year, then qualify the comparison. Epic was serving one industry; Microsoft was building a horizontal platform. The resemblance in founder-led engineering did not imply the same growth curve.

In 2001 EpicCare Inpatient extended the system into overnight hospital care, where much of the most complex and expensive treatment occurred. The suite could now cover outpatient clinics, inpatient stays and the billing associated with both. After more than twenty years, Epic could make a much broader promise to an integrated health system.

08

1997–2001 · 01:02:15

Let the patient see the story too

MyChart began as an extension of work already underway.

EpicWeb had given doctors remote access to records. A young programmer, Sumit Rana, wanted a more interesting assignment and discussed it with Carl Dvorak. Opening an analogous window for patients became the project that grew into MyChart, launched in 2000.

The appeal was concrete. Patients could retrieve information, arrange appointments and manage aspects of family members’ care. A parent or caregiver did not have to reconstruct every interaction by telephone. Better scheduling and wait-list functions could help a practice fill appointments as well.

The episode cites 191 million active, deduplicated MyChart users at its 2025 snapshot. The hosts value that access, while later acknowledging the other side: electronic messaging can create more work for doctors, especially when an institution’s processes do not account for it.

With MyChart and inpatient EpicCare added to its foundation, Epic crossed fifty million dollars in revenue and reached eighty-eight health-system customers. It could finally compete for much larger implementations. The next call would test whether that confidence was justified.

09

2003 · 01:10:34

Kaiser needed one answer

The largest prospective customer had already tried several approaches.

Kaiser Permanente combined a health plan and a provider network. At the time described, it included about thirty hospitals, four hundred clinics, eleven thousand physicians and eight and a half million patients. It was large enough that a system failure would be an institutional crisis.

Its California regions had pursued competing proprietary software efforts. Meanwhile, a smaller Pacific Northwest region was using Epic for ambulatory care. Doctors who transferred south asked why the larger regions were spending so much to build something they considered less effective.

Kaiser first selected IBM for a major project, then abandoned that path. When the process reopened, the real contest became Epic against Cerner, a much larger medical-software company. Kaiser initially contemplated a compromise: use Cerner for inpatient work and Epic in outpatient settings.

Judy rejected that arrangement. She told Kaiser to choose one vendor for the whole system. It was a high-risk position for a company only slightly above fifty million dollars in revenue, particularly because Epic’s inpatient product was new. But sharing responsibility would surrender precisely the integration advantage Epic wanted the customer to evaluate.

10

2003–2008 · 01:23:28

Prove the load, then refuse the equity demand

The most important sale still had to work after the contract was signed.

For a decisive technical meeting, Epic’s team prepared to explain its architecture theoretically. Carl Dvorak insisted that this was insufficient. They needed to model Kaiser’s actual flow of transactions and demonstrate the system’s capacity against it.

The team worked through the night on a spreadsheet. As Kaiser moved between the vendors’ presentations, Epic could answer questions with a more concrete account of how the workload would behave. The hosts describe this as a turning point, while acknowledging that the final decision was not yet assured.

Kaiser also requested equity. In the account the hosts heard, Cerner offered a ten-percent stake. Judy refused to give Kaiser warrants, arguing that doing so would create the wrong precedent for all large customers. Epic still won.

The headline four-billion-dollar figure described Kaiser’s overall transformation, including implementation labor and disruption. Roughly four hundred million dollars went to Epic; it was not four billion dollars of Epic revenue. Signing the deal changed the company’s standing, but the successful go-live mattered more. By 2006–2008, other health systems could see that the software worked at Kaiser’s scale. Epic reached roughly five hundred million dollars in revenue in 2007.

11

Early 2000s · 01:30:16

The partnership that left scar tissue

Indirect distribution promised reach and produced an expensive detour.

Not every large-company opportunity reinforced Epic’s confidence. Philips proposed a jointly developed offering, with Epic supplying software and Philips bringing customer relationships and distribution. Philips would market a branded version of the broader system, creating a possible path into more international accounts.

Epic hired in the Netherlands and invested substantial effort. The hosts describe the arrangement collapsing after a relatively short period, without claiming precise accounting for its cost. It left the organization wary of dependencies it could not control.

That experience helps explain the later insistence on building products internally and dealing directly with customers. The hosts explicitly treat it as one contributor to Epic’s closed reputation, not a complete explanation of every interoperability decision.

The distinction matters because Epic would eventually partner again. Its model was not an eternal technical inability to integrate. It was an unusually strong preference for control, reinforced by both the success of the integrated suite and a disappointing attempt to grow through someone else’s channel.

12

2000s onward · 01:33:27

A campus built to recruit a software factory

The whimsy had an organizational purpose.

A visit to Microsoft, where Judy’s son worked, supplied one model: a low-rise campus with private offices, open space and a collegiate atmosphere. A beloved lodge-style room in Epic’s former schoolhouse headquarters supplied another. Customers enjoyed it, and Judy decided to expand that sense of place.

The Verona campus combined farmland with imaginative buildings and themed interiors. The hosts describe seventeen hundred acres overall, with about four hundred ten occupied by the campus, and enormous auditoriums for employees and customers. Deep Space’s eleven thousand four hundred seats made it an ecosystem gathering place as much as an internal meeting room.

The purpose was partly to persuade talented new graduates to move to Wisconsin. Epic could offer difficult work, strong peers and an environment that felt like a world of its own. The hosts connect that recruitment model to Judy’s description of the company as a software factory.

The metaphor is more specific than simply employing programmers. Epic would take relatively inexperienced people, train them in a shared method, and turn that capacity into dependable medical applications. The campus, hiring process and internal standards were parts of the same production system.

13

The Epic operating method · 01:42:29

High trust, taught in considerable detail

A flat organization could still be intensely prescriptive.

Epic’s commandments concern running a company: stay private, avoid acquisitions, make software that works, keep commitments and be frugal. Their placement also reassures visiting customers that the integrated product will not be broken apart by a sale or assembled through a roll-up.

The company taught people how to write emails and take notes, not only how to code. With common expectations, it could operate with relatively few middle managers. The hosts describe an aversion to conventional annual budgets while making clear that financial controls still existed.

Developers went through months of training and spent time in medical settings. Testing was kept close to writing code; programmers had responsibility for fixing their own mistakes. The goal was to minimize defects, not a credible promise that a large system could never contain one.

Implementation roles put young employees into complex, high-stakes customer environments. That could be exhilarating, but it could also make Epic the center of their lives. The hosts describe long hours, an up-or-out approach and considerable early attrition. The whimsy should not conceal the intensity of the employment bargain.

14

The mature operating method · 01:50:09

A tiny sales team and a powerful customer relationship

The buyer’s confidence was a scarce and carefully maintained asset.

Epic systematized hiring through tests rather than relying only on conventional interviews. One early programming test came from work associated with Judy’s son; the episode distinguishes that historical test from the broader set used later. The intention was to find people who could learn Epic’s methods.

Selling looked equally unconventional. The hosts describe only eight people in a formal sales role, largely responding to inbound interest. Technical specialists supported customers over time, while senior relationships and detailed performance discussions helped Epic learn what the institution needed.

Epic strongly preferred standard implementations. Common configurations made updates, support and new modules easier to deliver. A powerful negotiating position let it decline or defer a customer that was not ready to work that way. The hosts describe discounts linked to standardization and upkeep, rather than suggesting every institution paid an identical price.

They also discuss modest annual increases and the claim that MyChart’s price had never changed. Ben cautions that a line-item claim does not reveal the customer’s total economics. Adding features to the suite can help a hospital and strengthen Epic’s position at the expense of separate vendors. Customer focus and competitive ambition operate together.

2006–2008 context · 02:03:31

Three different meanings of sharing

Useful exchange inside the network did not resolve every boundary outside it.

George W. Bush’s push for electronic records preceded the Obama administration. The hosts stress the bipartisan appeal: portability, fewer errors and lower costs sounded desirable even when the practical mechanism was unclear.

Interoperability, however, meant several different things. Epic-to-Epic exchange through Care Everywhere became widely available and ultimately required across its customer base. That was distinct from exchanging data with another EHR vendor, and distinct again from allowing a third-party application to work with sensitive records.

Technical complexity was only one obstacle. Hospitals competed for patients and did not always welcome sharing information with a rival. Vendors had their own incentives. Privacy protections were real, but invoking privacy could also reinforce a commercial gatekeeping position.

Ben and David preserve that tension. They admire the usefulness of shared patient information while acknowledging that business incentives can obstruct it. They do not conclude that all data should be freely exposed, or that every restriction is necessary. Nor does robust exchange among Epic customers mean that every outside developer receives the access it wants.

16

2009–2014 · 02:12:11

Pulling an entire market into the present

Stimulus rewarded systems that could be installed and meaningfully used.

The financial crisis created a search for productive fiscal stimulus. The 2009 HITECH Act, folded into the recovery legislation, offered incentives for adopting and meaningfully using electronic health records, followed by penalties for lagging adoption.

The episode distinguishes roughly twenty-seven billion dollars of direct incentives from a broader thirty-six-billion-dollar program figure. The money flowed through healthcare adoption, not as a thirty-six-billion-dollar check to Epic. The goal included interoperability as well as usage, but the incentives did not make those goals equally successful.

The hosts cite hospital adoption rising from nine percent in 2009 to ninety-five percent in 2014. Reliable vendors that could get systems running were well placed to win the compressed buying cycle. Epic’s Kaiser reference and integrated suite suddenly mattered to many more prospective customers at once.

Their counterfactual is qualified. They think Epic was already likely to become a leading vendor, but HITECH pulled years of procurement forward. That favored the competitive set already in the market and made entry harder for a hypothetical later, better system. They call it a regulatory tailwind rather than treating Judy’s advisory role as proof of regulatory capture; competing vendors also had voices in the process.

Explanatory interlude

The adoption surge and its tradeoff

Incentive

Buyers favor dependable vendors ready to handle the implementation.

Persistence

17

The costs of meaningful use · 02:19:55

Digitization was not the same as transformation

Patients gained access while clinicians inherited new work.

The hosts value what digitization made possible: patient messaging, online appointments, family care management and records that could actually be retrieved. They are much less certain that EHR adoption reduced the total cost of healthcare.

Some evidence they discuss points toward savings. Other arguments point toward additional procedures and more aggressive billing codes. A system that helped a hospital document and collect revenue could succeed for its buyer without reducing what the wider system spent.

Meaningful-use requirements also hardened workflows into mandatory clicks. Some burdens came from regulation, some from hospital implementation choices and some from complex software. Tasks previously delegated informally could now require the physician’s own electronic action. Work followed doctors home.

The episode cites a study reporting roughly two hours of EHR and desk work for each hour of direct patient care. Earlier paper-era administrative-cost figures use different measures and should not be treated as a clean before-and-after comparison. Obama’s later disappointment captures the gap the hosts emphasize: the country had digitized much of its system without achieving the transformation originally imagined. The stimulus debate ends with disagreement about how much credit to give job creation alone.

18

2011–2021 · 02:33:05

The enormous contracts Epic did not win

Losing a procurement could leave room to keep serving the rest of the market.

Epic crossed a billion dollars in revenue in 2011 while competitors consolidated. Cerner’s 2014 purchase of Siemens added another difficult integration to a business assembled through many acquisitions. The episode’s later merger totals should not be projected backward onto the 2003 Kaiser contest.

The Department of Defense selected a Cerner-based proposal in 2015 for a roughly 4.3-billion-dollar program; the VA followed in 2017 with a ten-billion-dollar initial contract. Cerner participated within larger contracting structures. The hosts’ account of overruns concerns the whole governmental implementation, not simply revenue paid to the software vendor.

At the 2025 recording, the Defense rollout had taken years and the VA work remained far from complete. Cerner also lost founder Neil Patterson in 2017 and cycled through leadership changes. The hosts do not isolate one cause for the company’s troubles.

Epic customers interviewed for the episode were relieved their vendor had not become absorbed in those programs. Epic kept winning major health systems, including Mayo Clinic and other academic centers. Revenue reached 2.7 billion dollars in 2018 and 3.2 billion in 2019. Oracle agreed to acquire Cerner in December 2021; the agreement date is not presented here as the transaction’s closing date.

19

2020s · 02:48:10

From the hospital to the rest of the system

Breadth inside the customer led toward adjacent institutions.

By staying focused on large and complex providers, Epic benefited as hospitals consolidated. The hosts consider that consolidation troubling for the wider healthcare system even as it favored an enterprise vendor. Financial pressure, administrative complexity and bargaining power all appear in their explanation; no single cause does all the work.

International expansion offered another avenue, but it was still a minority of revenue. Epic’s domestic strength did not automatically imply the same willingness to pay in countries with different healthcare economics.

The company’s system-of-connectedness ambitions reached toward insurers, pharmaceutical firms, home health and other participants around its provider customers. Prior authorization supplied a concrete example. If the necessary clinical information already existed in the system, some exchanges between provider and payer might be automated rather than repeatedly assembled by hand.

This repeated a familiar pattern: sell a hospital one product, learn which adjacent problems it faces, and add applications that belong naturally on the same foundation. Moving beyond providers could extend the pattern, but the hosts discuss those opportunities as growth vectors, not completed universal solutions.

20

The Cosmos expansion · 02:56:02

A different database for a different purpose

Anonymized aggregation was distinct from operating an individual hospital.

The patient data in an EpicCare installation belonged to the healthcare organization, not to Epic simply because Epic wrote the software. Those installations could run in different hosting arrangements. Cosmos was a separate undertaking: participating organizations could contribute anonymized information to an Epic-hosted research resource.

The episode cites about 295 million patients and fifteen billion encounters in that aggregate at the time of recording. Its scale could make uncommon patterns visible in a way that searching one institution’s files might not. The hosts discuss research, possible medication problems and connections between clinicians dealing with rare conditions.

These are possibilities and examples described in the episode, not proof that the database independently prevented a specified number of injuries. The Flint water discussion illustrates how electronic records can support public-health discovery; it is not a claim that Cosmos caused that discovery.

Contributing institutions could query the resource under the model described. The hosts find the AI implications compelling, but neither that excitement nor the historical availability of access establishes a promise about future pricing, permissions or clinical outcomes. Cosmos extends the story beyond the single-customer database without erasing those distinctions.

2024 results discussed in 2025 · 03:00:21

A vast footprint and a smaller revenue pool

Most of the cost of running an EHR did not become Epic revenue.

The snapshot is substantial: 607 health-system customers, about 3,200 hospitals, fourteen thousand employees and 5.7 billion dollars of 2024 revenue. The hosts cite 42 percent of hospitals, 58 percent of ambulatory physicians and 79 percent of Americans whose care touches Epic. Those are different denominators, not interchangeable market-share claims. They also qualify the remarkable retention story: one customer left for six months before returning, rather than literally no customer ever departing.

Dividing revenue by customers gives roughly ten million dollars annually per health system. The hosts are surprised by how modest that sounds relative to the system’s central role. Hospitals also pay internal staff, consultants and implementation specialists, and absorb downtime and productivity disruption. Those costs can dwarf the check to Epic.

They debate the pricing implication. David sees considerable headroom; Ben is more cautious about inferring it solely from Epic’s share of total spending. Both connect restraint to a long horizon and the desire to avoid giving customers a reason to reconsider the relationship.

Profitability is less transparent. The thirty-to-thirty-five-percent EBITDA margin range is an estimate they heard, not an audited disclosure. Applying thirty percent to 5.7 billion dollars yields about 1.7 billion dollars of estimated EBITDA. That arithmetic should not be mistaken for net income or a confirmed company report.

Succession and valuation · 03:06:14

An empire designed to stay private

A hypothetical market value was different from a realizable exit.

The hosts use several valuation thought experiments. Comparable multiples could suggest a value around fifty billion dollars; their own enthusiasm pushes the hypothetical discussion toward a hundred billion or more. Those are opinions about a company that is not for sale, not a traded price, an appraisal or a financing valuation.

That qualification also limits claims about Judy’s wealth and ranking among entrepreneurs. Her economic ownership was about half the company, while she controlled the voting shares. A hypothetical valuation can illustrate the scale of what she built without establishing a liquid personal fortune.

The episode describes non-voting shares moving to the Roots and Wings foundation, which sells shares back to the company to fund its work. Voting control has a different destination: a purpose trust intended to preserve independence after Judy’s death.

The planned trustees represent family, longtime managers and customer leadership. The hosts describe restrictions against selling or listing the company and requirements that a future chief executive be a longtime Epic employee and a software developer. They admire the continuity, while the value of an investment would still depend on whether one expected distributions or an exit that the structure is designed to prevent.

The hosts’ competitive analysis · 03:11:59

Patience first; several powers later

The advantages visible today were not all present at the beginning.

Ben and David struggle to assign a durable competitive power to early Epic. The single-database idea mattered, but it did not initially confer the overwhelming advantages of the mature suite. A small profitable business could survive while the market and product developed. Time itself was a resource the company could afford to use.

Today, switching costs are immense. Replacing the system means changing clinical operations, billing, integrations and staff habits while care continues. Scale lets Epic spread development across a broad installed base and add applications without separately recovering the full cost from each customer.

Network economies appear in two places: exchange between Epic institutions, and the pool of clinicians already trained on the system. Academic medical centers help reproduce that familiarity. Institutional trust also supplies a brand advantage, even though Epic is not primarily a consumer brand.

They discuss process power more tentatively. Training people into a distinctive engineering method may be difficult to copy, but the underlying MUMPS and Caché technologies are not exclusively Epic’s. The argument concerns the organization built around them. The hosts do not claim all seven powers, or that every possible explanation is equally strong.

24

The hosts’ operating analysis · 03:19:20

Build adjacent products, defend the boundary

The admirable and uncomfortable features are part of the same company.

The hosts contrast Epic’s development spending with its unusually small formal sales effort. The cited research-and-development percentage is a share of operating expenses, not revenue. A low sales-cost denominator is part of why that percentage looks high; the broader argument is that cumulative product investment compounds.

Good adjacent applications can grow from the same customer relationship and data model. That does not make the entire company a recipe another founder can copy quickly. Its independence, history and unusually long period of development are part of the explanation.

The boundary is defended aggressively. The episode discusses intellectual-property litigation, employee restrictions and the Particle Health antitrust case. Particle’s claims were allegations in ongoing litigation at the time, not a verdict establishing wrongdoing. The hosts contrast this adversarial posture with the care Epic takes over its customer relationships.

They call medical enterprise software an intensified version of normal enterprise sales. The buyer is often a CIO, CEO or CFO; the daily user may be a physician. Regulation, sensitive information and patient safety make casual bottom-up adoption harder. Understanding that buyer explains Epic’s success, while also explaining why a satisfied executive is not sufficient evidence of a satisfied clinician.

25

The hosts’ risks · 03:31:18

The bear case requires a difficult change

They see possible vulnerabilities without pretending a challenger has solved them.

International markets may be less lucrative than the United States. An antitrust challenge or information-access requirement could constrain how Epic controls integrations. Stronger interoperability might make separate best-of-breed products more practical.

The hosts discuss the Cures Act and data-access tools as possible openings. Their broad account is not legal advice that every extension, scraping method or transfer is automatically lawful. Even if another interface became popular, replacing the underlying clinical system would remain a much harder undertaking.

Epic could also answer a superior point product with a bundled alternative. A hospital might accept something less specialized if it arrived within an already purchased suite. The same breadth that made the original sale attractive remains a defense.

The most sweeping risk is a paradigm change in care delivery or AI that alters what an institution needs from software. Ben and David acknowledge how speculative that is. Their difficulty constructing a near-term displacement story is a judgment about durability, not proof that litigation, regulation or a technological shift can never hurt the business.

26

The hosts’ bull case · 03:35:11

AI might remove work—or reinforce the gatekeeper

An attractive possibility is not a demonstrated outcome.

The bull case starts with the existing base: more applications, more institutions, and expansion toward payers, research and pharmaceutical firms. None requires every healthcare cost to become Epic revenue. The hosts repeatedly distinguish enthusiasm for the business from frustration at the size and waste of the surrounding system.

Ambient documentation offers a specific AI use. A system could listen to a consultation and help produce notes, reducing the typing and clicking that physicians dislike. The episode names partners in that field and describes Epic’s position in deciding which innovations reach its customers. Partnership access is much more developed than it was years earlier, though it can involve commercial terms.

Why this kind of breadth worked here

The vertical was narrow in definition and enormous in economic size.

The last substantive exchange returns to a puzzle: why did building so much software internally work here? Ben and David answer by distinguishing a vertical industry from a broad horizontal market. Epic could spend decades understanding the related needs of a particular kind of institutional buyer.

In healthcare, the consequences of an integration failure are unusually severe. A consistent system has value across clinical work, billing and compliance. Listening carefully to hospital leadership and supplying more of what it needs can therefore outperform a collection of independent products, even when some individual applications are less elegant.

That is not a universal instruction to ignore platforms or partnerships. The hosts explicitly frame the argument around this market and this customer. The clinicians using the screens, patients receiving care and executives buying the system still have different interests; the episode never resolves those differences simply by declaring Epic successful.

Most vertical software companies face a limited addressable market. American healthcare is an exceptional vertical. In their closing comparison with Bloomberg, Veeva and Constellation, the hosts see how unusual Epic’s scale is. Their final admiration is for a company built patiently around one integrated system, with the costs and tensions of that system still visible.
