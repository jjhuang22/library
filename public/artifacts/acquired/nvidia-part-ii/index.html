Chapter 01 · 2004–2006

The software inside the chip company

A successful gaming business supplies the skills for a much less obvious bet.

By the middle of the 2000s, Jensen Huang had plenty of reasons to remain a graphics entrepreneur. Nvidia had survived its early crises and become a public company serving a growing gaming market. In this episode’s opening return to those years, Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal identify three things that made the next chapter possible: a fast chip cadence, an unusually deep software organization, and a relationship with developers who were beginning to program the GPU itself.

The cadence was concrete. GeForce 256 arrived in fall 1999, GeForce 2 in spring 2000, GeForce 2 Ultra that fall, GeForce 3 the following spring, and GeForce 3 Ti500 six months later. The hosts contrast this rhythm with competitors’ much longer development cycles. Nvidia had learned to keep delivering while a complicated product was still becoming obsolete beneath its feet.

Less visible was its decision to write its own drivers. Other graphics suppliers could leave this work to downstream partners. Nvidia accepted the fixed expense of supporting different systems and boards because a gamer buying an expensive card wanted it to work. That decision accumulated programmers who understood the difficult machinery close to the hardware. The hosts see this as an essential foundation for a company that would eventually sell far more than chips.

Programmable shaders supplied the third ingredient. With GeForce 3 and the Cg language developed with Microsoft, game developers could address Nvidia’s hardware more directly. An Nvidia developer was becoming a meaningful category. These were still gaming investments, built for gaming customers. Huang wanted to take the architecture somewhere larger.

The bridge is a story the hosts explicitly call almost surely apocryphal: a Stanford researcher supposedly bought GeForce cards on his son’s advice and accelerated his scientific work enough to complete it in his lifetime. The anecdote matters here as an expression of Huang’s conviction, not as verified biography. Researchers really were forcing nongraphical calculations through graphics programming. A chemistry problem had to masquerade as triangles, lighting, and pixels. What if Nvidia removed that disguise?

Chapter 02 · 2006–2011

A platform before a market

CUDA demands an entire development environment—and years of patience.

CUDA, the Compute Unified Device Architecture, was a much larger undertaking than adding another graphics interface. Beginning in 2006, Nvidia invested in a way for programmers to use GPUs for general computation. A language extension or API was only the beginning. Compilers, libraries, development tools, domain-specific frameworks, and developer support all had to exist before the hardware’s power would become accessible.

The architecture was built for parallel work. Where calculations can proceed independently, many cores can work at once rather than waiting for a serial processor to finish each instruction. The hosts explain the term “embarrassingly parallel” as a problem so suited to this arrangement that doing it serially would be the embarrassment. A faster processor was only useful if developers could express their problems in a form it could exploit.

Scientific computing was the initial market they could point to: researchers sharing supercomputers, drug discovery, and perhaps professional graphics or architecture. Ben and David struggle to make those markets large enough to justify the investment. Ben says CUDA would take more than six years to become a useful, usable platform. Looking back from 2022, he finds roughly 1,100 Nvidia employees with CUDA in their LinkedIn titles—an illustrative search, not an audited staffing count. This was a company building a computing environment while the business case remained elusive.

The economics were equally striking. CUDA was free to obtain and use, but proprietary to Nvidia hardware. OpenCL applications could run on Nvidia chips; CUDA applications could not simply run elsewhere. The hosts compare the arrangement to Apple giving developers a platform and earning money on the hardware that makes it work. They qualify that analogy: the integrated Microsoft–Intel–IBM world also had proprietary development environments. The relevant question was whether Nvidia could ever reach enough scale to pay for its own full stack.

Then the existing business stumbled. AMD’s acquisition of ATI put more resources behind Nvidia’s remaining serious graphics rival. Nvidia missed earnings in mid-2008 while CUDA was still consuming investment rather than producing a new revenue engine. From its roughly $20 billion peak market value, the stock fell about 80%. The financial crisis was part of the setting, but the hosts argue the company had also taken its attention off gaming. Huang kept building.

The detour into mobile did not solve the problem. Tegra, launched in 2008, combined an Arm-based CPU with the other components of a smartphone system on a chip. Its first shipping product, the Microsoft Zune HD, makes David laugh at the mismatch between ambition and outcome. Tegra later found homes in the original Tesla Model S touchscreen and Nintendo Switch, but smartphones had not become Nvidia’s next great business.

In 2011 Nvidia bought the British baseband company Icera, then ultimately shut that operation down. Its founders went on to start Graphcore, a future challenger the hosts would discuss in their bear case. This is an irony in the episode, not a verdict on Graphcore’s eventual success. Another earnings disappointment in 2011 brought another roughly 50% stock drawdown. David wonders whether mobile was an effort to reassure shareholders, while explicitly labeling that motive his speculation. Through it all, the central CUDA bet remained unresolved.

Chapter 03 · 2012–2013

The market arrives sideways

An academic image competition reveals what the waiting hardware can do.

The breakthrough came from people trying to teach computers to recognize images. Fei-Fei Li and collaborators built ImageNet, a large labeled-image database, with help from Amazon Mechanical Turk. They turned it into a recurring competition: teams could test their systems against a common task and compare error rates. A picture might contain a dog or a strawberry. The machine had to identify it without being handed the answer.

In 2012, a University of Toronto team took a dramatically different approach. Alex Krizhevsky, working with Ilya Sutskever and adviser Geoffrey Hinton, entered AlexNet, a convolutional neural network. The hosts describe an error rate around 15%, against previous leading results around 25%. The magnitude of the improvement mattered as much as the victory. Deep learning had demonstrated that the existing trajectory was not the limit.

Neural networks were not a new idea. What had constrained them was the computation required to train them. AlexNet used CUDA on Nvidia GPUs to make an old class of ideas practical for this problem. The matrix calculations that made graphics a natural parallel workload were also well suited to deep learning. Nvidia had spent years building a platform for general computation; here was an application with far larger implications than the scientific markets it had first imagined.

The hosts refuse to make this feel inevitable. David calls the arrival a miracle and rejects the idea that Huang planned AlexNet. Ben notes that Huang presents the strategy as intentional. Their shared point is more precise than either legend: Nvidia had prepared a platform, was watching closely, and reacted immediately when the important use case appeared.

Ben then describes a 2013 research effort involving Nvidia’s Bryan Catanzaro, Andrew Ng, and other Stanford researchers. They reproduced a Google Brain-style unsupervised-learning result with three nodes rather than a thousand. In the episode’s account, that work became central to cuDNN, the deep-neural-network library within CUDA’s ecosystem. The significance was accessibility: scientists who were not hardware specialists could do powerful work without rebuilding all the machinery themselves.

The commercial engine was not initially a robot or a self-driving car. It was the internet’s ability to predict the next piece of content or advertisement a person would want. Google, Facebook, and others had both the data and an immediate reason to improve those predictions. The hosts characterize advertising as the obvious, well-labeled use case that financed greater scale. Their larger technological excitement extends beyond it, but the cash-producing market matters to why this investment could finally pay.

Chapter 04 · 2012–2019

A breakthrough is not a straight line

The market takes years to believe; crypto introduces a different kind of uncertainty.

Understanding the technology did not mean the stock market immediately understood the business. The hosts recall Nvidia shares remaining below $5 through the 2012–2015 period, using the share-price basis in their 2022 conversation. Only in 2016 did the company regain the approximately $20 billion market value it had reached in 2007. Almost a decade had passed between those two peaks.

Huang was discussing neural networks openly on stages and earnings calls. Ben remembers the skepticism of people who followed semiconductors closely. Years of promises before CUDA found a large market had made the story easier to dismiss. Marc Andreessen’s 2016 observation crystallized what was changing: the deep-learning startups his firm saw were all building on Nvidia, much as earlier companies had built on Windows or the iPhone.

Another parallel workload complicated the picture. Crypto miners bought consumer graphics cards in 2016 and 2017. When the crypto market fell in 2018, demand for mining rigs fell with it. Nvidia’s revenue declined and its stock suffered another roughly 50% drawdown. A new use for the hardware had become large enough to unsettle the company without being a market Nvidia had deliberately built.

The distribution channel made the problem difficult to see. Nvidia sold cards into retailers and other intermediaries; the person taking a card home might be a gamer or a miner. Even management lacked a clean view of what consumers were buying them to do. When mining became less profitable, a demand stream disappeared that Nvidia had not been able to measure reliably. The episode treats this as a visibility problem as well as a cyclical one.

Later, Nvidia restricted mining performance on consumer cards and offered dedicated crypto hardware. The public explanation emphasized keeping cards available for gamers. Ben also sees deliberate segmentation: customers making money from the hardware could be directed toward a separate product, reducing arbitrage and making revenue easier to understand. He compares it to Nvidia’s separate rules for enterprise customers. The hosts do not suggest the mining cycle itself had become predictable.

Chapter 05 · 2018–2022

The box becomes the data center

Enterprise customers buy connected systems, and the economics change.

For years, gaming still paid the bills. Ben describes the business roughly two years before the recording as almost $6 billion of gaming revenue against about $3 billion in data center revenue. By the episode’s 2022 vantage point, data center revenue exceeded $10.5 billion annually and was roughly as large as gaming. The new market had finally become a business of comparable scale.

Enterprise customers paid for much more than a consumer card. The hosts discuss data center GPUs costing tens of thousands of dollars, contrasting them with a high-end gaming card in the low thousands. David estimates that Tesla’s then-current training cluster might have represented $50–100 million paid to Nvidia. He explicitly describes doing the math with assumed pricing; it is his estimate, not a disclosed contract value. He also distinguishes Tesla’s own on-car inference chips from the Nvidia hardware it was still using to train models.

Nvidia reinforced the product boundary. In 2018, it changed consumer GeForce terms to prohibit use in data centers. Meanwhile, enterprise products diverged physically: an A100 did not even have a video output. These were compute devices, despite the historical name “graphics processing unit.” Enterprise willingness to pay could now be met with a differentiated system and a distinct commercial arrangement.

The approximately $7 billion acquisition of Mellanox in 2020 expanded what Nvidia could connect. NVLink joined chips at high bandwidth; Mellanox supplied the networking capabilities to connect machines across the data center with high bandwidth and low latency. The enterprise customer was increasingly buying boxes full of components and, ultimately, a coordinated solution. Ben’s joke—he hears “gross margin” when David says “solutions”—captures their explanation of the economics.

This also brings the DPU, or data processing unit, into the story. The CPU remains the general workhorse. The GPU accelerates suitable workloads, with specialized features such as tensor cores for machine learning. The DPU handles movement and transformation of data. Ben describes a change in the unit of abstraction: instead of thinking about one machine as a black box, a developer could increasingly treat the data center itself that way.

Buying Arm would have extended that ambition further. Nvidia’s proposed acquisition failed under regulatory pressure. Arm licensed its instruction set and chip designs across the industry without manufacturing chips itself. Putting that supplier inside a vertically integrated competitor created an obvious concern about access and neutrality. The hosts find Nvidia’s proposed benefits plausible, including bringing its software to Arm-based hardware, while finding the opposition unsurprising.

The failed deal did not erase the CPU ambition. Nvidia had announced Grace, an Arm-based data center CPU, alongside the Hopper GPU architecture. At the March 2022 GTC discussed in the episode, Nvidia described three million registered CUDA developers, 450 SDKs and models, and 60 new ones announced at the conference. The significance is the accumulated ecosystem, not a claim that every developer or tool represented equal economic value.

Chapter 06 · 2022

Selling the stack twice

Free developer tools become an enterprise licensing opportunity.

By the recording, Nvidia had about 20,000 employees, $27 billion in annual revenue, and roughly 60% annual growth. The hosts cite about $8 billion in yearly free cash flow and $21 billion in cash. They repeatedly connect that performance with the company’s transformation from a component supplier into a seller of architecture, systems, software, and enterprise solutions.

Gross margin makes the change visible. They trace it from about 30% in 1999, past 50% in 2014, to 66% at their 2022 vantage point. These are different periods in the hosts’ comparison, not a continuous data series. The argument is that an integrated bundle captures more value than an interchangeable chip. CUDA was free, but that did not make the software economically irrelevant.

At the 2022 analyst day, Nvidia described licensing more of its software separately. Enterprises did not necessarily want to download components and assemble a working production environment. They wanted supported products that could run their business. David compares the opportunity with enterprise companies built around making open-source software usable, while keeping Nvidia’s own proprietary platform distinct from those examples.

Ben sees a potential strategy conflict. If software is what differentiates your hardware, selling it separately can undermine the hardware business. His explanation for Nvidia’s approach is segmentation: use pricing, channels, and terms to reach customers who would not simply have bought hardware and received the same benefits. Both hosts expect the opportunity to be incremental rather than an abandonment of the hardware model. That is their interpretation of the strategy, not proof of how future licensing revenue would develop.

Their admiration does not settle valuation. Nvidia was worth roughly half a trillion dollars in the conversation—about the eighth-largest company by market value—on revenue far below the largest internet businesses. They describe a revenue multiple around three times Apple’s and nearly twice Microsoft’s. Growth can justify a different multiple, Ben argues; it does not make every expectation safe. Their jokes about a trillion-dollar addressable market are directed at the looseness of the presentation, not a denial that the business was profitable.

Chapter 07 · 2022

Gaming feeds the machine

The old business benefits from the new one, while distribution remains less integrated.

Nvidia had not abandoned gaming. Ray tracing modeled the paths of light to create more realistic scenes, but doing that in real time made the trade-off between image detail and frame rate particularly demanding. Deep learning offered a way to alter that trade-off rather than merely choosing one side.

With DLSS, or deep learning supersampling, a game could render at a lower resolution and use learned information from nearby pixels and previous frames to produce a higher-resolution output. Ben delights in machine learning becoming part of the graphics pipeline itself. Game developers had to do integration work; it was not a capability detached from the developer relationships Nvidia had cultivated. AMD had a competing approach, another qualification the hosts include.

The consumer business was also less direct than the shorthand “buy an Nvidia card” suggested. Add-in board partners such as ASUS, MSI, and ZOTAC added cooling, branding, and other components to the products buyers saw. Nvidia sold its own Founders Edition reference cards too, but Ben did not think they represented most sales. He speculated that a company seeking this much control might eventually want to go more direct. The episode offers that as a prediction, not an accomplished change.

Together, these examples complicate a simple picture of vertical integration. Nvidia could control a proprietary software platform, differentiate a whole enterprise system, and still depend on retail and manufacturing partners. The hosts’ story is about the expanding portion of the experience it could own, rather than a claim that it already did everything itself.

Chapter 08 · 2022 ambitions

Simulate before you deploy

Cars, robots, and digital twins extend a proven digital business toward the physical world.

Automotive was still a small, slow-growing segment in the hosts’ account. They explicitly hesitate over its revenue, so an exact figure would give their discussion a certainty it does not have. What interests Ben is the proposed change in role: from supplying fairly ordinary automotive components to supplying much of the hardware and software for electric and autonomous vehicles.

His imagined result is an Nvidia computer surrounded by a carmaker’s chassis and brand. Established manufacturers know how to build and distribute cars, but many may not build an autonomous-driving stack themselves. Lotus and Ferrari serve as the hosts’ examples of why a supplier might gain more influence. They do not present these relationships as proof that this entire market was already won.

Three uncertainties govern the idea in Ben’s formulation: whether autonomous vehicles become real, whether they arrive soon, and whether the disruption is large enough for a component supplier to capture more of the value chain. The comparison with Android is suggestive, but the hosts do not erase the carmakers’ incentives to keep control. Nvidia’s own automotive opportunity remains a thesis in this episode.

Omniverse extends the same ambition in another direction. Ben distinguishes it from a social virtual world. The proposed customer is an enterprise that wants to simulate a real system before changing it: a warehouse, a robot fleet, or a climate model. Earth-2, the digital twin of the planet discussed on the show, is an illustration of the scale of the pitch.

Consider the warehouse example the hosts use. A company trains models with Nvidia’s hardware and software; robots use hardware to perform inference in the world. Before a new model changes how the robots move, the customer can test it in a simulation. Ben believes strongly in that future. David calls it an enterprise metaverse whose main purpose is running applications, often without a human present. These are their expectations in April 2022, not a report on what later happened.

Chapter 09 · Closing analysis

What can displace fifteen years?

Specialized chips threaten the GPU; the software makes the comparison harder.

The bear case has several distinct competitors. AMD remains a serious alternative in gaming, where games often run on either company’s hardware. In the enterprise, a new architecture might accelerate workloads away from the GPU itself. The hosts discuss Graphcore and Cerebras as attempts to do something more specialized than Nvidia’s increasingly versatile GPU.

Cerebras particularly fascinates Ben: a wafer-scale chip, described as dinner-plate sized, with redundancy to tolerate defective portions of the wafer. He cites enormous power requirements and a roughly $2 million system price in the discussion. Those are the episode’s illustrative comparisons for a specialized AI-training approach then deployed at research and beta sites, not a claim of a mature replacement market. Better performance for a particular task still has to become a usable and attractive system for customers.

The other threat comes from Nvidia’s customers. Companies paying large, high-margin bills have a reason to design their own silicon. Tesla had already switched to its own on-car inference chips, while its training change was still pending in the hosts’ account. Apple had its own GPUs. Google’s TPUs were the most developed data center challenge they discussed, available through Google Cloud rather than sold as retail chips.

Ben calls Google’s business model counter-positioned: it might accept lower chip margins to differentiate and grow its cloud service. The exact cost of a TPU was not public in their discussion. Nvidia, meanwhile, also benefited when customers rented its GPUs through AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, and was beginning its own cloud offering. The infrastructure market could have multiple routes to the same customer.

Yet reproducing hardware alone would not reproduce Nvidia. David returns to fifteen years of CUDA, libraries, and the hardware beneath them. Nvidia spread that investment across customers throughout the market. A company building only for itself had to make its own narrower opportunity worth the enormous expense. Google at least had a cloud channel through which to sell the result.

In Seven Powers terms, both hosts identify scale economies: the large fixed software investment can be amortized over a huge user and customer base. They also identify switching costs. Replacing Nvidia often means replacing CUDA and the work built on it, rather than swapping one nominally comparable chip for another.

Other powers remain less settled. Ben asks whether CUDA is a cornered resource; David entertains the idea without resolving it. They are less confident about process power today than in the early rapid-release years. Talent moves, and manufacturing partners can work with rivals. The hosts do not establish every item in the Seven Powers list merely because they enumerate it. Their final description is a platform with a “stew” of reinforcing advantages.

The Lapsus$ hack offers a revealing episode within that discussion. The attackers demanded removal of crypto restrictions and release of driver source code. Ben is careful that he does not think the latter demand concerned CUDA itself. To him, it illustrates the economic value of Nvidia’s tightly coupled proprietary software and hardware; it is not evidence that a particular competitor could instantly recreate the platform.

Chapter 10 · Playbook & grading

A great business at a demanding price

The closing question is how much future growth the shareholder already paid for.

David’s first playbook observation is the supplier’s position in a gold rush: one need not know which AI startup wins to see that many are buying the same tools. Ben emphasizes a mission that expanded from enabling graphics as a storytelling medium to accelerating computation wherever a CPU needs help. They connect this to Huang’s willingness to endure repeated periods when outsiders doubted the business. David credits both strategic decisions and fortunate events. Survival did not prove every gamble was planned.

The economics also depend on what Nvidia does not manufacture. Ben compares roughly $1 billion in annual capital expenditure with around $10 billion at Apple, $25 billion at Microsoft and Google, and $30 billion at TSMC. These are his period comparisons, used to illustrate the fabless model. Nvidia pays for design, software, and intellectual property while partners bear the capital burden of fabrication. Low capital expenditure should not be confused with the absence of costly software development.

He cites a 37% operating margin as another striking feature. The 66% gross margin measures what remains after direct costs; operating margin also reflects headcount, leases, and the other expenses of running the business. It is a measure of operating profitability, rather than the same thing as free cash flow. The hosts see a company whose hardware sales carry economics resembling a software platform.

When they turn to grading, they deliberately choose the prospective shareholder’s problem. A company can be excellent while its stock disappoints. At the price in April 2022, what has to go right? Ben’s positive case includes continued data center dominance, selling more complete systems, and Nvidia learning from challengers rather than assuming those challengers fail. The company had already changed what “GPU” meant; it might keep adding capabilities or following an innovation when that made sense.

The unresolved question is whether digital AI alone offers enough runway. David wonders whether autonomous vehicles, robotics, and Omniverse are optional upside or necessary to justify the valuation. Ben leans toward necessity: an A+ outcome means large physical-world markets with Nvidia providing much of the machinery. David agrees that is plausible but remains reluctant to assume the internet’s digital opportunities are nearly exhausted. The companion leaves their disagreement open.

Failure has two meanings as well. Ben finds it difficult to imagine the operating business collapsing quickly, but easy to imagine the stock falling if expectations unravel. David asks how much of the recent growth might have been pulled forward by the pandemic. He doubts it resembles the fullest pull-forward cases they discuss; both acknowledge that it is hard to quantify. A strong balance sheet and a compelling platform do not resolve the timing of demand or the price investors should pay.

That is where the episode’s business argument ends: a market that once seemed missing has become substantial, while a new physical-world vision asks investors to believe again. The closing carve-outs return to smaller pleasures—David’s Expanse short-story collection and Ben’s Sony RX100 camera—before the hosts’ community announcements and farewell. This account follows their April 2022 perspective throughout.
