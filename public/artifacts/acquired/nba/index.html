The NBA

A game becomes a world

From winter exercise and hockey’s empty nights to a league built around stars, global audiences, and the value of attention.

An illustrated reading companion to Acquired’s The NBA, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

Released · September 30, 2020Created · October 18, 2026About 20 minutes

The full business story and concluding discussion in the supplied transcript. Figures and forecasts retain the episode’s 2020 perspective.

01

1891–1946

A game travels before a league exists

A peach basket becomes a product; the YMCA provides the distribution.

James Naismith was teaching physical education at the YMCA Training School in Springfield, Massachusetts, during the winter of 1891. He needed a way to keep students moving indoors. His answer was a soccer ball, an elevated peach basket, and a short set of rules. The early game was awkward: the basket still had a bottom, and players could not run with the ball. Yet it required little equipment, offered exercise without the collisions of football, and was enjoyable enough to spread.

Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal begin their business history here because basketball’s first distribution network preceded any professional league. YMCA organizations carried the game across North America and overseas. In the hosts’ account, it reached China before 1900. Women also adopted the sport early, and schools and colleges gave it an enduring place in their winter calendars. The NBA would eventually commercialize a game whose audience and playing culture had already been developing for decades.

Professional teams appeared before a durable national league. Barnstorming clubs traveled from town to town, selling exhibition games rather than a regular league schedule. The hosts emphasize the Harlem Globetrotters, originally from Chicago, and the New York Renaissance Five, known as the Rens. These Black teams were accomplished competitors and major attractions, playing enormous numbers of games. Professional basketball already had talent and paying customers; what it lacked was a stable structure with access to enough large venues.

That distinction matters throughout the episode. Ben describes basketball as a combination of product-market fit and distribution. The game’s appeal did not automatically create a successful league. The organization that became the NBA first found an answer to the less romantic problem of where to play and how to fill seats.

02

1946–1966

Hockey’s empty nights

The league begins as a way to earn more from existing buildings.

On June 6, 1946, owners of hockey arenas founded the Basketball Association of America in New York. Their immediate ambition was to monetize nights when their buildings were otherwise empty. Maurice Podoloff, already an administrator in hockey, received the job of organizing the new venture. The first game of the lineage the hosts follow took place that November, when Toronto’s Huskies hosted the New York Knickerbockers.

Arena access solved one obstacle that had defeated earlier attempts at leagues. But the founders also imposed an exclusion that made the business and the sport worse: they initially barred Black players, despite the quality and popularity of the Globetrotters and Rens. The hosts condemn both the racism and the refusal to recognize where the best basketball was already being played. They identify Wataru Misaka, who played in 1947–48, as an early nonwhite player, and describe Black players entering the league in 1950.

The BAA combined with the National Basketball League in 1949 and adopted the National Basketball Association name. Stability did not arrive all at once. Teams moved, folded, and changed names. An early salary cap quickly collapsed under resistance from larger markets. The game itself also needed work: without a shot clock, a team protecting a lead could simply hold the ball. The hosts use the slow adoption of the clock as an example of owners failing to improve the spectacle they were trying to sell.

Bill Russell and Wilt Chamberlain helped turn a small league into something more compelling. Russell anchored the Celtics’ extraordinary championship run and later became their player-coach. Chamberlain’s scoring, physical presence, and celebrity gave the league another attraction. Television began to expose their talents to a broader public. The NBA secured a five-year, $4 million ABC rights agreement in the 1960s, according to the episode. Producer Roone Arledge used replays and close attention to individual matchups to make viewers see the game’s grace and intensity.

03

1964–1976

The players discover their leverage

A national broadcast creates a bargaining moment; a rival league creates a market.

The 1964 All-Star Game brought the league’s dependence on its players into sharp relief. Led by stars including Russell and Chamberlain, players threatened to withhold their labor shortly before ABC’s broadcast unless the league agreed to a pension plan. The network had paid for a national event and did not intend to air an empty court. Under pressure from both players and television, the league conceded. The hosts treat the confrontation as an important step toward the modern players’ union and a less one-sided division of the game’s proceeds.

A second source of leverage arrived with the American Basketball Association in 1967. Its founders saw cities the NBA had not served and pitched an eventual merger as their opportunity. The ABA could recruit players earlier than the NBA’s rules allowed, including college undergraduates. It signed Julius Erving, or Dr. J, and promoted a more flamboyant entertainment product: three-point shooting, perimeter play, and a dunk contest that could attract attention independently of a game’s final score.

The rival leagues bid for the same talent. The episode illustrates the change with Chamberlain’s $250,000 annual Lakers contract and the competition for Lew Alcindor, later Kareem Abdul-Jabbar. David recounts Alcindor asking for one best offer from each side and honoring his choice of Milwaukee even after the rival bid increased. Some details are recalled tentatively in the conversation; the central point is the bargaining power created by two buyers for exceptional players.

When the leagues moved toward a merger, the players had reason to resist losing that competition. Their antitrust challenge delayed the combination for years. The eventual 1976 settlement removed the reserve-clause barrier the hosts describe: teams could no longer use an extra year of control after a contract expired in the same way. Free agency made the labor market more open. The NBA’s modern structure emerged from negotiation with the people who produced its attraction, rather than from owners acting alone.

04

1976–1979

Winning the merger can mean paying to enter

The ABA’s innovations survive; its teams arrive on punishing terms.

The delay changed the balance of power. The ABA had creativity and stars, but its weaker teams struggled to meet payroll and sustain operations. By the time the deal closed in 1976, the NBA could dictate terms that looked much more like an admission fee than a merger of equals. Denver, Indiana, the New York Nets, and San Antonio entered the NBA. Most of their former rivals disappeared.

Ben details the price: a $3.2 million entry payment for each incoming club, no television revenue for their first three seasons, and restrictions on their initial influence over league decisions. The Nets faced an additional $4.8 million payment to the Knicks for entering their territory. To raise money, they sold Erving to Philadelphia. A team could gain the security of the NBA while losing the player who made it worth watching.

The hosts also linger on the unusual bargain made by the owners of the ABA’s St. Louis franchise. Rather than bring a team into the NBA, they took an upfront payment and a continuing share of television money associated with the four incoming teams. David cites more than $150 million received through 2009, the horizon of the book he is discussing. He is unsure of the arrangement’s later status and of some details of the television payments. The episode’s anecdote illustrates the enormous future value hidden inside media rights; it does not establish what happened to that agreement after the period cited.

By 1979, the stage was set for a different kind of growth. Larry Bird and Magic Johnson entered the league, the NBA adopted the three-point line, and ESPN launched. SportsCenter could turn a long schedule of games into short, memorable sequences of athletic performance. A dunk, pass, or rivalry could introduce viewers to the league in minutes. The ABA had shown that the surrounding entertainment could be a product in its own right; cable television gave that idea a recurring national home.

05

1983–1997

A partnership gets a bigger stage

David Stern links the economics of players and owners, then expands the audience.

David Stern came to the NBA through legal work, including the ABA merger, before joining the league as counsel. In 1983, before becoming commissioner, he helped negotiate a collective bargaining agreement that exchanged a salary cap for an aggregate commitment to pay players 53% of gross revenue. The hosts stress the word revenue: players’ participation would grow with the business, rather than depend on owners’ reported profits. They compare it to an economic partnership while making clear that players did not thereby receive equity in the teams.

The scale was still modest. Starting from a $3.6 million cap and the 53% share, David estimates roughly $6.8 million in revenue per team and about $150 million across the league. He and Ben disagree briefly over how profitable that business was; neither claims every franchise was profitable. Stern became commissioner in February 1984, the same year Chicago drafted Michael Jordan. Exceptional talent and a determined marketer arrived together.

The hosts describe the subsequent television reset as transformative. Two four-year NBC and Turner agreements negotiated in 1989 totaled $875 million, or more than $200 million a year. On their comparison, national television alone was now worth more annually than the whole league’s revenue near the start of Stern’s tenure. Revenue participation meant that the players shared in that expansion. Stern also addressed the league’s drug problem with testing and escalating penalties, a program the hosts discuss as part of his effort to rebuild the product and its reputation.

His larger ambition was to internationalize the NBA, not merely basketball. The 1992 Olympic Dream Team put its biggest stars in front of a worldwide audience. The hosts credit Stern with pushing the opportunity, while acknowledging the involvement of many people. Overseas offices would help both recruit talent and sell the entertainment. Following the women’s success at the 1996 Olympics, he championed the WNBA’s launch. League Pass, introduced in the mid-1990s, added another route to fans. The episode treats these as connected attempts to widen who could play, who could watch, and how the league could reach them.

06

1984–2000s

The person becomes a business

Jordan’s reach extends far beyond a salary or a playing career.

Jordan’s Nike agreement supplied a parallel version of the same expansion. He had wanted Adidas, the hosts say, but met with Nike and signed in 1984. David recalls the initial economics tentatively, then focuses on the outcome: by the episode’s 2020 recording, Jordan had personally earned more than $1.5 billion from the relationship, and the Jordan Brand had generated more than $3 billion in revenue in the preceding year. These are the hosts’ contemporary figures, not updated valuations.

The arrangement helped establish a model in which an athlete could build enduring commercial value around an identity. Jordan’s influence survived retirement. So did the possibilities for other players. The hosts point to Magic Johnson’s businesses, Kobe Bryant’s estate, Shaquille O’Neal’s ventures, and less famous alumni such as Junior Bridgeman and Vinnie Johnson. Their emphasis is on Black entrepreneurship and the ability to turn playing income and public recognition into lasting enterprises. The discussion does not attribute every subsequent business success to an endorsement contract.

Jordan also crossed into ownership. Ben describes his purchase of control of Charlotte in 2010 at a roughly $175 million valuation and a later minority sale valuing the club at $1.5 billion. In the episode’s timeframe, he was the exceptional former player among an ownership group dominated by wealthy entrepreneurs and investors. The trajectory from employee to brand owner to franchise owner makes the hosts’ argument tangible: players were becoming powerful economic actors outside the game.

But Stern’s willingness to promote stars had limits. After Jordan’s final retirement, the league faced falling ratings, criticism of players, and the fallout from the arena brawl known as the Malice at the Palace. In 2005 it introduced a dress code, including restrictions on chains, headgear, and other clothing. Ben and David criticize its racial and generational assumptions, particularly its treatment of Allen Iverson. They argue that the executive who had helped players prosper was now resisting their expression.

Players found a way to reclaim the stage. David describes Dwyane Wade and then LeBron James embracing formal dress while turning arena arrivals and press conferences into fashion events. Instagram would amplify those identities. The players were no longer only personalities selected for the league’s advertising; they were learning to build audiences of their own.

07

2002–2020

One league, many ways to belong

International talent, streaming, and player voices reinforce one another.

When Houston selected Yao Ming first in the 2002 draft, it connected the NBA to an enormous existing basketball culture. David calls it both bringing China to the NBA and bringing the NBA to China. He cites estimates of 300 million people playing basketball in China and 600 million watching it, explicitly as estimates. The Rockets became a powerful bridge between a US team and a worldwide audience. By 2020, the hosts put international players at a quarter of NBA rosters and see many of the next generation’s leading stars coming from abroad.

Audience growth was not confined to watching full games on American television. The league worked with Turner on League Pass rather than owning its own technology stack. It offered games alongside highlights, commentary, and behind-the-scenes material. In 2019 it announced a five-year, $1.5 billion streaming agreement with Tencent in China. The NBA also embraced basketball video games and elevated the 2K League alongside the NBA, WNBA, and G League in its public family of brands. The hosts see this as a larger entertainment ecosystem surrounding the core competition.

Ben gives the strategy a more direct name than David’s Disney-inspired phrase “unique and compelling content”: empowering the players. They cite LeBron’s roughly 119 million combined Twitter and Instagram followers at recording, compared with about 80 million for the league’s own accounts. These sums are platform follower counts, not deduplicated people. Players could introduce young fans to the sport through fashion, businesses, opinions, and ordinary life between games. A jersey sponsor could appear in those images too. Ben and David argue that the player’s independent audience could increase the league’s commercial reach.

The 2020 bubble made the relationship especially visible. The hosts discuss the league’s suspension of play in March, its return at Disney, Black Lives Matter markings on courts, and its response when Milwaukee players refused to play in protest. Ben’s interpretation is that the NBA often followed players’ leadership quickly rather than fighting it. David sees moral and commercial reasons for supporting their voices. Neither presents that alignment as seamless or as a guarantee that every institutional decision was right.

China exposed the conflict inside the strategy. Houston executive Daryl Morey’s 2019 tweet supporting Hong Kong protesters disrupted Chinese broadcasting and commercial relationships. The hosts cite Adam Silver’s estimate of a $400 million revenue loss, while acknowledging uncertainty about particular streaming arrangements. David emphasizes Silver’s later statement against regulating participants’ speech; Ben counters that the initial response included apologies and that Morey deleted the tweet. They agree that cultivating independent voices and maximizing business in China can collide. Yao, by then leading the Chinese Basketball Association, offers a possible bridge in their discussion, not a promised resolution.

08

The business as discussed in 2020

Competitors who share the proceeds

The association’s rules try to make individual teams part of a stronger whole.

The hosts place annual NBA revenue at approximately $8.8 billion, still behind the NFL and Major League Baseball despite its international audience. They distinguish two sharing mechanisms. The collective bargaining agreement directs roughly half of revenue to players. A separate arrangement among teams pools and redistributes substantial basketball-related revenue, helping smaller markets compete with larger ones. These are simplified descriptions in the episode, not a complete accounting of eligibility rules, salary exceptions, or luxury-tax thresholds.

The salary cap is another attempt to balance competition and cost. Ben cites about $109 million per team in the season under discussion and calls it a soft cap because teams can exceed it within a more complex system. Stars do not simply receive whatever fraction of a team’s payroll an unconstrained market might give them. Revenue growth lifts the broader compensation pool while collective rules shape the distribution.

Television supplies the largest bucket. The national ESPN and TNT package contributes about $2.6 billion annually. Adding local rights, Ben estimates around $5 billion of the $8.8 billion total may come from television. He also identifies a little over $1 billion from licensing and merchandise and estimates $1–$2 billion in ticket sales in a normal year. The hosts are unsure how League Pass is classified. China revenue, sponsorships, and other categories discussed elsewhere may overlap these buckets. Their figures should therefore be read as a rough business portrait, not added into an audited revenue bridge.

The tension is productive but unusual. Owners compete for wins and operate separate teams; they also accept rules and revenue transfers designed to preserve opponents worth playing. Ben compares it to competing companies agreeing to share a large portion of their income. A dominant franchise needs a viable league, because a championship cannot be staged alone. The hosts describe the NBA’s precise legal identity as context-dependent rather than resolving it into a conventional single corporation.

Sponsorship turns the same attention into another product. Jersey patches, equipment, merchandise, and arena names attach brands to games and stars. Ben’s survey of arena names finds banks, airlines, insurance, telecom, and beverages repeatedly buying this association. His interpretation is that businesses selling relatively undifferentiated products want the recognition and distinction that basketball can give them.

09

The hosts’ concluding analysis

A powerful league is not a guaranteed investment

Scarce talent, global attention, and expensive expectations shape the final debate.

David separates two competitive questions. Against other basketball organizations, the NBA has an extraordinary concentration of leading talent. Ben calls professional basketball itself a cornered resource in the American market; David frames it more narrowly as control of the best players. Cities, arenas, and existing fan relationships make another ABA-style challenger difficult to build. Ben adds scale economies: an entrant must become large enough to attract talent and negotiate major contracts, yet must survive the journey to that scale.

Against other sports and entertainment, David is less certain about durable power. He proposes that player audiences may create network economies: stars win fans, young people become attached to the game, and the next generation of stars inherits a larger audience. He presents this as a possible or developing source of power, not a completed proof. International execution has created enormous value, he says, but successful expansion alone does not meet Hamilton Helmer’s definition of power. Ben and David also recognize that leagues compete for attention and the media buyers’ finite rights budgets.

Their playbook returns to content, internationalization, and technology, with the players at its center. Ben adds the value of younger fans, who may have more years of future engagement, and an ownership group he characterizes as unusually entrepreneurial. His count puts 16 of 30 teams in the hands of people from technology, venture capital, or private equity. He suggests those incentives give the commissioner more room to experiment, while admitting he cannot determine how intentional that ownership pattern was.

Instead of awarding a retrospective letter grade, they imagine future scenarios from September 2020. Their downside is a worsening break with China: the NBA could lose both audiences and future talent, and the CBA might become a serious rival. Their upside is cooperation, much larger international rights payments, and stars whose businesses and audiences grow even beyond their playing careers. These are forecasts made in the episode. This companion does not treat them as events that subsequently occurred.

They also consider pressures within the league. Ben is uncertain about individual teams’ profitability and wonders whether rising ownership costs could make weaker franchises harder to sustain. David raises a future bargaining risk: players with growing influence might seek a materially larger revenue share. Both see the existing revenue-sharing arrangements and collective bargaining agreement as protections, while leaving the next negotiation unresolved.

Ben then asks what buyers have already paid for that possibility. He cites NBA teams trading at roughly seven to fourteen times revenue, compared with lower multiples for baseball and football. Scarcity matters too: there are only 30 NBA franchises, with very few available at a time. Steve Ballmer’s $2 billion Clippers purchase illustrates a wealthy buyer’s determination to secure one of those properties and the effect of a new price benchmark on the rest.

That leads to a final qualification of their enthusiasm. A great future for basketball may already be reflected in franchise prices. Ben doubts these assets compound like technology companies; David reaches for a real-estate comparison. Their admiration for the transformation from hockey’s off-nights into global entertainment survives, but it does not settle the return available to someone buying at a multibillion-dollar valuation. The conversation ends with that uncertainty, followed by recommendations for Joe Sweeny’s Just Raised podcast and the Meow Wolf documentary and immersive art experience.
