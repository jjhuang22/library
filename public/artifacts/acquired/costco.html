Costco
The member’s bargain.

A warehouse club built an extraordinary institution by making a simple promise expensive to break: share the savings, earn the renewal.

01

1916–1954

A lawyer learns to look at the whole bargain

The story begins with Sol Price, long before the first Costco sign.

Listening cue · 6:53 ↗

Sol Price was born in New York in 1916 and grew up in a family of Jewish immigrants before moving to San Diego. He became a lawyer rather than a shopkeeper. That background matters: a lawyer advising small businesses sees leases, disputes, financing, and the gap between what a contract promises and what a business can actually deliver. Retail was a collection of arrangements to examine, not simply a familiar industry to enter. Price’s eventual innovation would rearrange who paid for distribution and who received the resulting savings.

San Diego supplied a useful setting. The growth of the military and defense economy brought customers and commercial activity into a city that was still developing. Price’s legal practice put him close to entrepreneurs. The San Diego History Center describes a merchant whose commercial experiments later became inseparable from his philanthropy. He wanted a profitable enterprise, but his conception of success also included the people working inside it and the community outside it. That perspective would survive several changes of corporate name.

The important starting point is curiosity about an existing institution. Los Angeles already had Fedco, a buying cooperative for federal employees. Members pooled demand and gained access to lower prices. A membership card could therefore serve a practical economic purpose: it collected a particular customer group and gave the institution a reason to buy on their behalf. Price did not have to invent consumers’ desire for savings. He had to find an organization that could deliver savings repeatedly and grow beyond a single location.

A conventional retailer asks how much a shopper will pay for a product. A buying organization can start with a different question: what would it cost to bring a good product to these customers if unnecessary expense were removed? Neither question eliminates the need for profit. They place profit at different points in the relationship. This companion’s interpretation is that the later Costco story becomes easier to follow once that distinction is visible.

Business lesson

Start with the customer arrangement

A membership is useful when it changes how value is produced, not merely how a customer is described.

Reference: San Diego History Center · Sol Price
02

1954–1975

The warehouse becomes a school

FedMart develops both a retail format and the people who will carry it forward.

Listening cue · 13:56 ↗

Price opened FedMart in San Diego in 1954. Its industrial setting and membership origins contrasted with the presentation of a traditional department store. Discounting was the attraction; expensive surroundings were not. The format offered a way to convert an ordinary building into a place where customers could obtain a better bargain. In an industry accustomed to polished displays and large markups, even the appearance of the store communicated a different allocation of money.

One of its young employees was Jim Sinegal, a student at San Diego City College who started bagging merchandise. He advanced into management and distribution. The California Community Colleges’ profile traces his career from that early job to the creation of Costco. His education was unusually close to the physical work of retail: goods had to arrive, fit somewhere, be available when promised, and move through a checkout. Later, a warehouse would be a carefully managed operating environment rather than an abstract symbol of cheapness.

FedMart broadened the bargain into categories such as fuel and pharmacy. An everyday service could bring shoppers back more frequently than a discretionary purchase. This creates a useful distinction between a category’s direct earnings and its contribution to the whole institution. A customer visiting for a routine need may also buy other goods, but the service still has to deserve the visit. The reinforcing relationship depends on real value, not on making an inconvenient trip seem attractive through advertising.

The apprenticeship also explains why the story is larger than one founder. A set of convictions can travel through employees who know how to implement it. Price supplied the governing idea; Sinegal accumulated the operating judgment needed to apply it repeatedly. Neither a published mission statement nor a cheap lease would reproduce those years of decisions. What eventually crossed from one company to another was a practical knowledge of how to make a low-cost promise hold up on the selling floor.

Business lesson

Train judgment where the work happens

Managers who understand receiving, replenishment, and checkout can see the cost hidden inside a merchandising decision.

Reference: California Community Colleges · Jim Sinegal
03

1975–1976

A good format loses its founder

The capital that enables expansion can also change who controls the business.

Listening cue · 34:12 ↗

FedMart’s growth did not protect Price from a consequential ownership decision. In 1975 he sold control to German retailer Hugo Mann and was subsequently removed. Contemporary histories of Price describe the rupture as the end of his leadership of the company he had created. The episode treats this setback as a turning point in the lineage that leads to Costco. It is a reminder that a founder’s influence and a founder’s ownership rights are different things.

There were legitimate reasons to seek resources. National discount chains were expanding, and a successful local formula still needed capital and execution to compete at a larger scale. But additional money arrives with a counterparty’s objectives. Agreement on the appeal of a business does not guarantee agreement on what should be done with its stores, assets, employees, or cash. The central issue is who can decide when those objectives separate.

The lesson is more precise than avoiding outside investors. Most expanding companies require partners. A founder must understand the decision rights being sold along with the shares. A board seat, a majority vote, a financing commitment, and an informal promise of autonomy are not interchangeable protections. A transaction can be economically attractive at signing and still leave the operating institution exposed to a change in direction.

Price’s response was to start again with his son Robert. The next enterprise would use some of the knowledge acquired at FedMart, but it would reduce the complexity of the store itself. A painful loss of control therefore led to a more focused experiment. That outcome was contingent: the existence of accumulated knowledge and an experienced team made a second attempt possible. It does not make the original setback beneficial or inevitable.

Business lesson

Treat control as part of the deal

Evaluate what a financing partner can change after closing, alongside what that partner contributes today.

Reference: Supermarket News · Sol Price’s retail career
04

1976–1982

The customer walks into the distribution center

Price Club discovers that consumers will accept wholesale discipline.

Listening cue · 42:49 ↗

Sol and Robert Price raised $2.5 million from friends and family and opened Price Club on July 12, 1976, in a remodeled aircraft hangar on Morena Boulevard. The initial customer was a small business. A business owner could buy supplies through a membership warehouse rather than navigate the usual wholesale channels. Costco’s historical highlights record a $750,000 first-year loss. The concept had to find enough demand to support its intentionally stripped-down operations.

The opening to consumers was crucial. Group memberships expanded the pool beyond business owners, allowing households to participate in a format designed around wholesale economics. By 1979, Price Club had two locations, 200,000 members, and a $1 million profit, according to the company’s chronology. These milestones are evidence of an operating experiment gaining traction, not proof that every future warehouse would work.

The customers accepted part of the distribution job. They traveled to the building, selected large packs, moved goods through the store, and transported their purchases home. In exchange they received a lower unit price. This is a contract expressed through the format of shopping. The warehouse’s roughness was tolerable because customers could understand what they were receiving for it. A retailer cannot assume that removing service creates value; the savings must reach the customer and matter enough to compensate for the work.

The small assortment concentrated demand. Consider an illustrative category with a fixed amount of purchasing spread among ten nearly interchangeable products. Reducing the choice to two can give each surviving product much more volume without making the whole company larger. Buying becomes more consequential, inventory becomes easier to manage, and replenishment becomes simpler. The tradeoff is equally real: some people will leave because their preferred product is absent. A disciplined warehouse accepts those lost sales when chasing them would weaken the economics for everyone else.

Business lesson

Make the tradeoff legible

Customers will do part of the work when the benefit is clear, substantial, and consistently delivered.

Reference: Costco · Historical highlights

Explanatory interlude · conceptual model

Savings travel in a circle.

1. Select fewerConcentrate purchases in the products that deserve the floor.
2. Move moreHigher volume per item supports better terms and simpler handling.
3. Share savingsLower prices make the trip and membership more valuable.
4. Earn return visitsMore useful trips reinforce demand and the next renewal.

Conceptual relationships, not measured causal coefficients. The loop weakens if quality falls, savings disappear, or the trip becomes too costly in time.

05

1982–1993

The apprentice builds a replicable institution

Jeff Brotman and Jim Sinegal pair local opportunity with operating experience.

Listening cue · 1:07:39 ↗

Jeff Brotman came from a Tacoma retail family. His father Bernie operated stores in the Pacific Northwest; Jeff studied political science and law at the University of Washington before returning to retail ventures. HistoryLink’s biography makes clear that he was not a newcomer discovering stores from a spreadsheet. He knew a region, a customer base, and a network of people who could support an ambitious new enterprise.

Brotman and Sinegal developed their plans in 1982, and the first Costco warehouse opened in Seattle in September 1983. In a later interview, Sinegal explained why the location mattered: Seattle and Portland had comparatively expensive grocery baskets, so a lower-cost entrant could demonstrate an attractive difference even without the buying scale it would eventually achieve. This was a useful wedge into the market. A young competitor needs a place where its initial economics already produce a visible advantage.

The new company expanded rapidly, opening in Portland and Spokane and going public in December 1985. Expansion tested whether the operating knowledge could be reproduced beyond the founding store. Buying well was only one requirement. A growing chain had to recruit and train managers, secure sites, maintain standards, and avoid allowing growth to outrun its ability to operate. The episode’s distinction between Price’s invention and Sinegal’s execution is useful here: originality and replication are separate kinds of achievement.

The record also includes mistakes. Costco closed its Midwest division in 1988. Sinegal later acknowledged flaws in the early presentation and explained that subsequent entries had a stronger offering, including fresh food and additional services. That admission cuts against the idea that the warehouse formula was complete at birth. A customer has to find enough reasons to make the trip. A store that is efficient for the company can still be insufficiently useful for the household.

Business lesson

Choose a market where the first version works

Enter where your initial advantage is visible, then improve the offering before assuming the format will travel unchanged.

Reference: Jim Sinegal interview · founding markets and expansion
06

1993–1997

The family reunites under pressure

A shared operating ancestry meets the reality of national competition.

Listening cue · 1:14:22 ↗

In 1993 Costco and Price Club combined as PriceCostco. Costco’s own history records 206 locations and $16 billion in annual sales at the time of the merger. Walmart’s Sam’s Club was a powerful warehouse competitor. Bringing the two related businesses together enlarged their reach and purchasing base while placing a familiar philosophy inside one organization. The episode presents competitive urgency as a material reason to act, alongside the relationship between mentor and apprentice.

A merger can destroy the features that made each company valuable. Buyers may disagree about product standards; store managers may receive incompatible instructions; employees may interpret the deal as a contest between the two sides. In this case, the shared lineage reduced one source of friction. It did not remove the work of integration. The commercial question was whether the enlarged institution would keep its discipline while gaining the benefits of greater scale.

The Price name eventually left the corporate name; the philosophy did not. The company became Costco Companies in 1997 and Costco Wholesale Corporation in 1999. Names offer a neat timeline, but the deeper continuity sits in purchasing rules, store formats, and management expectations. A company can preserve its ancestry without preserving every old label. Conversely, a familiar name cannot compensate for an operating system that has been allowed to drift.

The official Code of Ethics offers a compact description of that system: obey the law, take care of members and employees, and respect suppliers, with shareholders rewarded through adherence to those obligations. These commitments are economically connected. Poor supplier treatment can compromise availability; a poor employee experience can weaken execution; disappointing members can reduce renewal. The ordering matters when a seemingly profitable shortcut would damage one of the relationships that makes recurring profit possible.

Business lesson

Integrate around the operating promise

The reason to combine is stronger when the larger company can preserve the standards that customers and employees already trust.

Reference: Costco · Mission and Code of Ethics
07

The operating model

A fee buys the right to expect restraint

The membership business changes how management earns its return.

Listening cue · 1:18:48 ↗

Membership turns a one-off sale into a continuing bargain. A household pays before receiving most of the benefit and then evaluates the experience over a year. The company must earn the next renewal through many purchases. That arrangement can reduce the need to maximize profit on any particular item: an attractive purchase also supports the member’s willingness to stay. The fee is economically meaningful because the warehouse delivers value, not because a card has been issued.

Costco’s 2022 annual report puts the scale in perspective. Net sales were $222.7 billion, membership-fee revenue was $4.2 billion, and net income was $5.8 billion. Membership fees were therefore a small part of revenue but a large amount relative to earnings. They are not identical to net profit, and treating them as a wholly separate costless business oversimplifies a company whose warehouses make membership worthwhile. The hosts likewise challenge the claim that merchandise contributes no profit.

The episode discusses a ceiling of roughly 14% on branded-product markup and 15% on Kirkland products. Markup is measured against cost; gross margin is measured against the selling price. A hypothetical $100 item sold at $114 has a 14% markup but a gross margin of about 12.3%, before operating expenses. Confusing these measures makes the model look more profitable than it is. The strategic restraint is the refusal to take every available dollar from a transaction.

The $1.50 hot dog and soda offers a small, memorable expression of that discipline. Costco’s chronology dates its first hot dog cart to 1985 and records that price at the start. The hosts return to the combo as a symbol of the member promise. Its significance goes beyond the economics of a single meal: a familiar price makes restraint visible in a way that an inventory policy cannot.

A paid membership also selects customers. Lower unit prices still require the cash to buy a large package and the space to store it. A shopper with a car and a pantry may see a compelling bargain that is impractical for someone without either. This helps explain why a low-price retailer can attract affluent households. It also defines a limit on the model: broad appeal does not mean universal accessibility.

Executive membership deepens the relationship by returning 2% on eligible purchases. It encourages members with higher spending to concentrate more of their shopping at Costco. The economic interpretation requires care. A reward changes the effective price and costs the company money; it is attractive only if the additional loyalty and spending justify that cost. The model’s integrity depends on increasing useful value rather than making the membership so complicated that customers cannot judge the bargain.

Business lesson

Measure the relationship as well as the item

A restrained product margin can support a durable fee stream, but the fee depends on an experience worth renewing.

Reference: Costco · 2022 annual report
08

1995 onward

One name stands behind many products

Kirkland Signature makes the buyer’s judgment visible to the member.

Listening cue · 1:33:23 ↗

Costco introduced Kirkland Signature in 1995, naming it after the Washington city where the company’s headquarters were then located. The brand gathered private-label products under an identifiable promise. Costco describes its standard as matching or exceeding the leading brand’s quality while offering savings. This is a demanding proposition: a low price alone cannot persuade members to substitute an unfamiliar product for a trusted one.

The distribution setting made that promise unusually powerful. A Kirkland product does not need to win attention against an endless shelf of alternatives. The warehouse has already made a selection. Buyers have to decide which products deserve scarce space and which manufacturers can deliver them. For the member, the name is a way to borrow the institution’s judgment across categories. A successful purchase can make the next experiment feel less risky.

That transfer of trust is both an advantage and an obligation. A disappointing private-label product can undermine confidence in unrelated items carrying the same name. The brand is therefore a form of accumulated responsibility, not free permission to charge more. My interpretation is that Costco’s strength comes partly from using a shared name to make its curation accountable. The customer knows which institution made the recommendation.

International expansion tests a related question: can the bargain cross cultural boundaries? Costco opened in Britain in 1993, Korea in 1994, Taiwan in 1997, and Japan in 1999. The early overseas locations did not all become immediate successes. Sinegal’s account emphasizes patient improvement and execution. A dense market with smaller homes presents different constraints from an American suburb, but a sufficiently useful offer can still attract customers. The underlying promise travels more readily than every detail of the assortment.

Business lesson

Build a brand that accepts responsibility

A shared label compounds trust when every new product deserves the confidence earned by the previous ones.

09

The modern warehouse

Keep goods moving; keep people learning

The store’s economics begin before the item reaches the shelf.

Listening cue · 1:45:45 ↗

The warehouse remains simple to the shopper because the organization manages substantial complexity elsewhere. Cross-docking moves inbound merchandise through depots toward stores with limited storage between the two journeys. The purpose is to combine freight efficiently while avoiding unnecessary dwell time and handling. A pallet that reaches the selling floor in usable form needs less individual replenishment than many small cases scattered through a conventional shop.

Speed also changes working capital. If goods sell before the supplier must be paid, the company can receive customer cash while it still holds that payment obligation. The diagram below illustrates the mechanism with invented round numbers. The advantage comes from selling reliably and quickly, not from pretending suppliers can finance any amount of inventory forever. Slow-moving goods, payment-term changes, or interruptions in demand can reverse the relationship.

People are another part of this operating design. Costco emphasizes wages, benefits, retention, and internal advancement; the annual report identifies employees as central to the company’s culture. Higher hourly pay and lower labor expense per sales dollar can coexist when each employee helps move more volume and the company spends less time replacing and training people. The relevant comparison is the total system’s productivity, rather than the wage attached to a single shift.

Internal promotion preserves knowledge about the floor, but it also creates a tension worth watching. An institution must transmit its accumulated judgment without assuming that established methods answer every new problem. Digital commerce, international growth, and changing member expectations can require unfamiliar capabilities. Continuity is valuable when it protects the bargain; it becomes a liability if it protects a procedure after the procedure stops serving that bargain.

Business lesson

Optimize the whole cost of delivery

Inventory speed, handling, retention, and purchasing discipline work together; isolated cost cuts can make the whole operation worse.

Reference: Costco · 2022 annual report

Explanatory interlude · illustrative working capital

The sale can come before the bill.

Day 0 · receive

A warehouse receives $100 of inventory; the supplier’s invoice is due on day 30.

Day 24 · sell

A member pays $110. The retailer has customer cash before settling the invoice.

Day 30 · pay

The retailer pays $100. The $10 difference must still cover operating costs and profit.

Invented numbers to explain the mechanism, not Costco’s actual payment terms, inventory duration, or margin. Fast inventory turnover and prompt customer payment can reduce funding needs; land, buildings, equipment, and slow inventory still require capital.

10

Concluding analysis · through 2023

The hardest thing to copy is the restraint

The hosts’ closing discussion asks what defends Costco and what could weaken it.

Listening cue · 2:03:05 ↗

The hosts’ central conclusion is that Costco’s strengths reinforce one another. In Nick Sleep’s formulation, scale economies can be shared with customers: greater volume improves costs, savings improve value, and stronger value helps produce more volume. The benefit does not require every gain to be immediately reflected in a price cut. It requires members to believe that the institution uses its increasing strength on their behalf.

Seven Powers helps distinguish the mechanisms. Scale economies arise in purchasing and distribution. Brand arises from trust in the selection and price. Process power lies in many coordinated routines learned over decades. The hosts also discuss counter-positioning against delivery-centered commerce: a competitor built around bringing a huge assortment to each home cannot effortlessly copy the cost structure of a warehouse where members collect their own purchases. This does not mean either format wins every shopping occasion.

The treasure hunt supplies another reason to visit. Alongside dependable necessities, changing merchandise makes a trip interesting and gives members a reason to buy when they find something useful. Scarcity can support sales, but the buyer still has to protect quality and value. A pile of unfamiliar inventory is not automatically an appealing discovery. The warehouse needs both reliability and surprise, with the latter staying inside the institution’s standards.

Selective vertical integration can protect those standards when the supplier market no longer serves them. Costco’s Nebraska poultry complex began processing in 2019; trade reporting placed the plant, hatchery, and feed-mill investment at about $450 million. Producing part of the chicken supply gave the company more influence over the size and availability of birds used for its rotisserie offering. The interpretation is specific: ownership became useful where procurement alone could not fully secure the product promise. It also added agricultural, operational, and animal-welfare responsibilities.

The digital question is equally concrete. Costco acquired logistics company Innovel for $1 billion in 2020 to support delivery of bulky merchandise. Furniture and appliances can justify different delivery economics from a small basket of pantry goods. The episode’s discussion of e-commerce is therefore a question about fitting capabilities to the bargain. An online offering can broaden useful service, but subsidizing every kind of convenience could erode the low-cost structure members pay to access.

The strongest expansion case is more households finding the bargain useful, including overseas, coupled with existing warehouses serving members better. The vulnerabilities sit in execution: congestion that makes a trip unpleasant, local assortments that miss demand, supplier disruptions, and cost inflation that squeezes already-thin merchandise margins. Membership can absorb some volatility, but renewal is a consequence of member value. It cannot permanently insulate a deteriorating shopping experience.

Finally, a moat is not an investment valuation. An excellent institution can command a price that assumes too much future growth. The business lesson remains independent of that question. Costco is hard to reproduce because copying its visible features is easier than accepting all its tradeoffs at once: fewer products, less transaction margin, more employee investment, and a willingness to refuse revenue that would make the institution less useful. Its next generation of managers must keep earning the trust that makes those choices economically possible.

Business lesson

Protect the promise while the system evolves

The durable advantage is coordinated value creation; the risk is changing one part until the other parts stop reinforcing it.
