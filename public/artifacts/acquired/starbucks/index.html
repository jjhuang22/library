Starbucks
The place
behind the cup.

A bean shop becomes a daily ritual. Then the convenience of the ritual begins to challenge the place that created it.

Chapter navigation follows the narrative; the official transcript supplies no reliable listening timestamps. Source notes →

01

1971–1982

Before the coffee bar

A bean merchant, a salesman, and a move across the country.

Howard Schultz did not found the original Starbucks. Jerry Baldwin, Zev Siegl and Gordon Bowker opened it in Seattle in 1971, drawing inspiration from Alfred Peet’s specialty-coffee business in the Bay Area. Schultz says the first store initially sold Peet’s coffee in Starbucks bags before developing its own roasting operation. He is careful to call this the story passed down to him: he was not there. The original business sold pounds of beans and equipment, not the lattes that would later define the name.

Schultz entered the story through a Swedish housewares company, Hammarplast. Working in its American business, he noticed that a small Seattle retailer was buying an unusual quantity of its non-electric coffee makers. On a West Coast sales trip, he went to see the customer. The Pike Place store surprised him: the aroma, the employees’ knowledge, and the care devoted to coffee made a tiny shop feel like a much larger idea. Tourists bought beans, then ordered more by mail after returning home. Reputation was traveling ahead of the stores.

This was a departure from the coffee world Schultz describes growing up around: instant coffee and stale, mass-market products. Starbucks’ founders were educating customers about specialty coffee and insisting on freshness. The early mail-order business shipped small quantities because, without vacuum packaging, Baldwin was unwilling to let the coffee sit. Schultz absorbed that attention to quality even as his ambitions began exceeding the founders’ plans.

Schultz had grown up in public housing in Brooklyn, in a family under constant financial pressure. He remembers his father as an uneducated blue-collar veteran who felt devalued at work. At Xerox, Schultz made fifty physical cold calls a day, learning to sell and endure rejection. A middling performance appraisal convinced him to leave despite the prestige of the job. Hammarplast gave him broader responsibility, but the Seattle coffee shop offered something he could imagine helping build.

After developing a relationship with Baldwin, Schultz persuaded Starbucks to hire him as head of marketing. In 1982, he and Sheri drove to Seattle with their golden retriever. The company was preparing its fourth store. Its expansion dream was Portland; his was already larger. He did not yet have the business that would make that ambition possible.

02

1983–1986

A different business inside the same cup

Italy supplies the idea; Seattle supplies the test.

On a 1983 trip to a housewares trade show in Milan, Schultz encountered one espresso bar after another. He describes the experience as walking out of a black-and-white movie into color. What captured him was not simply a new way to prepare coffee. People lingered, spoke with the barista, and met each other. Coffee organized a small piece of daily social life. He returned to Seattle convinced that Starbucks should offer that experience.

Baldwin and Bowker had seen Italy too, and did not want to enter that business. After persistent lobbying, Schultz received roughly one hundred square feet inside the sixth Starbucks store, at Fourth and Spring, for a coffee-bar experiment. He worked behind the counter serving espresso, cappuccino and lattes. In his recollection, a business attracting two hundred or three hundred daily customers reached five hundred within a week. Yet Baldwin still did not want to repeat it. He wanted a coffee merchant, not a restaurant.

Schultz left to create Il Giornale. Starbucks invested, even though it would not build coffee bars itself. Raising roughly $1.6–$1.7 million required a succession of pitches and refusals. Schultz recalls that both Faema and Lavazza rejected his proposal; the hosts count 242 investor approaches and 217 refusals in the original fundraising. Seattle investors including Jack Benaroya, Herman Sarkowsky and Sam Strum eventually helped get him across the line.

Three Il Giornale stores—two in Seattle and one in Vancouver—provided evidence that the idea could travel. Schultz and coffee expert Dave Olsen worked as baristas to contain costs. Schultz drew no salary for almost two years while Sheri worked and became pregnant with their first child. When her father told him that the venture was a hobby and he needed a job, Schultz felt humiliated. Sheri insisted they continue. In his account, her conviction was essential to keeping the company alive.

The initial format copied Italy closely: Italian opera and no chairs. But even people standing at the windows began staying and returning. Schultz started describing the stores as a “third place” outside home and work. The coffee was the conduit; the relationship was the larger proposition. That interpretation would outlast many of the original Italian details.

03

1987

The name changes hands

The chance to buy Starbucks almost disappears.

Meanwhile, the original Starbucks had bought Peet’s and become financially stretched. Schultz recalls a debt-to-equity ratio above six to one. Baldwin decided to keep Peet’s, move to California, and sell the Seattle Starbucks business. He offered Schultz the opportunity for $3.8 million, with roughly ninety days to raise the money. Schultz had neither the cash nor much time.

With only part of the money committed, Schultz learned that one of his existing investors had made an all-cash competing offer. In this interview he identifies that investor as Sam Strum. This consequential story is Schultz’s recollection, including his interpretation that Strum’s advisers believed they could run the business without him. After a basketball game, Schultz told attorney Scott Greenberg what had happened. Greenberg arranged a meeting with the senior partner at his firm, Bill Gates Sr.

Gates asked whether Schultz’s account was true and complete, then took him to Strum’s office. Schultz remembers Gates leaning over the desk and saying that Schultz would acquire Starbucks and would not hear from Strum again. Gates then helped him raise the remaining money. Sheri also called her clients looking for commitments; Jack Rodgers brought in an investor group. The intervention mattered, but it still had to be followed by fundraising. Schultz emphasizes that Gates never publicly sought credit for what he had done.

The acquisition closed in August 1987. Il Giornale bought six Starbucks stores, the name and a roasting facility, then adopted the Starbucks identity. The sellers’ remaining business became Peet’s. Combined with three Il Giornale locations and two stores under construction, the new company ended the year with eleven stores and about one hundred employees.

The hosts see the old company’s leverage as a constraint that had limited experimentation. Schultz explains his own aversion to debt in more personal terms: bill collectors and financial insecurity had dominated his childhood. The business he now controlled would seek expansion without repeating that experience.

04

Late 1980s

A store that pays for the next store

Frequency and personalization reshape the restaurant comparison.

The purchase brought together sourcing, roasting and beverage retailing. Schultz estimates that the early beverage business had an eighty percent gross margin. That is a gross-margin recollection, distinct from both store operating margin and the company’s net profit margin. The hosts contrast it with the restaurant businesses that potential investors had imagined.

Frequency changed the equation further. Schultz starts to say the average customer visited eighteen times a month, then corrects himself: perhaps the most loyal customer did. An affordable daily purchase could generate more repeat visits than a conventional restaurant. The store economics also gave the company a route to funding expansion. Schultz’s stated underwriting target was annual sales twice the store investment and an operating margin above twenty percent. The hosts describe strong stores recovering their investment in roughly a year and a half to two years.

Customers began asking baristas to change their drinks. That made the beverage more personal while increasing the ticket. The company did not have to invent every variation centrally. Employees, too, developed solutions: Schultz does not know who first wrote a customer’s name on a cup, but the practice helped match orders with people as stores became busier. An operational fix became part of the sense of recognition.

Even the cup required work. Schultz rejected the standard foam cup because he disliked its effect on the coffee and the experience. International Paper helped supply a paper cup and compatible sip lid. In hindsight, he wishes he had negotiated exclusivity on the lid. Language was another ingredient: with designer Terry Heckler, the company developed short, tall and grande rather than small, medium and large. Venti arrived later.

The hosts describe a reinforcing relationship among repeat visits, customization and personal contact. The cup also traveled into the street as a visible signal of a new habit. Schultz calls the early company an experiential brand built with no money for conventional marketing. In this account, the experience and the customer carrying it supplied the introduction to the next customer.

05

1987–1992

Building the company behind the counter

Ambition needs operators—and a reason for them to stay.

Expansion tested the gap between Schultz’s vision and the systems required to execute it. Chicago, the first market beyond Seattle and Vancouver, struggled initially. Howard Behar went there, stayed through the winter, and worked on the operation. Behar joined in 1989; Orin Smith followed in 1990. The trio became known internally as H2O: two Howards and Orin.

Schultz supplied much of the ambition and merchandising energy. Behar built the operating capability: opening stores, training managers and creating the flow of service. Smith was more than a finance specialist. Schultz remembers a quiet, wise colleague who could tell both Howards when an idea exceeded the company’s resources. Regular dinners gave them a place to argue. The systems were largely manual; sophisticated technology was not the original explanation for success.

A disagreement over Southern California made the tension concrete. Behar thought the company should start with San Diego; Schultz insisted on Los Angeles and already had a location in mind. They fought, went to Los Angeles, and found a powerful new source of visibility as celebrities embraced the stores. Schultz says he was not deliberately creating an exclusive luxury brand. A CEO and a truck driver could both buy the same small indulgence.

The employee proposition drew on Schultz’s childhood. Health coverage reached part-time employees in 1988, when the hosts put the company at thirty-three stores. In 1991, before the IPO, Bean Stock extended options to eligible employees working twenty or more hours a week. Schultz says he pushed to award equity before a public offering so employees could participate in that event. The initial award was framed as fourteen percent of base pay in stock options. Calling employees “partners” followed the program.

The board initially resisted the dilution, then accepted the argument that ownership could improve performance, retention and the brand. The hosts compare this with Costco’s investment in employees, but later distinguish the mechanisms: saving on turnover matters, while Starbucks also depends on employees sustaining personal relationships with customers. In Schultz’s telling, benefits were a way to build the company he wished had existed for his father.

The June 1992 IPO supplied public capital and personal validation. Goldman Sachs declined because the company was too small; Alex. Brown led the offering. Investors needed to learn why this was a retailer with different economics rather than simply another restaurant chain. The hosts put that year’s revenue at about $93 million. Schultz remembers an offering price of $17 and a market capitalization around $250 million. The public company still had to preserve the culture that had made the growth attractive.

06

1990s

The brand travels before the stores

Beans, books, airplanes—and a cold drink Schultz resisted.

Costco cofounder Jeff Brotman had invested in the 1987 acquisition and became a director and mentor. With Jim Sinegal, he helped Schultz see that selling Starbucks beans in Costco could introduce the brand to precisely the people who might visit its coffeehouses. Internal opposition was strong. The two Costco leaders took Schultz to a parking lot and pointed to the cars: these were his customers. Schultz says stores near the Eastside Costco subsequently saw higher volume.

United Airlines provoked another revolt inside Starbucks. Schultz saw a chance to surprise people with better coffee in a place where they did not expect it. Barnes & Noble offered a different extension of the same social setting: Schultz and founder Len Riggio discussed putting coffeehouses inside bookstores. Grocery distribution returned the company to its original bean business while extending a beverage brand. The hosts’ interpretation is that these were paid opportunities to put the product and name before customers, rather than simply expenses for acquiring attention.

Expansion still proceeded market by market. Behar wanted evidence of success in one place before adding the complications of another. Mail-order demand helped identify Washington, DC as a promising city. Boston was different. Coffee Connection already held good locations and customer loyalty. Starbucks bought the business in 1994 for the hosts’ stated $23 million, gaining twenty-three stores and about $16 million in annual revenue. Schultz describes a careful transition, including seeking founder George Howell’s help.

An unexpected asset was the Frappuccino trademark. Schultz initially disliked both the name and the blended cold drink; it offended his picture of a coffee-forward company. Customers wanted it anyway. A Santa Monica store manager helped reformulate the beverage, and Southern California embraced it. The episode makes this an important reversal: the founder’s taste could not be the only definition of what Starbucks sold.

Schultz then pursued a bottled version. Coca-Cola dismissed the proposal, he recalls, while Pepsi executives Roger Enrico and Craig Weatherup saw an opportunity. Weatherup and Schultz agreed on a fifty-fifty joint venture. Demand required new manufacturing capacity, and an early glass-related recall had to be resolved. Bottled Frappuccino became another way for the experience to reach someone before a Starbucks opened nearby. The hosts put in-store Frappuccino at seven percent of revenue the year after its launch.

Schultz describes the brand’s reputation as larger than the company itself. The hosts see the cup, the shelf and the airline as versions of the same billboard. Yet inside the business, the work felt less effortless. Infrastructure was always trying to catch up with growth. Smith’s role was often to insist that the company could not do everything Schultz wanted at once.

07

1996–2000

A double tall latte in Tokyo

International promise arrives before international experience.

Schultz initially set Europe aside because its existing coffee culture seemed difficult for an American entrant. Japan looked possible, but his board questioned why Starbucks should leave so much unserved territory in the United States. Consultants then warned that high rents, a nonsmoking policy and reluctance to drink coffee while walking would undermine the concept. Schultz remained determined.

A Japanese businessman who knew Starbucks from Los Angeles wrote to the company. The relationship developed into a joint venture. Schultz admits how little international experience the team possessed; the joke that Japan was attractive because Seattle had a direct flight was not entirely a joke. The launch was a bet made before the organization felt ready.

On the hot August morning of the 1996 Ginza opening, CNN was covering the event and Schultz worried about failure. Instead, he found roughly two hundred people waiting. He remembers asking whether the partner had hired extras. A college student who had waited overnight ordered “double tall latte.” For Schultz, the phrase captured how far the idea had already traveled. He says takeaway cups were soon visible throughout Tokyo, contrary to the study’s prediction.

Japan changed what the company believed was possible. By 1997 it passed one thousand stores worldwide; Schultz says it reached its goal of two thousand by 2000 a year early. Beijing followed in 1999. China would prove much harder than Japan: the initial partnership did not work, Starbucks bought the partner out, and losses continued for almost a decade. International expansion was not one repeatable opening-day triumph.

In 2000, exhausted and conscious of what he had missed with his children, Schultz became executive chairman. Smith became CEO. The hosts put Starbucks at about 3,500 stores and $2.2 billion in revenue at that transition. Schultz trusted Smith, but Smith had said he wanted the job only for a limited time. That unresolved succession problem would matter later.

08

2007–2010

Ten more customers

The crisis becomes tangible at the scale of a single store.

Smith eventually handed the CEO role to Jim Donald. Schultz speaks warmly of Donald personally but says the fit was wrong as operating cracks met a deteriorating economy. He agreed to return in January 2008. Conversations with Michael Dell, who was navigating his own return, helped him think through the transformation before he resumed day-to-day control.

The damage was worse than Schultz expected. The hosts describe the market value falling from roughly $30 billion to below $7 billion. Stores that should not have opened had to close; Schultz recalls about a thousand closures, along with layoffs. He apologized in tears to employees. Falling comparable-store sales were new to his experience. The absence of enough cash made the crisis more serious than the continuing profits might suggest.

Schultz decided to bring about ten thousand store managers together in New Orleans. The city’s need after Katrina helped determine the location. Managers spent a day in community service, then worked through training and a renewed operating plan. At the culminating meeting, Schultz told them the company faced potential insolvency in about seven months. That is his account of the warning he delivered, not a fresh forecast reconstructed from company filings.

He also made the recovery small enough to picture. Depending on the formulation in the conversation, roughly ten or eleven incremental customers per store per day could change the comparable-sales trajectory. Managers could imagine earning those visits through better service. A company-sized financial problem became a store-sized operating task.

Starbucks also closed stores for coffee training. Schultz says earlier efficiency efforts had diluted the product, including reducing the coffee used in a batch. The hosts read this as misunderstanding the economics: saving a little on a high-gross-margin drink could weaken the loyalty on which the whole store depended. With customers under financial pressure, a diminished experience made the habit easier to abandon.

The hosts put profits at $315 million in 2008 and $945 million in 2010. Schultz attributes the recovery to people and the restoration of the experience as much as specific tactics. He offers no clean metric for the trust he thinks the company draws down when it neglects those things. His warning is that strong earnings and a rising stock price can temporarily conceal damage to the experience.

09

2009–2024

The convenience that crowds the counter

The app strengthens the economics and strains the place.

Digital leaders Adam Brotman and Stephen Gillett helped assemble the mobile-app opportunity. Schultz freely admits that he did not understand it at first. The hosts date the first app to 2009, soon after the iPhone software-development kit made that kind of product possible. The company had come a long way from manual systems and DOS terminals.

Mobile ordering and payment added convenience, encouraged customization, and gave Starbucks an unusually attractive source of working capital. Customers loaded money before spending it. The conversation places that stored value around $1.7–$1.8 billion at the time, with about $14 billion loaded annually. The hosts compare it to an interest-free customer advance. Some balances are never redeemed. They also point out that larger reloads can reduce the number of small card-payment transactions.

Those advantages are a major disagreement in emphasis. Ben and David love knowing that their order will be waiting. Schultz sees the app as the company’s biggest Achilles heel. In a Chicago store near commuter traffic, he watched many customers arrive after receiving essentially the same seven-minute promise. The result was a crowded pickup area, uncertain ownership of drinks and anxious customers. A convenient interface had become a difficult physical experience.

Schultz says the company failed to anticipate the refinements needed as mobile ordering expanded. He particularly criticizes underinvestment during the years he was away, while acknowledging that the people running the business were trying to do a good job. He would have introduced availability more slowly and learned how it affected stores. By the recording, the hosts say mobile order and pay represents roughly a third of orders. Customers have formed expectations that make limiting it painful.

For the hosts, the financial model is extraordinary. For Schultz, that is insufficient if it consumes the relationship that made the model work. He says he would sacrifice economics to improve the experience. The tension stays unresolved: customers value speed and familiarity, while the company’s founder insists that a coffeehouse cannot be understood only as a transaction-processing system.

10

2010s

Control, trust, and local judgment

China improves when Seattle gives up decisions it cannot make well.

Starbucks originally chose urban corners by counting pedestrians and studying when they passed. Office buildings and grocery-store neighbors could provide frequent visits. As the company learned, more kinds of location became viable. Dense presence could look absurd from outside—two Starbucks across an intersection—but the company was serving flows of people and repeated daily habits.

Schultz resisted conventional individual franchising because he believed a patchwork of owners’ subcultures would weaken Starbucks’ own. That does not mean every store is company-operated. At the recording, the hosts describe roughly half the estate as licensed and half company-run. International arrangements include joint ventures with different ownership splits; airports and Target provide other kinds of licensed access. Starbucks supplies roasted coffee and consistent recipes and co-designs stores, while partners run operations. Schultz acknowledges that the intended consistency is not always achieved.

China exposed the limits of Seattle’s control. A tea-drinking market did not provide the familiar morning rush. Breakfast and real-estate assumptions were wrong. Belinda Wong, who had operated in Singapore and Hong Kong, agreed to take responsibility only if she could control the local business rather than have distant teams dictate it. Schultz eventually removed Seattle from that chain of decisions, with Wong reporting through him and international leader John Culver.

Schultz gives Wong extraordinary credit for the turnaround. The hosts trace the store count from about five hundred in 2011 to three thousand in 2017 and almost seven thousand at the recording. Benefits also took a local form: Wong arranged coverage for partners’ parents and grandparents. The idea was consistent with Starbucks’ employee promise, even though its implementation differed from the American version.

The conversation returns to how those commitments accumulate. Schultz recalls covering Seattle manager Tom Carrigan after he disclosed that he had AIDS and lacked insurance. Ben says he initially found such stories frustrating as explanations of a business model, then came to understand their cumulative importance. Thousands of local decisions could become the culture, rather than mere anecdotes around a financial machine.

11

2010s–2024

Back to Milan

The largest experiences try to renew what ubiquity wears away.

The Reserve Roasteries were a deliberate answer to the danger of becoming ordinary. Schultz gathered creative colleagues at his home to watch Willy Wonka. He wanted a place where making the product was itself theater. Roasting and manufacturing would happen in view, with scale, aroma and detail designed to astonish people.

The episode names six roasteries: Seattle, Shanghai, Tokyo, Chicago, New York and Milan. Schultz does not claim a uniform financial success. Some make money and some do not. He sees their role as elevating the wider brand, against the steady erosion that can come with ubiquity. They were showplaces for the product and experience, rather than a claim that every neighborhood store should operate that way.

Milan required particular care because it was the source of his original inspiration. Schultz rejected sites until he noticed a former post office in Cordusio Square. The building’s ownership turned out to involve Blackstone. He called Jon Gray, who had already seen the Seattle roastery, and a deal followed. The roastery opened before the traditional stores; by the interview, Schultz counts about thirty ordinary Starbucks locations in Italy.

He finds special meaning in espresso becoming the leading drink there. He takes it as evidence that Italians are using the stores, while acknowledging tourists at the roastery. He also controversially argues that much Italian coffee is less good than it once was. That is his assessment in the interview. The companion does not establish it as a fact about a country’s coffee.

Asked about independent “third wave” coffee shops, Schultz praises their handcraft and argues that they expand the specialty-coffee market rather than simply take customers away. He thinks they can create intimate experiences Starbucks cannot reproduce everywhere. His claim that Starbucks created an industry is also qualified in the discussion: without him, he assumes someone would have filled the demand, probably with a more conventional franchised chain.

12

The hosts’ closing analysis

The experience is the product

A strong model remains dependent on work that is difficult to measure.

By the recording, the hosts put Starbucks at about $36 billion in annual revenue, $4.1 billion in net income, and thirty-nine thousand stores across eighty-six countries. Their initial employee count of 380,000 is corrected by Schultz to more than 450,000 globally. These are the conversation’s recording-time figures, not a later company update. That scale sets up the closing question: what makes this enormous system work?

The closing playbook does not reduce Starbucks to caffeine. Ben raises the benefits of selling a product people repeatedly want; Schultz pushes back against making addiction the explanation. David observes that caffeine and coffee are intensely competitive markets. They invoke Hamilton Helmer’s idea of power only to ask why competitors have not reproduced the full Starbucks tapestry. They do not run a formal Seven Powers scorecard.

Customization supplies part of their answer. Ben can order his particular iced almond-milk latte with whipped cream without feeling that he is asking for something inappropriate. Schultz credits the baristas with making a vast set of variations, sometimes for the first time. The app accelerates that personalization even while creating other problems. More revenue per drink and a more personal relationship can come from the same customer choice.

The work itself also matters to their interpretation. Ben suggests that being a barista carries craft and social status in a way that some other food-service jobs do not. Schultz responds with the art of making a drink and knowing customers and even their dogs. He immediately adds that it is hard work and deserves recognition. The point is not that the labor is easy; it is that skill and personal contact are part of what customers buy.

Ben values ubiquity as reassurance: a familiar order and a familiar way to obtain it anywhere he goes. Schultz calls ubiquity an enemy when it becomes the company’s defining characteristic. The two positions can coexist only if a very large network still delivers a good encounter in one specific store. The hosts connect that encounter to employee investment, lower turnover, store cash generation, visible real estate and distribution that puts products in people’s hands.

The employee proposition continues beyond the early benefits. Schultz highlights the Arizona State University partnership providing a path to four-year college tuition for partners, with thousands studying and graduating. His estimate that tenure is about twice the industry’s is explicitly a belief in the conversation, not a newly verified industry comparison. The hosts distinguish Starbucks from Costco here: continuity helps preserve relationships, not just avoid hiring costs.

The founder is both an explanation and a vulnerability. Schultz admits that dependence on his merchandising instincts may have prevented other people from developing. He should have taken Smith seriously when Smith said he did not want a long CEO tenure, and should have cultivated successors earlier. Ben sees the danger in an organization relying on a founder’s exceptional bets without developing a way to keep making them after the founder leaves. Schultz adds the twin risks of defensive management and hubris.

Finally, Schultz insists that Starbucks is several businesses at once: buying agricultural products from around thirty countries, roasting different profiles, retailing, wholesaling, managing global partnerships, and creating opportunities for hundreds of thousands of people under public-market expectations. The hosts add its quasi-financial role. None of those systems eliminates the need to earn trust at the counter. Their conclusion is a tension to manage, not proof that scale has solved the human part of the business.

13

April 2022–June 2024

The founder outside the door

The episode ends in an unfinished present.

Schultz returned as interim CEO in April 2022 after initially refusing the board’s request. In the closing discussion, he says he found several years of underinvestment and disliked using buybacks to support per-share results. He suspended buybacks and describes redirecting more than $2 billion toward partners. He regards the year as an operational repair, but says he would not stay for a second interim year.

The labor dispute is discussed from Schultz’s perspective, rather than resolved in the episode. He says he underestimated the post-COVID groundswell for unionization, acknowledges early company mistakes, and describes feeling vilified while trying to defend Starbucks. Those statements are his account. The conversation does not provide a full account from organizing workers or settle the dispute. The tension belongs alongside the company’s claims about its employee culture.

Laxman Narasimhan was selected through a board-led search, with Schultz approving the hire. By this June 2024 conversation, disappointing results had renewed questions about the company. Schultz says external pressures matter, but that Starbucks is also failing to execute at its best. His public letters were intended as counsel, he says, and he holds leadership and the board responsible for what happens next.

He is explicit about his own position: no operational role, no board seat, and no desire or intent to return as CEO. His recommendation is to be more coffee-forward and prevent mobile ordering from becoming a runaway train that degrades the experience. The phrase he leaves with the hosts is a coffee company serving people. Ben closes as he began, openly rooting for Starbucks. The recording ends with faith in the company’s people and uncertainty about what management will do next—not a later verdict on whether they succeeded.
