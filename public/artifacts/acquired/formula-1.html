Formula 1
The championship is the asset.

How racers, engineers and a formidable negotiator assembled a global spectacle—and how a new owner changed the bargain behind it.

Chapter 01 · 1950–1960s · Listen 00:05:52

An airfield, a championship, a dangerous trade

Before there was a media company, there were mechanics and men willing to climb into their work.

On May 13, 1950, Giuseppe “Nino” Farina drove an Alfa Romeo to victory at Silverstone, a former British military airfield. The first race of the new world championship assembled a sport that already had a history into a continuing contest. Farina, born into an Italian automotive family, was an experienced competitor rather than a young discovery. His teammate Juan Manuel Fangio had come from Argentina. Their machinery, their different routes to Europe, and their rivalry gave the championship something a collection of independent races could not: a story that continued after the chequered flag.

The first championship did not create motor racing, and a championship did not automatically create a coherent business. Local organisers staged events. Manufacturers and independent entrants built cars. The governing body defined the formula: the technical constraints within which those cars competed. Ferrari brought Italy’s industrial ambition and an unusually enduring identity; British constructors would build a different tradition around specialist engineering. Monaco supplied a setting where a race could become a spectacle of wealth as well as speed. Those ingredients were valuable together, but nobody initially owned all of them.

For a team, the ambition was deceptively simple: find a faster driver, build a faster car, and survive long enough to try again. Each improvement invited another. An engine that gave one constructor an advantage forced others to respond; a chassis breakthrough raised expectations of what could be achieved. Winning did not produce a patent-protected monopoly. It produced a reason for competitors to spend more. That tension runs through the episode: the competition created a product people cared about while making its producers financially fragile.

Business lesson

A shared championship can create continuity before anyone has built a durable company around it.

Chapter 02 · 1930–1970 · Listen 00:30:43

The trader enters the paddock

Bernie Ecclestone learned to negotiate long before he learned to sell a television championship.

Bernard Ecclestone was born in Suffolk in 1930 and grew up near London. He left school, worked at a gasworks, and traded motorcycles and their parts. Racing became one of his interests, but dealing became his profession. The habits mattered: understand what somebody needs, judge how much they will pay, and keep the transaction moving. A racing team might obsess over the next lap. A trader noticed the arrangements that made the next lap possible.

His involvement was personal as well as commercial. He managed Stuart Lewis-Evans, who died after sustaining burns at the 1958 Moroccan Grand Prix. Later he managed and befriended Jochen Rindt. Rindt was fast, popular, and increasingly successful in Colin Chapman’s Lotus, a team associated with ingenious designs and aggressive pursuit of performance. At Monza in September 1970, Rindt died in practice. He had accumulated enough points to become Formula 1’s only posthumous world champion. Ecclestone was among the people who encountered the immediate aftermath.

These deaths belong in the story for more than dramatic colour. They show the environment in which teams and drivers bargained: physical risk, unreliable machinery, uncertain money, and a calendar that continued after disaster. The championship’s later commercial polish should not obscure that starting point. Ecclestone’s skill would be to make fragmented participants act together. It did not arise from a clean organisational chart or a carefully designed league. It came from somebody who knew the paddock’s people, understood their dependence on each other, and recognised the value of representing them.

Business lesson

Authority is easier to build when it starts with knowledge of the participants’ actual constraints.

References: Formula 1 · Bernie Ecclestone.

Chapter 03 · 1970s–1981 · Listen 00:37:42

One negotiating table

Representation became bargaining power; bargaining power became commercial control.

Ecclestone acquired Brabham around the turn of 1971–72 and became a constructor as well as a negotiator. He recruited designer Gordon Murray and pursued success with the intensity of an owner. But ownership also gave him a seat among the independent teams. They faced organisers separately, even though an organiser’s race was valuable because a credible field would appear. Acting together could change the price and the terms of that appearance.

The Formula One Constructors’ Association, or FOCA, became the vehicle. Max Mosley supplied legal and political skill alongside Ecclestone’s negotiating energy. Their opponents were not simply recalcitrant customers. The governing authorities had their own claim to determine the sport’s rules; manufacturer-backed teams had different interests from the independent constructors. Disputes about regulation therefore became disputes about who could speak for Formula 1. Threats to race elsewhere were credible enough to make a fragmented future a real possibility.

The 1981 Concorde Agreement supplied a settlement between the teams and the authorities. Its significance was the exchange: predictable participation and an accepted governance structure made commercial commitments easier to sell. A broadcaster did not merely want the right to point a camera at a circuit. It wanted assurance that the drivers and teams audiences recognised would turn up. The political bargain helped convert a volatile sporting activity into a dependable season of programming.

There was a lasting asymmetry in the arrangement. Constructors competed with one another every weekend, but the central negotiator could accumulate relationships and commercial knowledge across the entire championship. The more consistently the participants appeared together, the more valuable it became to control the contract that assembled them. Coordination solved an immediate problem while establishing a new centre of power.

Business lesson

Reliable supply is part of the product when customers buy a recurring live event.

Chapter 04 · 1980s–1990s · Listen 00:50:33

Sell the whole season

Television turned local races into a worldwide appointment.

A car going past a grandstand entertains the people beside the track. A championship on television can entertain people who will never visit it. Ecclestone’s commercial contribution was to treat those viewers as a market for a continuing series, and to negotiate from the strength of the assembled field. Countries could receive the same contest, recognise the same stars, and follow a title fight across borders.

This changed the economics around the race. A sponsor could buy exposure that travelled beyond the home country of a driver or team. A promoter could pay for the right to host an event that brought international attention to a place. A broadcaster could use a live appointment to hold an audience. Each customer wanted something different, yet each depended on the same scarce championship. The organiser of those rights could sell several complementary claims on one sporting season.

The appeal was not evenly distributed. America already had substantial domestic motorsport and other entertainment competing for attention. A European-centred calendar could also be awkward for American viewers. Global reach therefore did not mean universal penetration. That distinction matters to the later Liberty story: the American opportunity was not the invention of a sport from nothing. It was finding a new way to introduce a mature sport to people whose existing habits gave them little reason to try it.

The television business also created a choice that never disappears. Charging more for access can improve this contract’s revenue; reaching more people can improve the next contract’s value. There is no automatic answer. A championship needs money to sustain itself, but it also needs a continuing supply of new fans. The episode’s succession from Ecclestone to Liberty is partly a succession between different judgements about that tradeoff.

Business lesson

Distribution decisions determine who has a chance to become the next customer.

Chapter 05 · 1960s–1994 · Listen 01:08:08

The laboratory and its limits

The technological contest was a source of fascination and a problem the sport had to govern.

Formula 1 is not a competition between identical vehicles. The constructors translate a rulebook into different solutions. Lotus explored aerodynamic ground effect; later teams integrated electronics and ever more complex control systems. Regulations periodically closed one route and opened another. A team that understood a new formula earlier than its rivals could gain a decisive advantage before the rest of the field caught up.

That structure gave the sport a second championship inside the first. Viewers watched a driver overtake, while engineers watched whether a car’s performance validated months of work. The fastest team needed individual talent, specialised tools, accumulated knowledge, and the ability to connect separate disciplines. The visible lap was the final output of an organisation. This helps explain why a brand could value participation even when the team itself consumed money: the competition demonstrated technical capability in public.

But an escalating performance contest needed limits more serious than a budget. At Imola in 1994, Roland Ratzenberger died during qualifying; Ayrton Senna died in the race the following day. Senna’s stature made the shock especially visible, but both losses mattered. The FIA’s subsequent safety programme intensified research into cars, circuits, protection, and medical response. The HANS device later became mandatory, and further protections followed. Safety was a continuing engineering task rather than a single change that made racing harmless.

The commercial reading here should remain modest. Fatalities were human tragedies first. They also demonstrated why a valuable championship could not allow its participants’ competitive incentives to determine every limit. A team was paid and celebrated for finding speed. The governing system had to defend conditions under which drivers could continue to compete. The institution’s responsibility extended beyond whatever one competitor might accept in pursuit of victory.

Business lesson

A system needs rules for harms that its individual competitors cannot be expected to price adequately.

References: Formula 1 · FIA.

Chapter 06 · 1990s–2009 · Listen 01:33:18

Selling equity, retaining the wheel

The economic ownership changed more quickly than the sport’s method of administration.

As Formula 1’s commercial rights became a financial asset, Ecclestone reduced his ownership through a succession of transactions. The corporate story ran through holding companies, media investors, creditors, and finally private equity. EM.TV and then Kirch became part of that chain; Kirch’s collapse left banks holding a substantial interest. CVC gained control in 2006. Owning shares and operating the championship remained distinct activities.

The essential asset was not a warehouse of racing cars. It was the ability to commercialise a recurring world championship through long-term relationships and enforceable rights. Those rights could be financed, acquired, and valued separately from a constructor’s machinery. They also made the central business less exposed to a single team’s sporting failure: when one car slowed, somebody else could become the champion.

The arrangement did not make the teams content. Constructors created the spectacle, paid for its most expensive technical ingredients, and disputed how its rewards should be divided. The Formula One Teams’ Association, FOTA, threatened a breakaway in 2009. Its existence revealed a weakness in any simple monopoly explanation. The rights holder depended on the continued cooperation of people capable of producing an alternative competition. A title and a contract were valuable; so were Ferrari, the leading drivers, and the teams’ willingness to participate.

Breaking away would require more than signing a statement. A rival series would need rules, circuits, sponsors, television distribution, logistics, and agreement among organisations accustomed to trying to defeat one another. Those coordination costs favoured the incumbent. Yet they did not erase the teams’ leverage. Formula 1’s commercial stability came from periodically renegotiating the bargain, not from permanently resolving the conflict between the producers of the sport and the seller of its rights.

Business lesson

Control over distribution remains valuable only while the indispensable suppliers keep participating.

Chapter 07 · 2004–2016 · Listen 02:05:07

An energy drink, a rescued team, a new dynasty

Team ownership could serve a brand, an industrial ambition, and a championship dream at once.

Dietrich Mateschitz had built Red Bull around an association with energy, risk, and extraordinary performance. After sponsoring teams and drivers, he bought Jaguar’s team from Ford in late 2004. Red Bull Racing entered the next season under its new name. To somebody thinking only in terms of car manufacturing, a drinks company’s ambition looked unusual. To somebody thinking about attention, owning the team created a powerful stage for the brand.

The team did not succeed merely by changing its logo. Christian Horner led the organisation; Adrian Newey brought exceptional design experience. Recruiting a recognised engineer made a statement about seriousness and supplied capabilities that branding could not substitute for. Red Bull’s eventual championships demonstrated a broader point: a non-automotive owner could compete if it assembled the people and tools necessary to build a first-rate racing organisation.

A different sequence began when Honda withdrew after 2008. Ross Brawn and colleagues preserved the team, using a car developed for the new regulations and a Mercedes engine. Its double diffuser exploited the rulebook’s opportunities; Jenson Button won six of the first seven races in 2009 and took the drivers’ title. Brawn GP also won the constructors’ championship. The unexpected success did not mean the financial crisis had been imaginary. It meant an organisation whose funding had disappeared still contained valuable work and talent.

Mercedes acquired the team and returned with a works entry for 2010. Toto Wolff joined in 2013 as executive director and managing partner; Lewis Hamilton arrived that same season. When the hybrid power-unit rules began in 2014, Mercedes was prepared to dominate. An inherited organisation, an engine operation, and new leadership converged around a regulatory reset. The same championship that punished slow adaptation could reward years of preparation in one dramatic season.

Business lesson

Buying an entry supplies a platform; building the capabilities to exploit the next rules supplies the advantage.

References: Formula 1 · Formula 1 · Formula 1 · Mercedes.

Chapter 08 · 2017–2021 · Listen 02:42:33

A media company sees unfinished work

Liberty bought an established global property and changed what its management tried to optimise.

Liberty Media completed its acquisition on January 23, 2017. The transaction valued Formula 1 at about $8 billion including its debt, versus approximately $4.4 billion for the equity. That distinction matters: the price of the operating enterprise was not the cash value of its shares alone. Chase Carey became chairman and chief executive. Sean Bratches brought commercial experience from ESPN, while Ross Brawn took responsibility for the sporting side. The new organisation combined television, sales, and racing expertise.

Liberty inherited a sport with passionate followers and formidable barriers to imitation. It also inherited opportunities to make the experience easier to discover. Digital clips, access to personalities, and a more deliberate commercial organisation could widen the audience before asking every new viewer to pay for a live broadcast. Professionalising the business meant treating discovery, engagement, and monetisation as connected tasks rather than leaving them entirely to whichever broadcaster had purchased the territory.

The teams’ finances required attention too. A rising television cheque could disappear into the next development race. The financial regulations introduced a cost cap in 2021, with a baseline of $145 million for covered costs; the original schedule reduced that baseline in later years. Driver compensation, marketing, and certain senior salaries were excluded. It was a limit on a defined category of spending, not the total price of operating a team.

The pandemic made a difficult negotiation urgent. A team that lost events and income still had employees and factories to support. Sharing more revenue helped, but changing how much of the next dollar competitors could spend chasing each other helped differently. The route to profitable teams involved both a larger audience and a constraint on the competition to consume that audience’s money. The cap could not guarantee close racing. It could improve the chance that participation became economically sustainable.

Business lesson

A bigger revenue pool and a limit on the race to spend it solve different parts of the same problem.

Chapter 09 · 2019–2025 · Listen 03:05:03

Let viewers meet the people

The entry point became a character and a predicament before it became a technical rule.

Drive to Survive launched on Netflix on March 8, 2019, following the previous racing season. Its production gave viewers a way into the garage, the contract negotiations, and the pressures surrounding a place on the grid. A novice did not need to understand aerodynamic balance to understand a person whose job might disappear. A championship that looked forbiddingly technical became legible through familiar human stakes.

Ferrari and Mercedes did not participate in the first season; both joined for the second. That early absence had a useful consequence. The series could spend time with people farther down the standings, whose struggles were important even when they were not competing for the championship. A viewer could develop an attachment to a midfield driver and then have a reason to watch a race in which the leader seemed predictable. The live contest now contained several stories at once.

Netflix did not create those incentives or rivalries. It edited them into a format with its own conventions, which could sharpen drama and provoke criticism about authenticity. The business benefit therefore came with an editorial tension. The sport wanted accessible storytelling, while participants and experienced fans wanted an accurate representation of what had happened. A durable introduction needed to encourage curiosity about the actual racing rather than substitute permanently for it.

The important mechanism was complementarity. Watching a documentary could make an unfamiliar race meaningful; watching the race could make the next documentary season more interesting. Clips and social media could carry that interest between events. No single format had to teach every rule or satisfy every fan. Together, the formats lowered the effort required to become involved, then supplied more depth to people who wanted it. This is an interpretation of the growth mechanism, not a claim that Netflix alone caused every increase in demand.

Business lesson

An accessible story can reduce the learning cost of a complex product without removing its depth.

Chapter 10 · 2025–episode release · Listen 03:26:45

Attention becomes a contract

America illustrates the final step from discovery to monetisation.

By 2025, Formula 1 had three American races, at Austin, Miami, and Las Vegas. The sport’s official year-end review reported a worldwide fanbase of 827 million and a US fanbase of 52 million. These are estimates of interested people, not counts of paying customers or the audience for one broadcast. They describe the scale of potential demand; they do not by themselves describe the revenue earned from it.

F1 The Movie added another introduction. The official review reported more than $630 million in global box office, a cinema measure rather than Formula 1’s revenue or profit. The distinction prevents a popular film from becoming an imaginary income statement for the racing business. Its broader strategic role was exposure: a fictional story could give people another reason to recognise the sport, its teams, and its settings.

In October 2025, Apple and Formula 1 announced an exclusive five-year US broadcast partnership beginning in 2026. The agreement connected a major technology distributor with a sport whose American audience had become much more commercially interesting. At the episode’s release, this was an announced distribution change, not a completed experiment with a fully observed outcome.

A streaming partner could provide reach, product investment, and a new context for live coverage. It could also introduce subscription friction. The analytical question is what happens to the entire relationship: how many people discover the sport, how many watch regularly, and what the broadcaster is willing to pay when renewal comes around. Extracting value from an expanding audience is a success. Maintaining the channels through which the audience keeps expanding is the next challenge.

Business lesson

Treat a distribution contract as both current income and an investment in the next generation of fans.

References: Formula 1 · Apple.

Chapter 11 · As of March 2, 2026 · Listen 03:41:52

The championship is the asset

The league and its teams sell related products with different cost structures.

Formula 1’s central business earns its primary revenue from media rights, race promotion, and sponsorship. Broadcasters buy programming; promoters buy the right to stage a race; commercial partners buy association and visibility. Hospitality and other activities add further income. The teams have their own sponsorship relationships, receive distributions from the central business, and bear the cost of producing competitive cars. A successful team is not the same business as a successful rights holder.

Liberty’s results published just before the episode put 2025 Formula 1 revenue at approximately $3.9 billion, operating income at $632 million, and adjusted OIBDA at $946 million. OIBDA is a company-defined operating measure before depreciation and amortisation; it is not interchangeable with net income or cash flow. The numbers show a substantial commercial operation, but a comparison with team values needs care: an ownership valuation is a price for expected future economics, not a year’s revenue.

The central operation can benefit when sponsors, broadcasters, and hosts value the same additional fan. The fan’s attention need not be sold only once. But it is not costless or limitless. Race calendars constrain how much live inventory can be produced. More races mean more travel and strain on staff, while each venue must maintain a worthwhile experience. Global transport is handled through specialist providers; Acquired specifically corrected the episode’s claim that Formula 1 itself supplied all the logistics, identifying Rock-It’s role.

The underlying commercial proposition is a coordinated, scarce, recurring event. Its economics improve when it can charge several customers for complementary benefits while keeping the producers healthy enough to return. That last condition is what makes the cost cap, revenue distribution, and periodic Concorde negotiations part of the business model rather than administrative footnotes.

Business lesson

Separate the rights holder’s economics from the supplier teams’ economics before judging the whole ecosystem.

Chapter 12 · Concluding analysis · Listen 03:56:23

Why the system worked

The asset is a championship that its participants and audience keep choosing.

The hosts close by asking why Formula 1 worked and how necessary Ecclestone was. Their question is more useful than a simple verdict on his personality. The historical problem was coordination: teams, organisers, regulators, and broadcasters needed a reliable arrangement before a global commercial product could emerge. His negotiating authority helped establish that arrangement. The later opportunity was to broaden demand and improve the economics of the participants. The same centralisation that made the first task possible could constrain the second when too much judgement depended on one person.

In Seven Powers terms, branding is a strong lens. Formula 1’s accumulated history, famous teams, championship records, and iconic venues help make it a distinct purchase. A rival can build fast cars and stage races; it cannot instantly acquire the meaning of a Formula 1 title. Long-duration commercial rights are another structural advantage. The contracts matter, though they should not be confused with unconditional ownership of the public’s attention.

Network effects require more care. More fans can attract broadcasters, sponsors, and promoters, whose investment can improve reach and presentation. That is a reinforcing commercial system. It does not automatically imply that every viewer makes the sport better for every other viewer in the way a communications network works. The clearer advantage is coordination around the established championship: the participants each have good reasons to be where the other important participants already are.

What is hard to reproduce is the whole assembly. A racing organisation requires specialised people and facilities; a global calendar requires viable promoters; a live media product requires reliable production and distribution; a culturally important championship requires memory. Buying any one piece does not buy the system. Breakaway threats show why the participants’ consent remains essential, while the difficulty of coordinating a breakaway shows why the incumbent is resilient.

The bull case builds on this assembly. More people can discover a technical sport through accessible stories; larger audiences can support stronger contracts; financially healthier teams can attract owners who invest for years rather than scramble to fund the next season. Regulation resets can refresh the sporting contest, and different distribution formats can reach fans with different levels of commitment. These are possible reinforcing effects, not a promise that each will continue at its recent pace.

The bear case starts with the same dependencies. Sporting dominance can weaken suspense; expensive access can slow discovery; a crowded calendar can dilute attention and exhaust participants. Promoters must earn enough value to keep paying, and sponsors must keep believing in the association. Political controversy around venues, safety failures, or disputes over rules can damage trust. The central business has pricing power, but using it carelessly can weaken the supply and audience on which that power rests.

The episode’s concluding argument can be understood as a shift in what the business protects. Ecclestone made a fragmented championship commercially negotiable. Liberty’s opportunity was to make that established championship easier to follow and more sustainable to produce. The enduring lesson is specific: valuable entertainment needs both a convincing story for the audience and a workable bargain for the people who create it. Neither side can be treated as an inexhaustible resource.
