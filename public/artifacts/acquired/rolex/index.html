Rolex

The architecture of permanence

How a Bavarian watch merchant made an everyday instrument into a promise that could last a lifetime.

Based on Acquired · Rolex, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal

Released February 24, 2025Created October 12, 2026About 20 minutes to readScope Founder, history, operations and concluding business analysis
01

1881–1905 · Listen near 5:52

An outsider learns the trade

Before the crown, there was correspondence.

Hans Wilsdorf’s story begins in Bavaria in 1881. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal describe a Protestant boy who did not fit neatly into the national and religious identities around him. At twelve, he lost both parents within a few months. His uncles sold the family ironmongering business and used the proceeds to send Hans and his siblings to boarding school. Looking back in the autobiography the hosts discuss, he credited that early experience with teaching him to look after himself and his possessions.

At school, mathematics and languages came easily to him, especially English. A Swiss schoolfriend helped draw his attention toward Switzerland. After school he worked in the pearl trade, then joined the watch exporter Cuno Korten. His job was correspondence: answering customers, coordinating orders and communicating with British buyers. Those buyers were jewelers and retailers, rather than the people who eventually wore the watches.

The hosts linger on this position because it exposed him to both sides of the business. He could see Swiss producers, their components and their prices, while developing relationships with the British distribution network. He also became interested in the objects themselves. He took batches of pocket watches home, set them together and compared their accuracy the next morning.

In the story David recounts, Wilsdorf selected three accurate watches and submitted them to an astronomical observatory for chronometer testing. When they passed, his employer recognized that certification could help sell the product. Timekeeping was a necessity in this period, not merely a fashion choice. Ben and David compare the pocket watch’s place in everyday life to the smartphone: people relied on it, carried it everywhere and even reached for it in awkward moments.

Their contrast with other luxury histories is deliberate. Rolex did not begin as an inheritance from aristocratic life. Wilsdorf was a merchant learning a modern, technical industry. The hosts present his grasp of supply, distribution and accuracy as the preparation for the company he would build.

1905–1919 · Listen near 18:52

Give the invisible maker a name

A merchant becomes a brand owner.

Wilsdorf moved to London in 1903, aged twenty-two, and worked for a watch company whose name the hosts say they could not identify. He admired its commercial competence but thought it lacked specialization. In 1905 he decided to start his own importing business. The immediate obstacle was financing: before selling watches, he had to pay for an order. A lawyer introduced him to Alfred Davis, who supplied capital, and Wilsdorf and Davis went into business together.

Their customers were still retailers. A buyer entering a jeweler’s shop would see the jeweler’s name on the dial, not the identity of the importer or movement maker. Wilsdorf assembled a product from Swiss movements and cases, using the relationships he had learned upstream. The hosts stress that he was coordinating the product rather than manufacturing its most difficult component himself.

That component came from Aegler in Bienne. Aegler could make small movements with unusually good accuracy. At first, Wilsdorf’s interest was differentiation through specialty products, including traveling watches. The wristwatch conviction followed. According to the story the hosts tell, an account of soldiers using wristwatches during the Boer War made him see the wider appeal of reading the time without putting down what one was holding or reaching into a vest.

He placed an enormous order for small movements. Ben later notes that the order amounted to roughly five times the capital in the business. They acknowledge uncertainty about the financing and suggest credit and a multiyear order may have been involved. The market did not immediately arrive as Wilsdorf expected. Soldiers returned to a world where wristwatches were still often treated as wristlets. The company was small enough to serve the emerging market in warmer parts of the British Empire while waiting for wider adoption.

Wilsdorf wanted a product name customers would request. Inspired by Kodak, he chose the short, pronounceable Rolex. He returned to certification: a Swiss wristwatch chronometer rating in 1910 and the more demanding Kew certificate in 1914. World War I then accelerated demand while making a business carrying a German founder’s name difficult in Britain. The company became Rolex; import duties and a ban on importing precious metals pushed assembly to Switzerland, and the headquarters eventually moved to Geneva.

1926–1927 · Listen near 59:27

Put the promise in the water

The Oyster turns reliability into a public event.

The postwar customer could now imagine wearing a wristwatch, but the product remained fragile. A watch on the wrist faced dust, humidity and water that a pocket watch encountered less directly. David describes his realization that waterproofness was not primarily a boast about diving to an impressive depth. It was a way to keep the elements from ruining a delicate movement.

Early hermetic watches solved this by enclosing a watch inside another case. The hosts compare the outer shell to a diving helmet. It was bulky, and the owner had to open it every day to wind the watch. The crown remained the awkward point where daily use and protection met. Rolex tried to improve the seal, without finding a satisfactory solution.

In October 1925, two Swiss casemakers filed a patent for a moisture-proof winding arrangement. Wilsdorf, who followed the patent register closely, bought it and made it exclusive to Rolex. The hosts emphasize the commercial act rather than crediting him with inventing the underlying idea. He saw an essential piece of the product, secured it and organized its use. A screw-down crown and threaded case produced the Oyster, whose name Rolex registered in 1926.

He prepared to spend heavily on advertising, but wanted an event that would make the product visible. Mercedes Gleitze, a London secretary and endurance swimmer, had successfully crossed the English Channel in October 1927. After another swimmer’s false claim cast doubt over the achievement, she undertook a vindication swim. Wilsdorf arranged for her to carry an Oyster on a small chain around her neck.

Ben and David carefully separate the legend from what happened. Gleitze did not finish the later attempt, and the watch was not on her wrist. It nevertheless endured around ten hours in the water. Wilsdorf purchased the Daily Mail’s front page to announce the Oyster. The hosts see the origins of Rolex’s long association with people whose achievements could express the product’s promise. They also point out that women remained important to the business: the early catalog contained more women’s models than men’s, despite the male focus of much contemporary watch enthusiasm.

1931–1945 · Listen near 1:31:36

Close the loop around the crown

An automatic movement improves the whole system.

The Oyster created a new behavioral problem. To wind the watch, the owner unscrewed the crown, exposing the opening the seal was meant to protect. Forgetting to screw it down again defeated the system. For Ben and David, this is why self-winding mattered so much: it completed the protected wristwatch, rather than adding a clever feature unrelated to its existing weaknesses.

Ben explains the mechanism beneath the dial. A mainspring stores energy in a barrel; an escapement regulates its release; a balance and hairspring provide the oscillation that lets the gears advance at regular intervals. Tiny components operate with demanding tolerances and very little friction. Automatic winding had to capture the wearer’s movement and use it to tighten a spring already under resistance.

John Harwood had developed a hammer-style automatic system, but it eliminated manual winding. If an unworn watch stopped, restarting it was awkward. The hosts also describe the breadth of Harwood’s British patent as an obstacle to Rolex. After his company failed in 1929, Rolex developed its rotor arrangement. A weighted half-disc could turn freely rather than strike the ends of a hammer mechanism; ordinary wrist movement wound the mainspring, and the operation was quiet.

The resulting Oyster Perpetual Chronometer brought together the three elements the episode keeps returning to: precision, protection from the elements and automatic winding. Early examples acquired the collector nickname bubble backs because the added mechanism made the rear of the case protrude. The hosts describe the full system’s launch in 1934 and the emergence of Rolex’s steel-and-gold Rolesor look.

In 1936, Rolex secured Aegler’s production rather than continuing to share it with Gruen. Another war then disrupted access to its British market. The episode’s Panerai sidebar follows Rolex making watches for an Italian retail partner with a naval diving contract. Exports to Britain were later routed through Spain and Portugal. The hosts connect the war’s aviation, navigation and scientific requirements with the professional applications that would shape the next generation of watches.

05

1945–1960 · Listen near 1:47:33

A company built to outlive its founder

An ownership decision changes the horizon.

After World War II, Europe was devastated while the United States offered returning servicemen, disposable income and a growing market. Rolex needed a way to become meaningful to American buyers. For its fortieth anniversary in 1945 it introduced the Datejust and Jubilee bracelet, products Ben and David describe as the first recognizable modern Rolex combination.

Wilsdorf then used milestone chronometers as gifts. The fiftieth-thousandth went to the Swiss general Henri Guisan; the hundred-thousandth went to Winston Churchill through Guisan; the hundred-and-fiftieth-thousandth went to Dwight Eisenhower. The hosts present this as a carefully connected sequence, with each relationship preparing the next. When Eisenhower became president, his Rolex appeared on the wrist of one of the most visible figures in the American market.

André Heiniger helped turn that recognition into a wider campaign. The episode describes him joining Rolex after the war, working in South America and returning to Geneva to lead marketing. He brought in J. Walter Thompson. The advertising associated Rolex with people directing the world’s affairs, while Heiniger’s later work would connect it to professional accomplishment and sport.

The Datejust also brings the hosts back to women customers and to Wilsdorf’s second wife, Betty. They recount the story that her difficulty reading the date inspired the Cyclops magnifier, but openly treat the tale of a water droplet supplying the idea as corporate mythology rather than established evidence.

Wilsdorf had no children to inherit the company. The hosts describe him establishing the Hans Wilsdorf Foundation after his first wife died and transferring his ownership into it. They return to that arrangement throughout the episode: Rolex could keep its independence and think beyond the next reporting period. Later, they discuss the foundation’s charitable work and support for people in Geneva. Their estimates of the company’s resources remain estimates; ownership does not make private financial information public.

06

1953–1967 · Listen near 1:58:49

A watch for the person you might become

Tools acquire identities beyond their original jobs.

By the 1950s, the hosts argue, other Swiss manufacturers could produce watches technically comparable to Rolex. Heiniger needed differentiation beyond the mechanism. The professional-watch families connected a watch to what wearing it said about a person. The tool was still real, but so was the identity around it.

The episode follows several attempts. The Turnograph’s rotating bezel was promoted for timing international telephone calls, a proposition Ben and David find less romantic than the applications that followed. The Milgauss addressed strong magnetic fields and acquired an identity tied to scientists. The Explorer connected Rolex with mountain exploration. The Submariner connected it with diving, and the GMT-Master with the expanding world of international aviation.

The Everest account requires the official episode’s published correction. Rolex sent watches on the expedition, but Smiths was the official outfitter. Acquired says Rolex’s claim that its watch was worn at the summit was wrong and was followed by an apology. Rolex’s fast publicity nevertheless established the association. The corrected account belongs here alongside the recorded story, rather than leaving the original implication intact.

The Submariner’s story moves through diving culture and the appearance of a Rolex on Sean Connery’s James Bond. Ben and David explore how technical competence, adventure and popular culture became connected in a single recognizable object. The official corrections also revise their original Bond claim: the character wore several other watch brands before Omega, so this was not an uninterrupted Rolex history.

The GMT-Master gave pilots a way to track another time zone. Across these families, the hosts see a common shift from advertising the product alone toward advertising the life of its wearer. A diver, pilot or explorer supplied both a testing environment and an aspirational picture. Rolex’s partnerships were intended to endure, rather than function as short promotional arrangements. This is the lifestyle phase of the story that sets up the later debate about luxury.

1963–1988 · Listen near 2:29:57

The slow arrival of an icon

Collectors can change the meaning of an old product.

Rolex’s chronograph became the Daytona through its connection to motor racing. The episode explains its outer speed scale: time a known distance with the chronograph, and the position of the seconds hand can show the corresponding speed. Yet the current cultural status of the watch did not arrive immediately with its racing identity.

Paul Newman’s involvement supplied a particularly memorable story. His wife Joanne gave him a Daytona with an affectionate driving inscription. He wore it for years. In the hosts’ account, he then gave it to his daughter’s boyfriend in 1984 after asking the time and discovering the young man had no watch. They contrast that casual gift with the later auction price, which they recall as around $17.5 million.

The collector boom came later. In 1986, Italian dealers began buying the distinctive dial versions that acquired the Paul Newman name. The hosts mention a rumored magazine cover as one possible spark, but say nobody had located it. They do not claim to know why that moment occurred. What they do describe is a country with an existing collecting and style culture giving these once-unremarkable variants a new significance.

The episode then returns to the Rolex advertising of the period. Its lifestyle campaign imagined the person a customer might become, rather than explaining only how the watch worked. Ben and David see the Daytona’s collector following as a major step in the move toward luxury: one model acquired sustained hype and became difficult to buy even when other Rolex families remained available from a dealer’s case.

They also resist treating allocation as one universal company policy. Rolex sold through retailers, whose practices varied. One host repeats the distinction enthusiasts make between a Daytona waitlist and a wish list; the other reminds him that the experience depends on the dealer. The discussion leaves that local variation visible. The late collector market was important, but it was not a simple mechanism that Rolex had planned from the beginning.

1969–1990s · Listen near 2:45:56

When keeping time becomes cheap

The category changes beneath the craft.

Ben’s industry history interrupts the company chronology because the quartz revolution changes what a watch business can be. Quartz replaces the mainspring’s energy storage with a battery and uses an electronic oscillator and integrated circuit to keep time. The technical lineage in the episode runs from the room-sized quartz clock at Bell Labs to the miniaturized electronics needed for a wristwatch.

The Swiss industry had explored quartz too. Seiko’s Astron reached the market in 1969, initially at a very high price and without a dramatic accuracy advantage. Ben argues that its trajectory should have been understood as an electronics learning curve rather than an artisan manufacturing story. Fewer parts, much lower assembly labor and large upfront machinery investments would eventually make accurate timekeeping cheap.

The episode follows American electronic-watch entrants, the shift from LED to LCD displays, and the increasing strength of Japanese production. Swiss workshops and brands faced collapsing demand. The danger was not simply missing a technology. Their fragmented production system and the price structure of mechanical watchmaking were exposed to a fundamentally different cost model.

Ben contrasts responses. Omega’s compromises damaged the coherence of its offer, in his telling. Jean-Claude Biver’s revived Blancpain emphasized mechanical exclusivity from a much smaller starting point. Auction houses helped make surviving mechanical watches desirable through rarity, while elaborate new complications attracted attention. Rolex took a different route: it explored quartz and produced the Oysterquartz, but kept its emphasis on mechanical construction, engineering and its established product families.

André Heiniger’s reasoning, as the hosts recount it, was that electronics would become commonplace while high-quality mechanical construction would remain costly and distinctive. The recorded episode sometimes describes this as the turn toward luxury. Acquired’s official correction qualifies the timing: Rolex had already introduced the gold-only Cellini and a solid-gold Submariner before the crisis. The transition was underway; quartz changed the environment in which that positioning would prove valuable.

Build the institution behind the promise

Scale becomes useful when it protects consistency.

Patrick Heiniger took over from his father in 1992. Ben and David describe his major contribution as vertical integration: acquiring Aegler in 2004 and bringing a scattered production system into four large sites. They connect that consolidation to uniform quality. A globally recognizable luxury product could not have regional versions whose finish or reliability differed in ways customers would discover when comparing them.

The episode’s factory discussion combines human skill with industrial scale. Rolex bought suppliers, developed materials capabilities and invested in manufacturing processes. The hosts reject the idea that high volume must mean poor engineering. Their argument is the reverse: volume supports extraordinary expenditure on construction and testing, provided the company keeps directing the resources toward the product.

The 2008 financial crisis gives them another example of the ownership structure at work. Other brands cut prices and advertising. Rolex maintained prices and sponsorships and increased American marketing while a local enthusiast market was developing. After demand rebounded, the hosts say it also avoided the opposite whiplash of aggressive price increases. They distinguish modest ongoing price increases from abruptly extracting the maximum a hot market might bear.

Scarcity nevertheless creates a problem. They discuss frustrated customers, opaque lists and the gap between Rolex’s control of the product and retailers’ control of the last customer interaction. Buying Bucherer raised the possibility of more direct retail control, but Ben and David do not settle on a single explanation. Better margins, customer information and allocation control could help; they also argue Rolex already possessed considerable leverage over its dealers. The conversation explores motives rather than presenting a revealed internal strategy.

Tudor complicates the brand story too. The hosts debate whether it is a lower-priced entry point, a competitor in another segment, or a place to try ideas while protecting Rolex’s position. They favor the innovation role, but their original argument partly relied on an incorrect production estimate. Acquired’s corrections cite an estimated Tudor output closer to twenty percent of Rolex’s, rather than below one percent. They also clarify that Tudor’s movements have been considered in-house since 2015: Kenissi manufactures them, and the Hans Wilsdorf Foundation is a large Kenissi owner. Those corrections limit how confidently the original scale argument can be repeated.

When the hosts finally size Rolex, they emphasize secrecy. They estimate annual production around 1.1–1.2 million watches and sales around $11 billion, drawing on estimates discussed in the episode. The approximately $13,000 average selling price is a retail price, not Rolex’s own wholesale receipt. They cite roughly 30% of Swiss watch revenue and speculate about still higher profit share, but none of those estimates becomes an audited company disclosure in this companion.

The hosts’ concluding analysis · Discussion near 4:27

Brand, continuity and a different job

Ben and David’s concluding discussion starts with disagreement about the word luxury. One views Rolex as an industrial, consistent and exceptionally engineered product produced at a much larger scale than the most exclusive watchmakers. The other emphasizes how it manages the market: it protects the brand, controls supply and declines to charge everything a momentarily enthusiastic buyer might pay. They agree on the underlying position even while describing it differently.

Their numerical image is a price-and-quantity curve. Rolex combines a large number of units with a high price, rather than an enormous price with a tiny production run. It is expensive and inaccessible to most people, but can be a meaningful, almost unreasonable milestone purchase for a substantial professional class. That breadth helps explain the hosts’ estimate of around 30% of Swiss watch revenue. They compare its engineering and stable product families with Porsche, while distinguishing the much smaller, more expensive collector market of companies such as Patek Philippe.

The discussion of secondary trading adds another layer. Small differences among references create rarity for enthusiasts without requiring the basic families to change continuously. An exceptional collectible can operate in a different price world from a new mainstream model. The hosts explicitly recognize that Rolex does not directly collect all the money circulating in that resale market. Its influence on prestige is different from its contribution to company revenue.

Their financial discussion is deliberately speculative. They guess that operating margins could be closer to 40% than 30%, discuss the possibility of a very large accumulated cash balance and compare a hypothetical valuation with Hermès. They offer a broad range of possible enterprise values rather than a dependable price. These are the hosts’ estimates and valuation thought experiments. Rolex’s private accounts are not available, and comparing it with a traded company does not turn the guesses into measurements.

In Seven Powers, they identify branding as the dominant present-day explanation. Scale economies also matter: the company can spend on engineering and testing at a level competitors may struggle to match. They consider whether the earlier professional-watch strategy was counter-positioning and decide that rival Swiss companies were not structurally prevented from pursuing it. The coherence and execution were exceptional; the strategy was not automatically an example of that particular power.

The playbook conversation asks when Rolex actually took a major risk. The huge early Aegler order stands out. Later decisions, such as remaining patient during quartz disruption or advertising through 2008, could look daring from outside but were more manageable because Rolex had cash, independence and an appropriate structure. Ben and David’s answer centers on continuity: strategy, positioning, product families and culture were maintained for unusually long periods, allowing good execution to accumulate.

They extend that idea to the owner’s feelings about an old watch. Slow iteration means a purchase from decades earlier still resembles what Rolex sells today. The customer can continue to feel that the original decision was good. The hosts contrast that experience with technologies whose rapid replacement can make an earlier purchase feel obsolete. Their discussion of long employee tenure connects continuity with incentives, secrecy and ongoing technical work inside the company.

Smartwatches produce a more complicated argument than another round of direct timekeeping competition. An Apple Watch and a Rolex compete for wrist space while doing different jobs. The hosts suggest that smartwatches introduced some people to wearing and spending money on a wrist device, even as they damaged lower-priced watch categories. They then ask an unresolved question: how long can a mechanical watch remain a status symbol after its original necessity has faded from living memory? They do not predict an imminent collapse or claim to have an answer.

Their closing formulations bring the argument together. One stresses Rolex’s remarkable point on the price-and-volume curve. The other insists that the market should be defined by the customer’s job rather than by the word watch: modern mechanical watches serve a different purpose from their timekeeping predecessors. They admire the fact that the brand a newcomer recognizes can also be the product an enthusiast returns to for engineering and serviceability. The final Apple comparison joins strong recognition, mass production, branded components and high-quality engineering.
