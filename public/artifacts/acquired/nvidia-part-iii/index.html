Nvidia
Part III

The data center becomes the computer

A research breakthrough finds a mass audience. A graphics company has spent years preparing the system it needs.

Based on Acquired · The Dawn of the AI Era (2022–2023)
Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal

Released September 6, 2023Created UnpublishedAbout 28 minutes to read

Scope: the September 2023 recording and its official corrections. Earlier research supplies the foundation; the business story ends at the recording. Chapter navigation has no audio timestamps.

01 · 2012–2015

Two graphics cards and an unexpected lineage

The people behind the breakthrough become the people behind the next one.

In 2012, three researchers at the University of Toronto gave Nvidia’s gaming hardware a different future. Alex Krizhevsky, his adviser Geoffrey Hinton, and fellow doctoral student Ilya Sutskever entered AlexNet in the ImageNet competition. The task was to recognize what appeared in a huge collection of labeled pictures. Ben Gilbert recalls an error rate falling from roughly 25% to 15%—an enormous improvement in a field accustomed to small increments.

The neural-network ideas were older than the breakthrough. What changed was the ability to train them economically. The team used two GeForce GTX 580 graphics cards, about $1,000 of consumer hardware, and CUDA, Nvidia’s programming platform. Work that could be divided into many simultaneous calculations could exploit processors originally built to draw many pixels at once. The hosts call this the “big bang” of the modern AI era, while emphasizing that parallel hardware is useful for some problem spaces, not every program.

After AlexNet, the researchers started a company that Google acquired within six months. They joined the concentration of talent around Google Brain. Google also acquired DeepMind; Facebook recruited Yann LeCun. These organizations had the resources, colleagues, and commercial reasons to attract researchers who previously worked across separate universities. Recommendation systems made the research valuable immediately: better YouTube suggestions, social feeds, and advertising could earn the money to finance more research.

The hosts distinguish those narrow tasks from general intelligence. They also qualify the consequences of that concentration. David Rosenthal speculates that access to research talent might help explain why some independent social platforms struggled. Ben calls that a few steps too far. The stronger argument is that startups and other institutions faced an increasingly concentrated source of expertise.

In 2015, Elon Musk and Sam Altman convened a dinner at the Rosewood Hotel on Sand Hill Road. Their concern went beyond competition between technology companies. If artificial general intelligence became extraordinarily powerful, who would control it? Most invited researchers had little reason to leave well-funded teams full of outstanding colleagues. Sutskever was interested. He left Google to become a co-founder and chief scientist of OpenAI, an independent nonprofit research organization. His route from AlexNet to OpenAI joins the episode’s two stories: the researchers seeking more capable intelligence and the company supplying the machines to build it.

02 · 2015–2018

A costly way to pay attention

A new model makes large-scale language training possible; it does not make it cheap.

The early OpenAI did not begin with a straight road to ChatGPT. Its projects included game-playing systems and simulated environments. In a short Nvidia video discussed by Ben, Andrej Karpathy described the possibility of learning patterns in Internet conversations and eventually talking to computers as naturally as to people. The idea preceded the method that would make it practical at much larger scale.

Google’s 2017 paper, Attention Is All You Need, supplied the Transformer architecture. The hosts explain it through translation. Translating one word at a time in rigid order fails when languages arrange words differently. Attention lets a model weigh relevant parts of its input; positional information preserves the relationships that word order carries. The official correction notes that attention itself predates the 2017 paper. The paper is the turning point in this account, rather than the invention of every ingredient.

The important hardware connection is parallel training. Earlier recurrent approaches carried sequential dependencies that limited how the work could be divided. Transformers made it possible to train sequence models through many calculations at once. That created an unusually good match with GPUs. It also brought expense: in the hosts’ simplified explanation, attention comparisons grow quadratically with input length. Doubling that length means four times as many comparisons, before considering implementation details. Parallelism makes work feasible; it does not erase the work.

The hosts initially describe recurrent networks as a later state of the art than convolutional networks. Their correction withdraws that succession story: both had existed for a long time and served different tasks, including language and vision. The distinction that matters here is the way the Transformer permitted large language-training workloads to be parallelized.

A model could now learn from a large body of text without humans labeling each example in the manner of ImageNet. Pretraining could learn patterns from the text itself; supervised fine-tuning could then shape behavior for particular tasks. The hosts are struck by how much capability emerged as data, compute, and model size increased. GPT-1 had roughly 120 million parameters, GPT-2 about 1.5 billion, and GPT-3 about 175 billion in their account. They explicitly treat GPT-4’s much larger parameter count as a rumor, not a disclosed fact.

That increasing scale raised the stakes for OpenAI. Google had both research expertise and the money to run enormous experiments. It was also building TPUs and releasing TensorFlow, giving developers a software framework that could connect to Google’s own hardware and cloud. OpenAI could see the new direction, but a nonprofit still needed a way to pay for it. The bottleneck was becoming financial as well as scientific.

03 · 2018–2023

The money to train a new interface

Microsoft supplies a route from research ambition to an enormous cloud workload.

Musk left OpenAI in 2018. The hosts discuss reports that he wanted control, but leave the precise ultimatum uncertain. What matters to their chronology is the subsequent pivot: pursuing larger Transformer models required access to capital on a different scale.

In March 2019, OpenAI announced a for-profit entity with capped investor returns and excess value intended for the original nonprofit. Ben presents its rationale as a costly attempt to preserve the mission, while David acknowledges the skeptical reading of turning a nonprofit project into a valuable commercial organization. David attributes much of the financing strategy to Altman. This is the hosts’ interpretation of the tradeoff, not a claim that the structure resolved every tension.

Microsoft invested $1 billion in 2019 and became OpenAI’s exclusive cloud provider. The episode traces GPT-3 in 2020, Microsoft’s commercial license for its own products, GitHub Copilot in 2021, and another $2 billion investment. Then, on November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT. In the hosts’ telling, it reached 100 million active users faster than any previous app. Microsoft followed with a $10 billion investment in January 2023 and plans to integrate the technology throughout its products. By the time of the recording, GPT-4 had made the new interface still more capable.

The timing was startling. During 2022, rising interest rates, collapsing crypto enthusiasm, and falling technology valuations had suggested a long winter. Nvidia had taken a painful inventory-related write-down. The official correction makes the reversal more striking: the majority of that write-down came from the data center segment, rather than primarily from softer crypto-mining demand as the hosts originally suggested. Nvidia had believed it had ordered too much AI hardware capacity shortly before ChatGPT changed demand.

The hosts draw three threads together. Generative AI had become a product ordinary people wanted to use. Training it demanded enormous GPU resources. And those resources would largely be accessed through the cloud. This combination benefited more than Nvidia’s existing graphics-chip business: it created an opening for the data center platform the company had been assembling for years.

04 · 2019–2022

The data center becomes the computer

Memory and networking turn a collection of chips into a system.

Ben pauses the chronology to explain why “buy a faster processor” is not an adequate description of the problem. His deliberately simplified CPU example loads two numbers, adds them, and stores the result. Much of the work involves moving data rather than doing arithmetic. He uses the von Neumann bottleneck to make that movement visible, while explicitly setting aside caches and other architectural complications.

For the huge language models under discussion, the problem expands beyond arithmetic speed. Their memory requirements exceed what one accelerator can hold. Multiple chips, servers, and racks must cooperate, with high-bandwidth memory close to compute and rapid transfers between components. The hosts’ organizing idea is Jensen Huang’s: the data center is the computer. The system’s connections matter alongside the capability of any individual chip.

In March 2019, Nvidia announced the roughly $7 billion cash acquisition of Mellanox; the transaction completed in 2020. Mellanox supplied InfiniBand networking, an alternative to Ethernet. The hosts describe a technology that looked excessive for ordinary enterprise workloads but became valuable when many GPUs needed to behave as one training cluster. Ben later connects the acquisition to Nvidia’s own research: in August 2019, it released Megatron, an 8.3-billion-parameter Transformer model trained on 512 GPUs for nine days. His inference is that doing the research helped Nvidia understand the networking problem before the market fully appreciated it.

The platform added Grace, Nvidia’s Arm-based CPU designed to work within its accelerated data center systems. The company was no longer merely plugging a graphics card into a motherboard controlled by someone else. It could coordinate CPUs, GPUs, NVLink connections, and Mellanox networking as an integrated design. The hosts see Grace as a supporting component with a specific reason to exist, rather than a general attempt to compete in every CPU market.

The episode also contrasts Hopper data center GPUs with the Lovelace consumer graphics architecture. High-bandwidth memory and advanced packaging become central to the discussion. The hosts describe TSMC’s CoWoS packaging as a way to bring memory and logic close together, and identify limited advanced-packaging capacity as a constraint on supply. Their technical account is intentionally broad; the argument is that a manufacturing bottleneck cannot be solved by simply accepting more orders.

These commitments preceded the scale of demand visible in 2023. Nvidia had prepared an integrated system while the opportunity was still uncertain. The hosts admire that preparation without claiming every part of the outcome was foreseen. The capacity reservations that had looked like a financial problem in 2022 were suddenly precious.

05 · 2022–2023

One system, several ways to buy

Nvidia integrates the product while allowing different routes to the customer.

A sophisticated cloud operator does not want exactly what a conventional enterprise wants. A hyperscaler has its own architecture and engineers. A Fortune 500 company may instead want a packaged AI supercomputer and the software to use it. Nvidia’s answer, as the hosts describe it, is to build the system as a whole and go to market in a disaggregated way.

The official correction is crucial here: H100s are not sold individually, even to hyperscalers. They are supplied eight at a time as part of DGX or HGX servers. The spoken episode repeatedly describes customers buying a single chip and builds some price arithmetic on that framing. This companion follows the correction and does not present those examples as a literal purchasing menu or an audited margin calculation.

The underlying distinction survives. Some customers integrate Nvidia infrastructure into their own environment; others buy more of the company’s complete solution. CUDA gives developers a common foundation across these arrangements. The hosts cite a starting price around $500,000 for a DGX H100 system and describe larger integrated installations whose price is available by inquiry. Their estimate of hundreds of millions for a large installation is explicitly speculation, not a quoted contract.

Huang’s pitch is that an expensive accelerated system can cost less than trying to perform the same work on unsuitable infrastructure. Faster completion, lower energy for a given workload, and capabilities otherwise impractical can justify the purchase. David adds a condition to the energy argument: these machines also enable work that might never have happened without them. Ben’s compression analogy distinguishes costly training from repeated inference. Model weights are a compact, useful representation; creating that representation is expensive, and changing it later is not free.

Customers renting raw cloud capacity still need people capable of operating it. DGX Cloud offers a more managed Nvidia experience, initially through infrastructure in Azure, Oracle, and Google data centers. The customer uses Nvidia’s interface, with integrations such as Hugging Face, rather than assembling every piece close to the hardware. Nvidia has introduced a cloud service through other clouds.

At the recording, the hosts quote a starting DGX Cloud price of $37,000 a month for an A100-based system. A listener estimated roughly $120,000 to build an equivalent system. David’s approximately three-month capital-payback comparison covers Nvidia and its partners together, uses an estimate, and is not a complete calculation of operating profit. The strategic point is more durable: Nvidia gains a direct enterprise relationship while the machines sit in a partner’s data center.

That matters because approximately half of data center revenue, according to the CFO’s remarks discussed by Ben, came through cloud service providers. A handful of very large buyers therefore intermediated much of the customer relationship. Nvidia already owned a strong developer relationship through CUDA; DGX Cloud offered a route closer to the organizations ultimately using the compute. The hosts also note new GPU-focused clouds such as Crusoe, CoreWeave, and Lambda alongside the established hyperscalers.

06 · 2023

The quarter that changed the argument

Real deliveries transform a speculative market story into visible demand.

In the earlier Nvidia episodes, Ben had challenged a trillion-dollar addressable-market slide assembled from vast industries such as cars, robotics, and factories. Taking 1% of a $100 trillion collection of markets was a blunt explanation for a valuation. By 2023, the physical-world applications had not all arrived. A different computing market had.

Nvidia’s first quarter of fiscal 2024, reported in May 2023, produced $7.2 billion of revenue, up 19% sequentially. The surprising number was its forecast: $11 billion for the following quarter. The hosts describe a roughly 25% after-hours stock increase as investors recalibrated demand. The company’s January fiscal year-end makes the labels confusing; this fiscal 2024 discussion belongs to calendar 2023.

The subsequent quarter exceeded even that forecast. Total revenue reached $13.5 billion, up 88% from the preceding quarter and more than 100% year over year. Data center revenue was $10.3 billion, up 141% sequentially and 171% year over year. These were recognized sales, Ben emphasizes, rather than expressions of interest or preorders. A business segment had added billions of delivered revenue within three months.

Huang now framed the opportunity around roughly $1 trillion of installed data center assets and $250 billion of annual spending to refresh and expand them. The hosts find that framing more concrete than the earlier industry-total slide. Nvidia’s opportunity is to take a larger role in what organizations already spend on computing—and in new spending enabled by AI.

They keep the missing step in view. Executives buying infrastructure believe useful applications will follow, but infrastructure revenue is not proof that all those applications will produce adequate returns. ChatGPT offers evidence of demand. The episode’s OpenAI revenue figures remain rumors, and Ben is unsure whether the largest figure he mentions is a current estimate or a forecast. The hosts’ question is whether GPT-like experiences will create lasting value across many private and public applications.

07 · 2006–2023, viewed from the recording

The software inside the hardware company

CUDA connects the developer, the machine, and the customer.

CUDA began in 2006 under Huang, Ian Buck, and others as a costly bet that graphics processors could do scientific work. The company needed more than parallel silicon. It needed to make programming that silicon practical, and it wanted a relationship with developers of its own.

Ben describes a platform that now includes a compiler, runtime, programming tools, CUDA C++, and specialized libraries. Developers can build on work already done rather than implement every operation from scratch. A broad installed base of CUDA-capable Nvidia GPUs gives that investment somewhere to run. The hosts cite four million registered developers by May 2023 and roughly 500 million CUDA-capable GPUs in the broader ecosystem.

David argues that comparing Nvidia only with a cyclical equipment supplier such as Cisco misses this relationship. Microsoft is another useful analogy: a programming environment, a software stack, and developers whose livelihoods depend on it. Ben adds old IBM, because Nvidia also supplies tightly integrated systems. The hardware is indispensable to the story, but the system and software change the terms of competition.

The hosts report gross margin near 70% in the latest quarter and guidance of 72% for the next, compared with roughly 24% in the pre-CUDA graphics business. They attribute the current level partly to extraordinary scarcity. Ben expects the shortage premium to fade, while believing substantial differentiation can remain. That is his expectation, not a guaranteed future margin floor.

China illustrates both demand and vulnerability. Export controls prevented sales of the highest-performance products discussed in the episode. Nvidia supplied A800 and H800 variants with reduced capabilities that met the restrictions then in force. David sees continued demand for those products as evidence of the platform’s lead; Ben notes buyers’ concern that controls could tighten. Neither observation removes the risk of a market becoming harder to serve.

The company still has other ambitions. Omniverse had about 700 enterprise customers in the hosts’ account, and they see a possible convergence between graphics and AI. A demo of an unscripted game character made that combination tangible. Simulated industrial environments offered another possibility. These are emerging opportunities in the recording, not established contributions equal to the data center business.

Culture also enters their explanation. Nvidia had about 26,000 employees. The hosts describe an unusually demanding organization centered on Huang, with a broad set of direct reports and senior specialists doing what they consider their life’s work. Their observations about office policy and succession are qualified impressions. The clearer theme is sustained intensity: they credit rapid shipping and deep technical commitment, while recognizing how much of the organization appears to be an extension of its founder.

08 · The hosts’ concluding analysis

A moat that has to keep moving

Their framework contains disagreement, revisions, and a temporary resource advantage.

The most interesting competition may not be AMD selling a similar chip. Ben worries about disintermediation: Amazon’s chips, Google’s TPUs, Microsoft’s possible custom hardware, or a software framework that lets developers choose other processors underneath it. Nvidia’s exceptionally attractive margin pool gives many wealthy companies a reason to try.

The hosts first debate counter-positioning. David thinks rearchitecting the data center challenged incumbents’ incentives. Ben asks what those competitors are refusing to do now. They are all pursuing accelerated computing. David accepts the objection. Their final assessment does not award a strong present-day counter-positioning advantage simply because Nvidia moved first.

Scale economies are much stronger in their account. Millions of developers and a large hardware business support a huge fixed software investment. Ben estimates about 10,000 person-years invested in CUDA by sketching staffing over time. This is his illustrative estimate, not a disclosed company accounting measure. Open-source competitors can pool resources, but they are trying to catch up with many years of accumulated work.

They compare Nvidia’s integrated approach with Apple and the emerging alternatives with Android, while warning that the analogy is imperfect. PyTorch is a framework, not a complete substitute for Nvidia’s whole stack. Its move into a foundation nevertheless gives other companies a place to contribute and potentially direct workloads toward their own hardware. David reminds Ben that open source is powerful. Ben replies that a moat adequate for a smaller prize may not be adequate when the prize grows enormously.

Switching costs include both code and organizations. Existing models, libraries, developer knowledge, procurement decisions, and data center designs create inertia. Companies make infrastructure commitments for years. The hosts think Nvidia wants to ship as much as possible while it has the lead, building a larger installed base before rivals arrive. They reject the idea that Nvidia should enjoy being unable to meet demand; David suspects management would exchange some current margin for greater sales throughput.

Reserved advanced-packaging capacity at TSMC is their cornered resource. They regard it as valuable and partly fortunate, rather than an everlasting exclusive right. AMD has substantial TSMC capacity for other products; that does not mean it can instantly obtain the particular capacity Nvidia needs. The hosts also acknowledge Google TPUs as a real alternative for large-model training, while noting that access is confined to Google Cloud. David calls that limiting for a general infrastructure business, then acknowledges it makes sense for Google’s own highly profitable applications.

Network and scale economies overlap in their discussion. Developers build libraries that other developers can use, and Nvidia’s large compatible hardware base makes the platform more attractive. Ben hesitates over which category best describes the effect. David calls the combination characteristic of platforms. The companion preserves that overlap rather than pretending the framework offers a clean accounting of every advantage.

Brand also changes weight as they talk. David initially places the consumer graphics reputation low on the list. Later he becomes more convinced by the enterprise version: nobody gets fired for buying Nvidia. Ben thinks recent buying momentum may matter more than the old consumer reputation. Process power is their weakest and most disputed category. David proposes the culture and six-month shipping rhythm; Ben asks whether that cadence matters as much as the rest of the system when many customers can still use older A100s. The ability to ship quickly is valuable without proving a separate dominant power.

09 · The hosts’ playbook and cloud debate

Who owns the next computing relationship?

The integrated stack still has to coexist with the industry around it.

Ben sees an Apple-like progression: hardware differentiated by software, followed by services. Nvidia builds a complete system but lets customers buy through different arrangements. The hosts think competition should be assessed at the system level—multiple chips and racks working together—rather than solely by comparing one chip with another.

They connect that strategy to Huang’s preference for doing things other companies cannot do. Grace arrives when Nvidia has a specific orchestration problem to solve, rather than as a long-standing desire to compete everywhere. David adds patience: Nvidia endured years inside infrastructure controlled by Intel before it had the opportunity to expand its own role. Their admiration is conditional on the underlying invention and market judgment; Ben explicitly says the playbook only works if those difficult technical bets are right.

The cloud discussion stays unresolved. Ben asks whether the winners of the first cloud era necessarily win the AI era. Data already stored in a cloud is costly to move, and training near it is attractive. Microsoft’s role as OpenAI’s exclusive cloud provider demonstrates that continuity. Yet customers asking specifically for Nvidia’s full experience could give Nvidia and specialist GPU clouds a way to compete for a larger relationship.

David distinguishes an AWS-like platform from merely owning data center buildings. There is far more software and service capability in a hyperscaler. Still, if the architecture of the data center changes, the market may shift with it. Ben ultimately bets existing customer placement will strongly influence the next era: incumbent clouds can offer the Nvidia products their customers demand. David points to the funding behind Crusoe, CoreWeave, and Lambda as evidence that a new opportunity exists. The hosts see both incumbent advantages and new openings, rather than declaring the old cloud moats gone.

10 · The hosts’ bear and bull cases

Real revenue, unfinished demand

The company can be extraordinary while its valuation assumes too much.

The first bear case is the coalition of competitors. Nvidia’s customers and partners include some of the richest technology companies in the world. They can invest tens of billions to reduce dependence on it. PyTorch creates a possible software route around CUDA, even though reproducing all the necessary hardware and software is difficult. David counters that enormous resources do not guarantee successful execution through a major strategic shift.

The second bear case is a gap between current enthusiasm and the market ultimately realized. Ben expects some trough of confidence: disappointing applications could make boardrooms reluctant to approve large infrastructure changes. David reports that researchers they spoke with often considered the present overhyped while expecting much greater change over a decade. Nvidia’s sales prove that customers are placing real bets. They do not prove those customers have already captured equal value.

Their personal experiences sharpen the qualification. David still struggles to fit ChatGPT into Acquired’s accuracy-sensitive research workflow. He recognizes that his unusual business is a poor proxy for the whole market. Both hosts find coding assistance compelling. Games, creative work, writing, and marketing may each be separate markets. Ben argues that AI’s usefulness can be the sum of many niches even when one person does not belong to an early one.

Another bear case is a shift from training toward inference, where Nvidia is less differentiated. Ben rejects the notion that models will simply become good enough and training will stop; research beyond Transformers can create new demands. But he accepts a related risk: cleverer models may need less compute, and many valuable AI tasks do not require the scale of a frontier language model. David agrees inference becomes a larger share as usage grows. China adds restrictions, uncertainty, and incentives to build domestic alternatives.

A stock-price or demand reversal could damage morale and compensation. Ben thinks Nvidia’s repeated recoveries make it unusually well disposed to survive that shock. This is a cultural argument for resilience, not a claim that a downturn would be painless.

The bull case begins with accelerated computing expanding. Ben sees workloads that could be parallelized but are not worth rewriting until tools make the change easier. David adds a central nuance: CPUs need not disappear or shrink. AI can be added to existing applications, generating new accelerated workloads that dwarf the old ones while conventional computing continues alongside them.

The second bull case is that generative AI creates lasting user value and shifts spending toward Nvidia’s systems. The third is that Nvidia’s speed and culture keep it positioned for the next development. The fourth is a larger share of existing and growing data center spending. In their back-of-the-envelope comparison, David corrects the comparison: annualizing the latest data center quarter gives roughly $40 billion, excluding gaming. Those are run-rate illustrations, not a full-year result or precise market-share study.

The hosts also cite TSMC’s expectation of substantial AI-related growth as a signal that suppliers are making costly commitments of their own. That remains a forecast at the recording. Their last bull argument returns to identity: Nvidia is not merely Intel or Cisco at the top of a cycle. It has software, hardware, developer relationships, and increasingly direct customer relationships. Microsoft and old IBM may be better analogies. Ben still suspects much inference will happen on phones and other edge devices; David does not expect frontier training to move there soon.

Ben closes with a competitor’s gauntlet. Matching a GPU is only the beginning. A rival needs chip-to-chip links, assembly partners, server and rack networking, customers willing to switch, scarce manufacturing capacity, competitive software, and developers willing to adopt it. It must assemble all that while Nvidia keeps improving. His conclusion is that a successful challenge is more likely to come from an unforeseen flank or a different computing future than an ordinary head-on imitation. It remains the hosts’ assessment in September 2023, paired with their warning that the valuation already expects a great deal to go right.

The conversation ends by acknowledging that both the technical and financial explanations were generalized to tell a lasting big-picture story. The hosts thank Ian Buck and other research contributors, then turn to their personal recommendations: Alias and Moana. The final tone is admiration for an extraordinary system, with its future still an open question.

Revisit the closing question

What has to remain true?

The hosts’ strongest defense is the combined stack: processors, networking, software, manufacturing access, developers, and customer commitments. A better isolated chip does not reproduce it.

Customers have paid real revenue for infrastructure. The open question is whether applications create enough lasting value to justify that investment and the expectations in Nvidia’s valuation.

Competitors can attack through a software framework, custom cloud chips, efficient models, or a new computing approach. The hosts expect a flank to be more plausible than a head-on reconstruction of every layer.
