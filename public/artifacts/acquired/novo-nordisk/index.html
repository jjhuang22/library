Novo Nordisk
The Long Horizon

A physician’s illness, two feuding factories, and a research program that took decades to become an overnight sensation.

A reading companion to Acquired: Novo Nordisk (Ozempic), with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

Released January 22, 2024Created UnpublishedAbout 25 minutes

The episode’s history and concluding analysis, through January 2024. Figures, expectations and medical uncertainties retain that recording-time context.

01

1921–1923

A lease on life

The discovery is only the beginning of the supply problem.

Before insulin, the accepted treatment for diabetes could be a starvation diet: keep a patient alive on a few hundred calories a day while hoping a treatment arrived. Ben and David begin in Toronto in 1921, where Frederick Banting, Charles Best and their laboratory colleagues extracted the pancreatic substance that made survival possible. The Nobel Prize later went to Banting and John Macleod, a choice the hosts describe as controversial because it excluded Best. Even the credit for this breakthrough was tangled.

The first recoveries did not solve the next problem. Insulin did not restart a patient's own production of the hormone. Its effect wore off, and another injection was necessary. A handful of usable vials could save a handful of people for a short time; putting someone on treatment created an obligation to keep supplying it. The hosts describe families and powerful officials seeking access while the laboratory struggled to reproduce and refine its process.

The University of Toronto was wary of commercial profit from a medical discovery. Yet the need forced a partnership with industry. Eli Lilly received a one-year exclusive development license for North America, with an obligation to share manufacturing improvements back with Toronto. It could retain something else: its brand. The arrangement combined shared production knowledge with an enduring commercial identity.

Lilly's work was as much about organizing a supply chain as performing an experiment. Slaughterhouses had cow and pig pancreases, but separating and shipping them interrupted tightly run operations. Someone had to persuade them to do it. The discovery needed factories, collection systems and reliable distribution before a miraculous laboratory result could become a dependable treatment.

02

1920–1924

Marie’s journey

A physician’s illness connects Copenhagen to Toronto.

August Krogh had won the 1920 Nobel Prize for his work in animal biology. That same summer, his wife Marie, herself a physician, was diagnosed with diabetes. Hans Christian Hagedorn, a young Copenhagen physician with expertise in the disease, helped stabilize her using the limited treatment available. August delayed his international lecture tour until 1922, when the couple traveled to America together.

In Boston they met Elliott Joslin, who told them about Toronto. Marie wrote home to Hagedorn and suggested bringing the new treatment back to Denmark. Macleod invited the Kroghs to visit, but Marie became too ill to make the trip north. August went alone, stayed in Macleod's home and observed insulin production. The personal connection mattered: he could see the work and speak with the committee responsible for distributing it.

The Toronto committee gave its blessing and rights for Scandinavia. In Denmark, where drugs could not then be patented, the permission was not a conventional legal barrier to competition. It was nevertheless scientific and institutional legitimacy. Back in Copenhagen, the Kroghs and Hagedorn began obtaining animal pancreases, grinding them and extracting insulin. They partnered with August Kongsted's Lion's Chemical Factory to turn that work into production. By summer 1923, the project had successfully treated eight patients in trials.

Ownership remained unsettled. Hagedorn had left medicine to work full time on the project, but being an employee of Lion's Chemical was not the institution he wanted to build. In 1924, the partners established Nordisk Insulin as an operating company owned by a foundation. Its original mission was specific: sell at cost in Scandinavia, export at market prices elsewhere, and use export profits to fund diabetes research and support. Scientific purpose was built into the ownership structure.

03

1920s–1930s

Two doors down

A factory dispute becomes sixty-five years of competition.

Among the project's first employees were Harald and Thorvald Pedersen, brothers with complementary practical skills. Harald built and adapted equipment in Krogh's laboratory. Thorvald had factory-management experience as well as training in pharmacy and chemistry. He seemed an obvious person to help run insulin production, until his idea of who should be in charge collided with Hagedorn's.

Hagedorn fired Thorvald after months of arguments. Harald resigned in solidarity, and the brothers appealed to Krogh, who sided with Hagedorn. The hosts recount a legend in which Krogh dismissed their ability to make insulin independently and Thorvald promised to show him. The dialogue is a company story, not a verified transcript of the confrontation. The consequential decision is clear in the episode: the brothers started a competing insulin business, Novo, nearby.

A legal ability to copy Nordisk was not enough. Why would a physician choose two engineers over a venture backed by a Nobel laureate, a leading diabetes physician and Toronto's blessing? The brothers needed a better practical product. Nordisk's early insulin came as solids that patients dissolved in sterilized water before measuring and injecting it with glass syringes. Novo developed shelf-stable liquid insulin and a more efficient process. It marketed its product at half price.

Nordisk responded through science, developing longer-acting protamine insulin, associated in the episode with NPH—Neutral Protamine Hagedorn. Fewer injections made an enormous difference to daily life. Nordisk licensed its technology internationally, including to Lilly, while refusing to license Novo. Novo developed an alternative; litigation reached the Danish Supreme Court, where Hagedorn represented Nordisk himself. The rivalry drove both companies toward better products while pushing them into different business models: licensing on one side, expanding production on the other.

04

1940s–1970s

The factories keep running

War and its aftermath change the balance between the rivals.

The Nazi occupation of Denmark in April 1940 changed the companies' economics in opposite directions. Nordisk depended heavily on licensing income from Allied countries. Those manufacturers could keep producing insulin, but payments to occupied Denmark stopped. Nordisk continued some domestic production, while much of its international income disappeared.

Novo had built production capacity rather than relying chiefly on licenses. Under the occupation, the German authorities directed it to expand supply across occupied Europe. The hosts hold two realities together: these drugs kept people with diabetes alive, and Novo's commercial position expanded dramatically under Nazi rule. They do not treat the period as an uncomplicated business triumph. After the war, the Danish state required Novo and the Pedersen brothers personally to repay most of their wartime profits.

Novo emerged with scale and resources to build research capabilities. Its Lente insulin supplied a slower, longer-lasting background treatment, complementing faster insulin around meals. Lilly licensed it for the American market. Ben challenges David's broader characterization of Lilly as a technology follower, noting that their history of this period leans heavily on a Novo Nordisk account. Their interpretation is not an uncontested description of Lilly's entire scientific contribution.

By the 1970s, Novo had developed highly purified monocomponent insulin. But it had also diversified into industrial enzymes, including uses in laundry detergent. When that market weakened, the second business consumed money just as insulin factories needed major investment. Novo approached its smaller, cash-rich rival about a merger. On paper, the match was attractive. Nordisk's new leader, Henry Brenham, saw a different opportunity.

05

1970s–1989

The rival says no

A product transition gives Nordisk room to return.

Brenham came from the lumber industry rather than pharmaceuticals, and Nordisk had already been through several leadership changes. He rejected Novo's approach. If the whole market was moving to highly purified insulin, he argued, Nordisk could use its reserves to rebuild manufacturing, hire an international sales force and enter America while larger competitors retooled. The hosts describe sales compounding at roughly 30% annually through the 1970s.

The turnaround did not instantly make the companies equals. In 1980, the episode puts Novo's annual insulin sales near $100 million and Nordisk's near $30 million, against approximately $160 million for Lilly. The entire insulin market was still only about half a billion dollars. The subsequent transformation combined a growing population with type 2 diabetes and a way to escape the limits of animal-derived supply.

Genentech and Lilly brought recombinant-DNA technology to insulin. Rather than collecting enough pancreases, manufacturers could use genetic engineering to produce human insulin. The hosts stress that the enduring advantage was manufacturing scale, not an automatically superior clinical outcome simply because the molecule was human. Novo had declined an opportunity to work with Genentech. It instead raced to chemically modify pig insulin into human insulin, a costly effort the hosts describe as commercially disappointing.

Novo did use the enthusiasm for biotechnology to raise $100 million in an American share offering. Nordisk took a more cautious view of the human-insulin marketing race while building its own genetic-engineering capability. By the late 1980s, the episode places Nordisk at about 20% global insulin share and Novo at about 30%. Merger talks now had a different foundation: the rivals were closer in size, and capital, research and distribution requirements increasingly rewarded scale.

In January 1989 they announced a merger of both operating companies and both foundations. David says he believes the final economic split was 62% Novo and 38% Nordisk; the episode does not establish that ratio with certainty. Together, their insulin sales were roughly $1 billion, about half of a global market that had grown to approximately $2 billion. The reunited institution was larger, but management still wondered whether even that would be enough.

06

1989–2004

The owner refuses to sell

Stability has a practical meaning when a merger reaches the board.

Pharmaceutical companies were combining into much larger groups. Novo Nordisk's managers spent years discussing mergers or a sale, believing their focused company would need the shelter of a diversified giant. Meanwhile, the business kept growing. Genetically engineered supply, a larger diabetes market and expanded treatment drove revenue and profit upward. The enzyme business became Novozymes in 2000, and Novo Nordisk won large American supply agreements, including Walmart and the Veterans Affairs hospital system.

By the end of 2003, annual revenue exceeded $4 billion. In 2004, management and the operating-company board were prepared to combine with Switzerland's Serono. The foundation still had to agree. Its rules required a convincing argument that a transaction was necessary to preserve and expand the company's competitive international position.

The foundation board looked at years of strong growth and was unconvinced that independence had become untenable. Management presented its case at meetings in August and September. The board blocked the deal. Ben and David regard this as a decisive consequence of foundation control: an offer attractive to ordinary public shareholders could not simply overrule the institution's purpose.

At the time of the episode, the foundation's holding company controlled about 77% of votes while owning about 28% of the economic interest. That distinction explains why management needed this particular owner's consent. It does not mean the foundation owned three-quarters of the business economically. Nor does its charitable purpose mean the operating company does not pursue commercial profits.

07

Early 1990s–2005

Protect the molecule

Lotte Bjerre Knudsen keeps pursuing a drug others doubt.

Lotte Bjerre Knudsen joined Novo in 1989, initially working in enzymes, before moving into diabetes research. Her team screened compounds for type 2 diabetes. In the early 1990s, she began pursuing glucagon-like peptide-1, or GLP-1, a hormone involved in regulating insulin secretion. The promise was attractive: help the body regulate its own insulin rather than only supplying insulin from outside.

The obstacle was duration. Ordinary GLP-1 was broken down so quickly that simply putting more of it into the body was not a practical medicine. The hosts describe its active life in minutes. Knudsen continued despite skepticism inside and outside Novo Nordisk. Around 1995–1996, management gave the program an ultimatum: produce a viable drug candidate within a year or lose support.

The breakthrough was liraglutide, an analogue of GLP-1 with a fatty acid attached. The hosts explain that this let it bind to albumin, a large protein in the blood, protecting it against degradation and rapid clearance. They compare liraglutide's roughly 13-hour half-life with only a few minutes for ordinary GLP-1. The goal shifted from an impractical succession of doses to a potential once-daily treatment. This was molecular engineering built on decades of experience making injected medicines last longer.

A candidate was still far from a commercial drug. Animal studies and successive human trials took years. Meanwhile, a parallel research path involving a GLP-1-like compound associated with Gila monster venom led to Byetta, commercialized by Lilly and approved in 2005. Novo Nordisk had not invented the only route into the category. Byetta's twice-daily injections, however, left room for a more convenient treatment.

08

2005–2016

A second indication

The scientists see appetite effects before management sees a business.

Liraglutide entered large phase-three diabetes trials in 2007. The hosts distinguish early safety and dose work, preliminary efficacy studies and the much larger trials needed to confirm efficacy and collect robust safety evidence. They emphasize that a drug can consume years of work and still fail at successive gates. A promising experiment does not remove development risk.

Knudsen's team had also noticed animals eating less, and appetite effects were appearing in humans. That observation suggested a second indication: obesity. Management had reasons to hesitate. Earlier weight-loss drugs had a history of ineffectiveness and serious harm, while obesity was widely treated as a question of willpower. As recently as 2005, Novo Nordisk's own CEO had publicly said that obesity was a social and cultural problem with no business for the company.

The researchers argued for testing a higher-dose version of the same molecule. Evidence from the diabetes program gave them a base to build on, without proving that every dose, use or long-term outcome would be safe. Novo Nordisk began human weight-loss trials in 2007. The hosts frame the change as both a scientific opportunity and a challenge to the stigma surrounding obesity as a disease.

Victoza, the diabetes brand of liraglutide, received American approval in early 2010. The episode puts initial-year sales near $300 million and sales in its first full year, 2011, above $1 billion—the industry's threshold for a blockbuster. Some demand came from off-label weight-loss prescribing. The hosts explain that clinicians can sometimes prescribe approved drugs for other uses, while manufacturers face different restrictions on promoting those uses; they explicitly resist making this an absolute rule.

Saxenda, the obesity version of liraglutide, did not transform the market to the extent investors anticipated. The hosts describe an average long-term reduction of about 8% in the BMI measure they discuss, short of the roughly 10% weight-loss threshold they repeatedly heard would change adoption. That threshold is an industry expectation in their account, not a biological law. Alongside mounting controversy over insulin prices, the disappointment helped form the backdrop to a roughly 40% stock decline in 2016 and a change of CEO. A century-old company approaching a breakthrough still looked vulnerable.

09

2008–2023

The weekly breakthrough

A long research program suddenly looks like an overnight arrival.

Semaglutide was the next step in the same line of work, with early trials dating to 2008 in the hosts' account. It lasted much longer, enabling a weekly injection, and produced substantially larger weight-loss effects than liraglutide. The episode describes long-term reductions around 15% or more, enough to cross the adoption threshold the hosts had discussed. Ozempic was the diabetes brand; Wegovy was the obesity brand. The active molecule was the same, though the products and approved uses differed.

Ben explains two mechanisms associated with the appetite effect: signals to the brain that a person is satiated, and slower movement of food through the digestive system. Users described less preoccupation with food. The hosts do not say that energy balance stops applying. They describe a medicine that can change the impulses and behavior surrounding eating. They also stress nausea, vomiting, constipation and other side effects that can make continued treatment difficult.

Ozempic reached the market in 2018 and exceeded $1 billion in annual sales in 2019, according to the episode. Wegovy's American weight-loss approval arrived in 2021. Its opening wave of prescriptions, the hosts say, matched Saxenda's lifetime prescriptions in a little over a month. Public attention grew around the same time clinical results made the effectiveness much harder to dismiss.

Demand did not mean everyone could obtain the drug. Ozempic and Wegovy were supply-constrained, and management expected that constraint to persist despite new factories. The episode describes company revenue rising from about $20 billion in 2019 to $30 billion in 2022, with strong reported growth during the first three quarters of 2023. These are the recording's historical figures, not present-day financial data.

The business mix had already changed. The hosts put diabetes GLP-1 products at 51% of revenue and obesity GLP-1 products at another 18%, against 22% for insulin and 9% for other areas. The old insulin specialist had become predominantly a GLP-1 company. Trials for additional uses—including cardiovascular, kidney and Alzheimer's disease—suggested further possibilities. Ben repeatedly calls those possibilities early; the episode does not establish them as approved uses or guaranteed outcomes.

10

The January 2024 market

A patient does not simply buy a drug

Manufacturing scarcity meets a fragmented system for paying.

The episode pauses its company history to unpack the American drug market. A manufacturer develops and produces the medicine, a wholesaler buys and distributes it, and a pharmacy dispenses it. Payment adds another chain: insurers, employers and pharmacy benefit managers, or PBMs. A PBM negotiates a formulary and discounts, often through rebates. What appears on a sticker is different from the manufacturer's net receipts and different again from the patient's out-of-pocket cost.

The hosts describe a highly concentrated PBM market, with three large groups controlling about 80% in the figures they cite. A manufacturer seeking broad patient access needs agreements with these gatekeepers. Ben reports Lilly's claim that discounts and rebates amounted to 75% of insulin's sticker price. He also notes that PBMs say much of the rebate goes onward to health plans. The discussion does not trace a uniform rebate amount into every patient's pocket.

For GLP-1s, the episode quotes American monthly list prices above $1,000 for Ozempic and $1,300 for Wegovy, before insurance. Those prices are January 2024 context, not a current price list. Even insured patients could face substantial monthly costs, and many people could not afford treatment. Adequate manufacturing supply alone would not remove this access problem.

Ben's second concern is time horizon. He cites average private-sector job tenure of 3.7 years to illustrate how an insurer or employer can pay for treatment now while another payer receives the benefit of fewer complications later. That average is an illustration of churn, not a fixed contract length for every patient. The federal government ultimately bears much later-life expense through Medicare, but at the time of the episode Medicare Part D specifically excluded drugs used for weight loss.

The hosts explore whether earlier treatment might prevent expensive future illness. They do not claim the economic case is settled or propose universal coverage as a proved solution. Covering a very large population at the quoted prices would create an immediate budget problem. Treatment persistence adds another uncertainty: Ben cites research suggesting as many as 68% of users stop within a year, with cost, coverage changes, shortages and side effects among the explanations. It is a cited high-end finding, not an inevitable outcome for each patient. Regained weight after stopping complicates both the personal benefit and a payer's return.

11

Competition & Seven Powers

How long can the advantage last?

The hosts separate a powerful business from a permanent monopoly.

Lilly was already competing. Its tirzepatide combines GLP-1 and GIP activity, sold for diabetes as Mounjaro and approved in November 2023 for obesity as Zepbound. Early trial comparisons suggested stronger weight-loss effects. Novo Nordisk had next-generation work of its own, including CagriSema. David's bullish analogy is another insulin-like cycle: repeated improvements, more supply and demand large enough for multiple manufacturers. That is his scenario, conditional on continued safety and efficacy.

Insulin itself offered a warning about durability. Pricing controversy, regulatory pressure, biosimilar competition and the arrival of GLP-1s had weakened the economics the incumbents once considered secure. Ben recounts allegations in 2021 that Novo Nordisk raised insulin prices more than 600% between 2001 and 2019 alongside rivals; Novo denied the allegations and pointed to declining net prices since 2017. The difference between accusation, response and the hosts' interpretation matters.

Ben and David discuss Novo's shift as timely self-disruption but stop short of crediting a master plan to escape insulin's decline. David says he found no evidence that the timing was anything other than coincidence. The scientific program had started decades earlier. Their explanation preserves both accumulated competence and luck.

In Seven Powers terms, patents are the clearest cornered resource. Ben cites 2032 as semaglutide's patent horizon in this discussion, without supplying a country-by-country legal schedule. New molecules and delivery devices may support further patent cycles, but existing protection is finite. Scale matters in research, factories and commercial access. Manufacturing expertise and the ability to make molecules survive in the body also matter; Rybelsus, an oral semaglutide product, is the hosts' example of a difficult delivery problem.

They also see switching costs when a patient has found a treatment that works. Ben argues that Ozempic has unusual brand power, recalling how he initially mistook Mounjaro for a lesser imitation. David admits he had made a similar mistake about Wegovy. These anecdotes explain consumer recognition; they are not clinical comparisons.

Their disagreement about network effects remains unresolved. Ben describes visible weight loss as a walking advertisement and suggests that more users can reduce stigma. David accepts the word-of-mouth effect but doubts it is a network economy: another person's treatment does not directly improve yours. They are similarly cautious about counter-positioning, rather than assigning every possible power to a successful incumbent.

12

The hosts’ concluding analysis

A hundred years, and an open question

Focus explains the capabilities; it does not guarantee the outcome.

The foundation returns at the end of the episode as more than a corporate curiosity. The hosts describe its stated objectives as stability and scientific and humanitarian support, rather than a requirement to maximize growth. Novo Holdings had an approximately $120 billion portfolio in their account, but Ben stresses that most of it was the concentrated ownership stake in Novo Nordisk. It was not $120 billion of accumulated cash available to give away. He separately cites $4.5 billion in grants over six years as evidence of meaningful research funding.

Ben examines a management structure less driven by stock options than the American model he knows. The hosts connect mission, patient research and a long time horizon, while acknowledging Danish culture and uncertainty about the causal story. David's qualification is essential: sustained focus can make an extraordinary outcome possible, but it does not guarantee one. The company also needed useful discoveries, favorable developments and luck.

Novo's concentration contrasts with an industry increasingly drawn toward rare diseases and expensive specialty treatments. The hosts explain the pressure to pursue conditions with fewer adequate alternatives, the rising cost of development and the difficulty of improving on medicines that already work. They describe pharmaceutical returns as a power law: most candidates fail, many approved drugs never recover development costs, and a small group of winners funds much of the whole system.

That is why Ben resists reading an 84% gross margin as proof of effortless excess profit. Manufacturing costs for a successful medicine leave out years of research and failed programs. He cites an industry return on invested capital of roughly 13%, broadly comparable with other industries in the data he consulted, while emphasizing that Novo outperformed its peers. His point is about risk-adjusted economics across the portfolio, not a denial that Novo captures enormous value.

The discussion then broadens to a healthcare system built largely around acute and infectious illness, facing chronic conditions that benefit from prevention over decades. Ben questions the incentives of consolidated intermediaries and argues that drug developers bear substantial innovation risk. But he closes by admitting that mechanisms which first looked irrational often had defensible reasons behind them. High patient prices and unexceptional industry-wide investment returns coexist; the hosts leave the tension open rather than offering a simple villain or a solved policy design.

Their bull case is a long GLP-1 cycle with repeated advances and enough unmet need for Novo and Lilly to prosper together. Their bear case is that long-term safety, effectiveness, adherence or some other limitation prevents that cycle. They mention early concerns about lean-mass loss and discussion of suicidal thoughts, while noting that they had not found an established broad-population signal for the latter. These are the uncertainties as discussed in January 2024, not new medical conclusions.

The story ends where its ownership structure began: with an institution trying to sustain work beyond the horizon of a single product. A discovery in Toronto, a physician's illness, two competing factories, a rejected sale and a scientist's persistence all helped bring the business to this point. Ben and David's interpretation is that the accumulated work mattered deeply. How much of its promise would endure was still a question.
