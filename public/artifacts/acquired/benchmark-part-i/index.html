Benchmark
Part I

The firm belongs to the people doing the work.

Five equal partners set out to challenge venture capital’s hierarchy. An auction website made them rich. What they did with the firm afterward would test the promise that brought them together.

Acquired · Ben Gilbert & David RosenthalReleased September 28, 2022Created UnpublishedAbout 28 minutes to read

Part I only · Biography, partnership history, and the hosts’ concluding analysis, as discussed in September 2022. Chapter navigation follows this companion; the untimed transcript supplies no verified listening cues.

Chapter 01 · Flint → Sand Hill Road · before 1994

Who owns the firm?

Bob Kagle’s objection began with the rewards for work already done.

Bob Kagle grew up in Flint, Michigan, in a General Motors family, raised by a single mother. College was not an obvious next step. His route ran through General Motors University, a school built to train people for the industry. Stanford Business School, consulting at Boston Consulting Group, and an invitation from Dave Marquardt eventually took him into Technology Venture Investors. At TVI, he made successful investments including Synopsys. After roughly a decade, he had helped create considerable value. He still did not own the keys to the firm.

The distinction mattered more than a generous paycheck. A venture fund has a pool of capital, an allocation of its investment profits called carried interest, and a finite life. The management company sits above those funds. It receives management fees and governs the continuing business: who works there, who gets paid, and what funds come next. A younger investor could receive carry in a particular fund while remaining an employee of a management company controlled by the founders. Good investments did not necessarily buy a voice in the institution.

TVI had been the only venture investor in Microsoft, putting in $1 million for 5% of the company. Its senior partners had already won on an extraordinary scale. Kagle’s insistence that the people doing the work should share equally in the ownership did not produce an agreement. TVI chose to ‘declare victory.’ Existing investments and board responsibilities would run off; the firm would stop building its next generation together. Marquardt went on to co-found August Capital.

For Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal, Kagle’s background helps explain his fervor. They interpret equality as a matter of fairness before it became a competitive strategy. They did not interview Kagle for the episode, and they do not claim to know every element of his motivation. But the principle that emerged was unusually concrete: Benchmark’s active general partners would share fund carry equally, and ownership of the management company would be given to the current partners. New partners would not have to buy it from the people leaving.

Across the Valley, John Doerr’s Kleiner Perkins offered a very different picture of success. Doerr was the star investor and institutional leader. Kleiner could help arrange distribution and commercial relationships among its portfolio companies, a powerful advantage in an Internet world that still depended heavily on business development deals. It could also win a founder with the senior partner and then try to hand the board seat to someone junior. To challenge that franchise, a small new firm needed a reason for entrepreneurs to choose it. Kagle’s demand would supply one.

Chapter 02 · 1994–1996

Five signatures, one promise

An equal partnership was difficult to recruit and expensive to believe in.

Kagle did not have to go far to find Bruce Dunlevie. TVI and Merrill Pickard occupied the same building at 2480 Sand Hill Road. Dunlevie had been Kagle’s intern at BCG after studying English literature at Rice and later attending Stanford Business School. He had since worked in the PC industry and at Goldman Sachs, then become a rising investor at Merrill Pickard, where he backed Palm. Kagle approached his former subordinate as a prospective equal.

Dunlevie initially declined. He was younger and did not have Kagle’s financial cushion. When discussions about equal economics at Merrill Pickard went nowhere, however, the possibility became a necessity. Dunlevie and colleague Andy Rachleff joined Kagle in planning a new firm. They could not simply run it as a two-person boutique. Their model required taking board seats and doing the work; two people could not cover enough companies to make a sufficiently diversified portfolio. They needed more investment capacity.

Other established investors largely thought leaving a good firm was foolish. The recruits turned instead to Kevin Harvey, a Rice contemporary of Dunlevie’s and an entrepreneur whose companies had been acquired by Apple and Lotus. Dunlevie had backed Harvey’s second business, Approach Software. Harvey brought the experience of sitting on the other side of a venture board. The fifth founder was Val Vaden, who had worked in venture and moved into software buyouts and technology special situations. The original Benchmark was Kagle, Dunlevie, Rachleff, Harvey, and Vaden—not just the four names that later became most familiar.

They named the firm Benchmark Capital and proposed 30% carried interest, against the customary 20% of fund profits. The name and the price said that this unproven combination belonged at the top. Some limited partners agreed. Horsley Bridge made a large commitment to the first fund. Others saw established relationships breaking apart and a new manager asking to charge more. In the hosts’ account, Stanford’s endowment went beyond declining: it called other endowments, including investors already committed, to discourage them from backing Benchmark. A later Stanford team eventually became an investor.

The founders pitched endurance as well as audacity, handing prospective investors Jim Collins’s Built to Last. They closed an $85 million fund in 1995. Raising it did not make the partnership gel. The firm missed the excitement around Amazon and Yahoo, and by the end of 1996 had invested about $16 million. Vaden’s buyout orientation did not fit the others’ formation-stage technology investing. He left the active partnership. The hosts describe the separation as generous: Benchmark continued to feature him and support his effort to raise a fund for his own strategy. Equality had survived its first test, but it had not yet delivered a great investment franchise.

Chapter 03 · 1997

The recruiter who would take the bet

David Beirne brought the confidence the partnership feared it had lost.

David Beirne was already making millions of dollars in cash at the executive search firm that bore his name. From Westchester County, New York, he had cold-called Silicon Valley presenting Ramsay/Beirne as a leading technology recruiter before it had earned that position. Winning John Doerr as a client helped make the claim real. Beirne recruited Jim Barksdale for Netscape and worked on other prominent CEO searches. Benchmark persuaded him to leave an established, lucrative business for uncertain investment profits.

The fit was emotional as well as practical. Beirne was intensely competitive, and the remaining partners needed a fifth person who would push them toward ambitious bets. One early opportunity was Louis Borders’s Webvan. Borders wanted an online store for everyday needs, beginning with groceries and delivering to the doorstep. Benchmark already had the business plan. Some partners were hesitant about its breadth and wanted to narrow it to food. Beirne embraced the bigger vision. Rosenthal thinks it may have been his first Benchmark deal, but explicitly cannot prove that ordering.

Benchmark and Sequoia split a $7 million round, investing $3.5 million each. Beirne and Mike Moritz joined the board. Webvan later raised capital from other investors and went public; its peak market capitalization reached roughly $8 billion before the collapse. The hosts use an assumed 10% initial stake, ignoring possible dilution, to illustrate how $3.5 million might have become $800 million of paper value. They do not know how much Benchmark actually realized. The distinction is central: this is their defense of the original risk, not a claim that Webvan produced an $800 million cash return.

At less than 5% of the first fund, the commitment bought exposure to an enormous ambition from an entrepreneur trying to exceed his earlier success. The hosts regard that as an appropriate venture bet even though the business failed. Their interpretation is that the vision arrived a decade or two too early. It restored a willingness to swing hard when the downside was bounded and the upside was not.

The partnership also let author Randall Stross observe its meetings and portfolio companies for what became eBoys. Access on that scale was unusual and itself a declaration of confidence. The hosts draw on scenes from the book while repeatedly warning that it is dramatized, gossipy, and prone to turning people into types. Benchmark’s public swagger was useful. Its next great investment would depend on something more specific: the different partners recognizing how their talents could fit together.

Chapter 04 · 1997–1999

The company that did not need the money

AuctionWeb needed a different kind of partner—and a reason to remain independent.

Pierre Omidyar already knew Dunlevie from Ink, a pen-computing company that had pivoted into ecommerce software and sold to Microsoft. Omidyar later worked at General Magic and built a collection of Internet services on the side. AuctionWeb was one of them. It became the part that worked. When traffic made his server bills difficult to pay, he reluctantly asked users for listing fees. Checks arrived at his apartment faster than he could process them. Jeff Skoll, fresh from Stanford Business School, joined as the business person, initially helping turn that mail into cash.

The company was profitable and growing roughly 10% a month when Omidyar and Skoll looked for investors. They did not need venture money to cover a burn rate. They wanted experience and a professional board. Almost every venture firm declined. The website looked crude; a marketplace for collectibles did not resemble the semiconductor, networking, and enterprise businesses that technology investors understood. Benchmark saw a business already working beneath an unpersuasive exterior.

Dunlevie supplied the relationship, Kagle the interest in consumer businesses, and Beirne the confidence that he could recruit the management team. Harvey and Rachleff supported the deal. Benchmark offered $6.7 million at a $20 million pre-money valuation. The alternative was not another venture term sheet: newspaper company Knight Ridder offered to acquire the business for $50 million. For two people running a barely formed company, that was difficult money to refuse.

Here the history becomes disputed. The hosts know Benchmark obtained more than 20% of eBay; they cite reported ownership of 22.1% at the IPO. They also recount reporting of equity-backed loans of $750,000 each to Omidyar and Skoll, giving the founders personal liquidity and a reason not to sell. Another contemporary partner’s account described roughly half the round as secondary money; still another account denied a secondary altogether. The hosts think some founder liquidity probably existed, but cannot reconcile the structure or establish whether it was additional to the $6.7 million. This companion leaves that uncertainty intact.

What matters to the story is the willingness to solve the founders’ actual problem. The company could remain independent while its founders gained some financial security, if the liquidity accounts are correct. Benchmark also delivered on recruiting. Meg Whitman came from Hasbro, with earlier experience at Disney and in consulting, to lead eBay. A little over a year after the investment, the company went public in September 1998. Benchmark’s stake was then worth about $400 million.

Its lockup kept it from distributing immediately. By the following spring, the stake was worth more than $4 billion. The hosts are confident that $4 billion was actually distributed—a realized result, rather than a dot-com mark. They discuss a possible additional $1 billion from the disputed loans or secondary arrangements, but make it conditional. Likewise, the first fund’s famous 92× figure was a mark around the publication of eBoys, not a permanently fixed net cash multiple. The hosts describe the eventual outcome as at least roughly 50×, while doing back-of-the-envelope comparisons. The secure anchor is that extraordinary $4 billion distribution against an $85 million original fund.

A smaller episode tested whether Benchmark would behave like the interconnected empire it had opposed. Entrepreneur in residence Danny Shader formed accept.com to handle online payments, initially around eBay auctions. Benchmark funded it; eBay considered investing, then did not, leaving the business without its expected distribution partner. Benchmark did not force eBay to support it. Amazon, preparing its own auctions business, bought accept.com for $175 million in stock. The two portfolio relationships could take different paths. The hosts see a contrast with a model in which one company must serve the interests of an investor’s wider network.

Chapter 05 · The partnership in practice

Equal money is only the beginning

The scarce asset was a group in which helping a colleague did not weaken your own position.

eBay made the most direct incentive visible. Kagle, Dunlevie, Harvey, Rachleff, and Beirne shared the result rather than competing to claim the investment. Someone who could improve the company’s outcome had a reason to do so, regardless of who held the board seat. But the hosts argue that the more powerful daily effect concerned careers. In a hierarchy, helping a senior partner can make that person’s record stronger while widening the gap between their standing and yours. A great executive candidate might be saved for your own portfolio. At Benchmark, there was no higher internal rank to win.

For entrepreneurs, the structure also made the service promise credible. There was no junior partner to whom a star could hand off the relationship. The whole firm could know the company and contribute its different abilities. Rich Barton, who founded Expedia and Zillow and spent years as a Benchmark venture partner, described the challenge to Gilbert as cooperating your way to success. That cooperation could become an example for the companies themselves. Founders interviewed for the episode described weekly conversations, and sometimes daily calls or texts, with a board member who felt almost like another co-founder.

None of this makes an equal split universally superior. Gilbert and Rosenthal insist that it works only if people contribute comparable value and keep bringing their full effort. Otherwise an equal partnership can drift toward mediocrity, resentments, or departure by the strongest contributor. Benchmark’s combination of competitive people and collective incentives had to produce an upward standard, rather than the comfortable middle its critics expected.

The difficulty is that investment outcomes arrive slowly and unevenly. Even a talented, committed partner can have a dry spell. Colleagues must judge whether the problem is temporary without destroying the confidence needed to make an unusual bet. The hosts describe a delicate balance: support someone enough to preserve their courage, while remaining honest about whether they are doing the work. Much of this is implicit, maintained through relationships and long partner meetings rather than a published rulebook.

Retirement is part of the same bargain. At the point when a partner wants to ease off, the model requires making room for someone who wants the full job. Giving away management-company ownership is not a charitable afterthought. It is the mechanism that prevents the young partners of tomorrow from finding themselves in the same position that drove Kagle out of TVI. Whether the founders would actually honor that mechanism after making fortunes remained an open test.

Chapter 06 · 1998–2000

Brains, nerve, and a sixth seat

Bill Gurley arrived as Benchmark was deciding what success entitled it to become.

After eBay, the partners could have stopped raising institutional money and invested their own wealth. Instead they recommitted. Calls arrived from large corporations offering joint ventures, from prospective partners, and from investors eager to supply more capital. Benchmark now had a brand that could be used far beyond its original business. Some partners wanted to exploit those opportunities. Others thought success was a reason to keep the model small.

One decision commanded agreement: add a sixth equal general partner. This was an unusually consequential hire. There was no junior seat in which to test someone before giving them the economics and authority. The hosts describe a narrow recruiting window: someone experienced enough to have learned the craft, young enough to have a long career ahead, fiercely competitive, and capable of being a generous teammate. They were looking for an exceptional investor who was not yet fully rewarded at another firm.

Gurley had worked as an engineer at Compaq, become a Wall Street analyst, helped with Amazon’s IPO, and moved into venture at Hummer Winblad. His Above the Crowd writing had made him well known. He was analytical, hard working, and inventive about distribution: the episode recounts him using conference attendees’ fax numbers to circulate his work. His reputation was established before his Benchmark investment record was.

In eBoys, the partners worry that he might think too much before acting. A hunting-trip story, in which he pursues an animal over difficult terrain, is presented as reassurance about his nerve. The hosts treat the anecdote with the same caution they apply to the book’s dramatized scenes. More consequentially, Gurley brought a different way of seeing a business. He could examine marketplace economics, take rates, financial statements, and adoption data, then act aggressively when the price failed to reflect what he saw.

His first Benchmark deal was Epinions, then led by Naval Ravikant. The relationship did not end well for Ravikant, who later built Venture Hacks and AngelList. Gurley also backed businesses such as OpenTable, Zillow, and Grubhub. Those were substantial wins, though not another eBay. Over the following years, cheaper company formation and eventually AWS changed what an early investment could buy. In consumer businesses, angels could absorb the risk of getting a first product into the world. A Series A investor could then examine actual use.

The hosts see Benchmark’s adaptation as central to the second generation. The firm did not simply preserve the founders’ gut-based formation investing. It moved toward consumer Series A investments with evidence of product-market fit while remaining willing to fund repeat enterprise founders much earlier. Gurley’s analytical abilities fit that opening. But in 1999 and 2000, the world that made this especially attractive was still years away. Benchmark was about to try a much larger transformation first.

Chapter 07 · 2000–2005

The empire they tried to build

The firm designed to avoid administration acquired a great deal of it.

Benchmark’s reputation for refusing to scale can make its actual history sound tidier than it was. The fourth US fund reached $1 billion. A European fund raised $750 million, later reduced to $500 million after the bubble burst. An Israel fund added $220 million. The firm recruited seven partners to run the international businesses. Their local partnerships were equal internally, with separate economics and an agreement to use Benchmark’s brand. They were not all one worldwide pool of equal owners.

The reasoning was plausible. eBay could have started somewhere other than Silicon Valley. Benchmark had the capital, the name, and a model it thought could travel. Dunlevie spent substantial time in London setting up Europe. Corporate relationships also expanded. The firm’s website displayed a network of industry executives and companies intended to facilitate partnerships and advice. Benchmark even made a late-stage investment in 1-800-Flowers before its IPO. It never added junior partners, but it tried many of the other ways to become a larger institution.

A separate partnership did not mean separate demands on the original partners. Someone had to recruit the teams, answer governance questions, maintain LP relationships, and transmit the culture. The hosts argue that a central advantage of the small model—spending almost all one’s time investing and helping companies—was being consumed by running the firm. Other successful venture firms were built with leaders and staff who could manage expansion. Benchmark’s architecture assumed that its partners would remain players on the field.

They tell the cost through three misses, while acknowledging that the causal links are not equally certain. Gurley describes Google as a failure to pursue, rather than a company Benchmark formally rejected after a clear pitch. The hosts believe Benchmark had a credible opportunity, though winning against Kleiner and Sequoia would not have been assured. Skype later exposed a more direct organizational problem: should the European partnership invest, or should Gurley and the US team do it? In the handoff, Benchmark lost the opportunity.

Facebook was different again. Benchmark already backed Friendster. The episode recounts a story in which Friendster’s leadership blocked Benchmark from pursuing Facebook because of the conflict. Rosenthal qualifies the details as a reported account. The hosts regard this as another painful omission in the same period, not proof that an international office directly caused the missed investment. A firm seeking generational results cannot repeatedly miss the companies that define a generation.

Around 2004–2005, the partners decided to unwind the expansion and return to early-stage investing with appropriately sized funds, concentrated near home. The episode attributes the moment to a legendary partner-meeting intervention, probably from Harvey: why were they doing all this? Europe and Israel separated. The hosts recognize that value was created in those international teams even though the arrangement was wrong for Benchmark. Refocusing required more than announcing a principle. Partners had to admit together that a large experiment should end, without turning the correction into a personal defeat.

Chapter 08 · 2005–2011

The founders give the firm away

Succession required surrendering future economics, then betting on people who were not yet legends.

The first wave of retirement made Kagle’s original promise tangible. Beirne, Rachleff, and Alex Balkanski, who had also joined the partnership during the intervening years, stepped back. They kept their interests and board obligations in old funds. They did not retain ownership of the management company or carry in new funds simply because they had helped build the firm. They became major limited partners with their own money. The distinction was clean: old work retained its old rewards; the next generation owned the next business.

The active group was down to Dunlevie, Kagle, Harvey, and Gurley. To rebuild, Benchmark recruited Peter Fenton from Accel. He had grown up around the business: his father Noel was an entrepreneur and later a co-founder of Trinity Ventures. More immediately, Benchmark partners kept meeting companies and discovering Fenton had reached them first. He had experience and momentum, but not the full senior economics available at Benchmark.

The move was still a bet. Fenton was leaving economics in Accel’s Facebook fund to join a firm whose best fund lay a decade behind it and whose founders were stepping back. Gurley had not yet become the marketplace investor his later record would make famous. Fenton accepted. His subsequent investments included Twitter, New Relic, Zuora, Hortonworks, and Elastic. The episode emphasizes how repeat relationships mattered: New Relic was incubated at Benchmark after Fenton had backed Lew Cirne’s earlier company at Accel.

Mitch Lasky came by a different route. A games-industry operator, he had led mobile games company JAMDAT, where Gurley was an investor and board member. JAMDAT’s IPO and sale to Electronic Arts had been a bright spot in Benchmark’s difficult period. Lasky knew products and an industry that many investors did not. He went on to back Riot Games, thatgamecompany, Snap, and Discord, among others. His contribution extended well beyond a narrow games category.

Matt Cohler had been an early employee at LinkedIn and employee number five at Facebook. He had helped build the consumer-social future the partnership wanted to understand. He did not come with a conventional venture record, and neither did Lasky. The hosts explicitly acknowledge that both hires departed from the safer recruiting specification they had described. Benchmark was making judgment calls, not following an infallible template.

Cohler’s formulation captured the moment: a venture investor’s job was to see the present very clearly. With products cheap enough to launch and adoption visible in data, early-stage investing could sometimes happen after crucial risks had already fallen away. This was the hosts’ interpretation of a particular historical opening, not a claim that startup investing became risk-free. Cohler understood mobile advertising and described the smartphone as a remote control for the real world. Gurley, Fenton, Lasky, and Cohler became the ‘Fab Four,’ with the original generation overlapping during the transition. Fund six included Instagram, Asana, and several important enterprise outcomes. The partnership had been refounded rather than merely inherited.

Chapter 09 · 2011–2015

A small team at a very large opening

Mobile, marketplaces, games, and enterprise software met a partnership ready to act.

Benchmark raised $550 million for fund seven in 2011. Gurley understood marketplace economics; Cohler had helped shape social products; Lasky knew games; Fenton could move between enterprise, open source, and consumer businesses. The categories overlapped in practice. Lasky did Snap, Fenton did Twitter, and the partnership could give a company to the colleague best equipped to serve it. The hosts see the team’s composition, timing, and confidence as unusually well matched.

The fund included Uber, Snap, Discord, Stitch Fix, Duo Security, Docker, Elastic, Nextdoor, WeWork, and thatgamecompany. This was concentrated ownership, not a long list of tiny checks. The episode describes typical ownership in the 15–20%-plus range, often higher in that era, and roughly one new board seat per partner per year. A 2018 Wall Street Journal report put fund seven at 25× before fees. Gilbert thinks it might be lower at the time of recording, perhaps still above 20× depending on realizations. These are dated reports and estimates, not this companion’s valuation of the fund.

Uber brought the partnership’s division of labor into focus. Cohler helped source the opportunity; Gurley had spent years studying transport and trying to get Taxi Magic to become something like Uber. Once Travis Kalanick was CEO and the service showed traction, Benchmark backed Uber’s roughly $10–11 million Series A at a $60 million post-money valuation in 2011. Gurley took the board seat. When asked why he chose Benchmark, Kalanick answered simply that they were the best.

The episode preserves the exuberance of the chase. While Kalanick was pitching Sequoia on Sand Hill Road, Benchmark partners called away the Uber he had asked to wait outside. He ran down the road to their meeting; they later sent him running shoes as a joke. It is an anecdote about competitive confidence, not evidence that the investment was easy or that the future was settled.

The hosts argue that Series A had briefly offered an attractive mismatch between observable traction and price. By 2022, they say, the market knew what breakout consumer adoption looked like and priced it aggressively. Their praise for the earlier opportunity should not be read as a recommendation that the same round label always buys the same risk. Benchmark had adapted to a moment, and the moment changed.

The partnership also converted some paper success into cash. The episode cites roughly $900 million of Uber shares sold to SoftBank while a much larger stake remained in 2018, and about $1 billion of gains from selling almost half its Snap stake that February. WeWork, too, had been a profitable realization for Benchmark in the hosts’ account, despite the company’s broader troubles and litigation around SoftBank transactions. Picking winners was only part of the result. Deciding when and how to sell would be an important qualification to the model’s apparent simplicity.

Chapter 10 · 2017–2018

When the co-founder relationship breaks

A board’s obligations became larger than its bond with a founder.

The close relationship between Gurley and Kalanick did not move directly from mutual admiration to a lawsuit. In the intervening years, Gurley publicly warned about private valuations, abundant capital, and companies remaining private at unprecedented scale. The hosts read those warnings partly through Uber, while acknowledging that he was not naming it. By then the company’s valuation was at least around $60 billion; the conversation is uncertain about whether an $80 billion figure belonged to private expectations or the anticipated IPO.

The crisis also had several distinct threads. Uber accumulated ill will through its confrontational stance toward regulators and incumbents. The hosts regard #DeleteUber as a misunderstanding of an attempt to help, amplified by that accumulated distrust. They distinguish it sharply from Susan Fowler’s account of misconduct, where they say people at Uber were acting with malice. A stance that had helped build the company had not evolved with public expectations and the company’s growing consequences.

Paper valuations also reached far beyond a founder and a board member. University endowments and other LPs planned around reported fund values; employees, customers, drivers, and institutions all had exposure to the outcome. Rosenthal offers this as an interpretation of the pressure, not a single proven explanation for every decision. A board could no longer treat preserving one relationship as its overriding duty. Benchmark participated in replacing the CEO and sued Kalanick, accepting the possibility that other founders would regard it as a betrayal.

Gilbert is skeptical of the idea that litigation between parties to serious contracts is uniquely unthinkable. Both hosts also believe that, by the recording in 2022, there was no clear direct evidence that the lawsuit had meaningfully damaged Benchmark’s ability to win deals. Gilbert adds a necessary limit: outsiders cannot know which opportunities the firm never saw, or why. Competitors could still use the lawsuit against it.

The larger internal cost, in their view, was the strain on the partnership. They suspect the Uber experience helped bring the Fab Four era to an earlier end. That is their inference, not an established account of each partner’s retirement. Lasky and Cohler stepped back around fund nine in 2018; Gurley stayed for another cycle and had stepped back by the time of the episode. Fenton remained. The structure could pass on ownership. It could not make the work emotionally costless.

Chapter 11 · 2014–September 2022

The third partnership

The open question is whether a renewed team can find its own defining companies.

The next generation was already arriving before the previous one left. Eric Vishria joined in 2014 after an operating career that included Loudcloud and Opsware. The hosts describe an understated, courteous colleague with formidable competitive intensity. His first Benchmark investment was Confluent’s $7 million Series A at a $24 million post-money valuation. Amplitude, Benchling, and Cerebras were among his other investments. The company valuations mentioned in the episode belong to its 2022 recording, not to the present day.

Sarah Tavel joined in 2017, becoming Benchmark’s first woman investor and therefore its first woman general partner. At Bessemer she had sourced Pinterest, then joined Pinterest and helped build it before moving to Greylock. She combined investing experience, operating experience, consumer judgment, analytical frameworks, and public writing. Yet her first Benchmark investment was Chainalysis, outside a simple consumer-marketplace label. The hosts use it as an example of a non-consensus bet, while treating the reported $8.6 billion valuation as contemporary context.

Chetan Puttagunta arrived from NEA in 2018 with investments in Elastic, MuleSoft, and MongoDB behind him. Young but already tested, he fit the recruiting profile unusually closely. Miles Grimshaw later came from Thrive, where his work included Airtable and Benchling. Existing board relationships gave Benchmark partners a chance to see him at work: Fenton overlapped at Airtable and Vishria at Benchling. The September 2022 partnership consisted of Fenton, Vishria, Tavel, Puttagunta, and Grimshaw.

The hosts stop short of declaring another replication complete. Their opening question remains: can a third generation do what the first two did? The mechanism is in place, but the outcome still depends on the people and the opportunities available to them. A distinguished brand can attract talent. It can also create an incentive to protect prestige instead of making the strange consumer bets that once looked ridiculous.

Gilbert sees a team especially well equipped for enterprise investing, with Tavel the clearest consumer specialist. He wonders whether Benchmark will continue to define consumer categories or become more concentrated in high-performing enterprise software. Rosenthal points out that the consumer landscape itself is in an uncertain interval. Mobile supplied a great opening for the Fab Four; machine learning, augmented reality, or crypto might or might not supply another. They do not know. Their question is deliberately left in 2022, before the next partnership’s results can be judged.

Chapter 12 · The hosts’ concluding analysis

What the small firm can—and cannot—promise

A recognizable design emerged from experiments, reversals, and a willingness to hand it on.

The first power Gilbert and Rosenthal identify is counter-positioning. A senior investor at a hierarchical firm would have to surrender economics to copy Benchmark. More fundamentally, the firm might not have a set of partners who contributed equal value. Merely changing the pay split would not recreate the team. A new competitor could adopt equal economics and no associates, but it would still need an exceptional group willing to cooperate and perform. The hosts explicitly acknowledge that other firms have equal partnerships. The arrangement alone does not produce Benchmark’s returns.

Their second clear power is branding. Venture firms all supply dollars, but founders sometimes accept a lower valuation to work with Benchmark. The hosts hear this from entrepreneurs and believe the reputation has practical value: recruiting, judgment, access, and confidence from later investors. Gilbert’s claim that close to 100% of Benchmark companies can raise a Series B is a conversational estimate supporting that argument, not a measured statistic or a funding guarantee. The brand can make the same dollar more useful without costing Benchmark more to supply it.

Other firms respond by raising growth funds that can provide the next round themselves. Benchmark’s refusal to do that creates another kind of value. A founder can discuss a company with a board member who is not also the most important prospective buyer of the next financing. When the company is doing well, that member can help arrange the best outside round without competing to put more of the firm’s own capital into it. When it is struggling, the relationship need not become a continuous pitch for rescue funding. The hosts are careful that the conflict is reduced, not abolished: follow-on participation, possible bridges, and signaling still matter.

The small structure also protects time. There are no layers of investors to manage and no promotion ladder for the partners to climb. The work is current companies and prospective companies. Founders interviewed for the episode describe the intensity of that attention, and Gilbert is struck by how much unhurried time a Benchmark partner can make available. This is not evidence that services are unimportant. The partnership supplies service through senior people doing the work themselves rather than a large platform organization.

The hosts reject the idea that this is a rigid, thesis-driven machine. Partners follow compelling founders and learn across categories. They study misses, including by inviting founders of companies they did not back into conversations with the partnership. The learning matters because the firm has changed its mind repeatedly. International expansion, corporate partnerships, a billion-dollar fund, mezzanine investing, and a 2011 growth investment in Dropbox all complicate the image of one timeless rule. Even public-market ownership has a place: the episode notes that Benchmark held OpenTable after its IPO rather than automatically selling.

Gilbert therefore refines his own initial impression that Benchmark simply knows to sell around an IPO. He comes to see a value-oriented element in the decisions: hold a company that seems undervalued, sell when the price warrants it, and break the usual rule when the circumstances justify doing so. That is his interpretation of the pattern, not a disclosed mechanical selling formula. It helps explain why realized cash from eBay, Snap, Uber, and WeWork must be distinguished from headline paper values.

The all-star team remains both the attraction and the vulnerability. It can dedicate its attention to investing, but it cannot expand indefinitely while preserving everyone’s context, equal contribution, and trust. It must recruit carefully without a junior trial period. It must support a partner through uncertainty without accepting permanent underperformance. And it must let successful people leave without charging the future for the past. The hosts’ admiration rests on Benchmark having actually made those transitions, not merely advertised them.

They leave the growth-fund question open and the next consumer wave unresolved. They also retire their own ritual of grading episodes: a single grade adds little to a story containing two extraordinary funds, a failed expansion, and a painful founder confrontation. Their final view is not that one organizational design is best for every firm. It is that Benchmark’s particular people, incentives, and willingness to refound the partnership have twice made a deliberately small institution capable of very large outcomes. The third time remains a question.
