Acquired · Fall 2020

Airbnb

A few nights on an apartment floor became a global network. At the IPO, the hosts ask what that network can sustain.

A reading companion to Acquired, with Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal
Released December 11, 2020 · Created Unpublished
December 2020 perspective · About 28 minutes of reading

Chapter 01 · 2000–2007

Two designers, one room to fill

Before there was a travel marketplace, there were two friends looking for a life beyond their day jobs.

Joe Gebbia arrived at the Rhode Island School of Design from Georgia and befriended Brian Chesky, a student a year ahead of him from upstate New York. David introduces Brian as a hockey player and aspiring bodybuilder, Joe as the slighter, scrappier friend. Both were interested in making things and starting businesses. Joe became student body president; Brian was chosen to give his class’s graduation speech. The story told to author Brad Stone has Joe taking Brian to dinner afterward and predicting that they would one day start a company together and somebody would write a book about it. David treats the neatly prophetic anecdote with appropriate doubt: it is, after all, being told to the man writing that book.

After graduating in 2004, Brian moved to Los Angeles and joined the product-design consultancy 3D-ID. Designing chairs and medical products as a junior employee did not feel like the future he wanted. The rise of YouTube caught his attention: young founders had made something on the internet that people everywhere could use. He researched the company and its backers and began imagining a different life for himself. Joe graduated the following year and tried selling CritBuns, portable foam cushions shaped to make the long, uncomfortable design critiques at RISD more bearable. The product supplied an early outlet for his design and sales instincts, but it did not become a large business.

Joe moved to San Francisco in 2006, first interning and then working at Chronicle Books, designing packaging and marketing materials. He lived with roommates in the South of Market neighborhood. In 2007 he sent Brian a CritBun in Los Angeles. As the story goes, the package carried an invitation: take another shot at doing something together. Brian visited, enjoyed himself, returned to Los Angeles to think, and finally quit his job. In September he moved into the room being vacated by Joe’s roommate Nate. That solved the vacancy and created a more pressing problem. Nate had been a programmer with a paycheck. Brian was now a designer without one, and the rent still had to be paid.

The World Design Congress was coming to San Francisco the following month. Hotel rooms were scarce and expensive, a recurring problem in a city full of conferences. Joe emailed Brian a proposal for a ‘designer’s bed and breakfast’: wireless internet, a little desk space, a sleeping mat and breakfast for visiting designers. They built airbedandbreakfast.com on WordPress in three days and pitched design blogs. The hosts are unsure whether the first sleeping arrangements were mats or inflatable beds, and whether two or three guests ultimately stayed. One was a recent Arizona State graduate from India named Amal, paying $80 a night. They went to the conference together, explored the city and became friends.

Amal wanted to visit Stanford’s d.school, so Brian and Joe went with him and attended a lecture by IDEO co-founder Bill Moggridge. Brian promptly pitched becoming the accommodation provider for an industry association. Nothing came of that pitch. More surprisingly, the founders did not emerge from the weekend certain that this was their company. They treated it as a successful way to make rent and resumed looking for their real idea. Ben asks whether the original site already let anyone host. David thinks it served only their own apartment, while acknowledging that he is not completely sure. There were no other hosts at that first conference.

Chapter 02 · 2007–winter 2009

Launching again, staying alive

The idea survived less because the early numbers were persuasive than because the founders kept finding another way to try.

Nate Blecharczyk rejoined the friends’ brainstorming. He had studied computer science at Harvard, but David stresses an earlier education: teaching himself to code in high school, writing AOL bots and selling software, then consulting in what the hosts bluntly call spam. Nate told Stone that this business made him almost $1 million and helped pay his way through Harvard. David connects its closure to the arrival of the CAN-SPAM Act in 2003 without claiming to know the undisclosed reasons. The important combination was a developer who could build internet products and understood online distribution. Initially the three considered permanent roommate matching, only to find an existing competitor and little conviction in the idea.

In January 2008, Brian and Joe returned to AirBed and Breakfast and pitched Nate on building it. South by Southwest in Austin offered the same shortage of rooms that had produced their first weekend. They contacted people on Craigslist who were offering or seeking accommodation. The result was two bookings, one of them Brian’s. In Austin, Brian met another of Joe’s former roommates, Michael Seibel, then building Justin.tv. Seibel liked the founders and offered to introduce them to angel investors. He and Justin Kan followed through without, according to David, taking advisory equity. But the introductions did not overcome investors’ disbelief about the product and its tiny usage.

Nate meanwhile moved back to Boston to be with his girlfriend Elizabeth, then in medical school. Progress stalled again. Seibel and Kan suggested Y Combinator. Brian and Joe visited Startup School in spring 2008, where speakers including Jeff Bezos and Sequoia’s Greg McAdoo made an impression. While waiting for the next YC application cycle, they aimed at another event: the presidential conventions. Local press outreach helped produce roughly 100 bookings that summer. It was progress, but not enough money to sustain them.

Their response was not a change to the booking product. They designed limited-edition cereal boxes, Obama O’s and Cap’n McCain’s, filled them with cereal and sold them online. Ben admits that he had misunderstood this famous story: they were not reselling an existing novelty cereal at a markup; the packaging was their own design. David puts the resulting profit at $20,000–$30,000. It bought survival. Both hosts resist turning the episode into proof of inevitable success. Scrappiness matters, but selling something unrelated to the company can also be the last profitable act of a business that never works.

They missed YC’s application deadline, and Seibel lobbied Paul Graham to grant them an interview anyway. Nate flew back from Boston to appear with the team. In the version told in The Upstarts, Graham was unconvinced by strangers sleeping in each other’s homes. Joe had brought cereal boxes, forgot to hand them over and ran back after the interview. The explanation of how they had kept themselves alive changed Graham’s view of the founders. He might not understand the market, but these people would not easily die. They entered the winter 2009 batch. Graham asked where bookings were happening, heard New York, and told them to go there. Meeting hosts, improving photographs and making payment smoother began turning an improbable proposition into a usable service. During YC, AirBed and Breakfast became Airbnb.

The episode opens with a deliberate false start: a company connecting travelers with strangers, using photos, reviews and identity verification, sounds like Airbnb but is actually CouchSurfing. David argues that its missing element was facilitated payment. A community based on free hospitality could build trust, yet lacked the financial exchange that made hosting compelling to a much larger supply of people. Airbnb did not invent every trust mechanism. Its combination of those mechanisms with payment and economic incentives is the distinction the hosts want listeners to notice.

Chapter 03 · 2009–2010

A small check buys a large stake

The financial crisis made investors cautious and made spare rooms newly useful.

Greg McAdoo was Sequoia’s liaison with YC, which Sequoia had backed. Talking with Graham about resilience, he asked which founders in the batch best embodied it. Graham pointed to ‘the Airbnbs.’ McAdoo had already examined HomeAway, Vrbo and vacation rentals; he saw something different here. The timing cut both ways. The financial crisis had venture firms conserving cash and protecting existing investments. It had also left homeowners and tenants struggling to cover housing costs. A platform that let them earn money from spare capacity met a real need. Travelers still wanted to go places and had less money to spend. An $80 or $100 room could open a trip that a conference-priced hotel would have closed.

McAdoo persuaded a skeptical partnership to make a small seed investment rather than a conventional Series A. Sequoia invested $585,000; Keith Rabois, Kevin Hartz and YouTube co-founder Jawed Karim collectively supplied another $30,000. The $615,000 round valued the company at $2.4 million after the investment. Sequoia obtained 24⅜% and the angels 1¼%. The distinction between the check and the ownership is central to the hosts’ amazement. A tiny allocation from a venture fund could still buy a consequential share of an enormous outcome. Later Airbnb rounds would raise far more dollars while selling a much smaller percentage of the company.

Nate’s distribution skills now had a product worth distributing. The Craigslist story had two directions. Airbnb approached people already listing accommodation there, but also encouraged Airbnb hosts to publish their listings back to Craigslist. That second direction supplied demand to a site that initially had little traffic. A traveler could discover a room on Craigslist, transact through Airbnb and become an Airbnb customer for the next trip. Ben describes Airbnb as capturing some of the value that Craigslist created without itself capturing much of it.

The team also used Google search advertising to reach travelers looking for places to stay and early Facebook interest targeting to recruit supply in specific cities. An ad could invite a wine lover to rent to another wine lover, or a yoga enthusiast to host someone with the same interest. David dryly notes that there was no guarantee the eventual guest actually shared it. The tactics worked in an unusually open moment for those channels. Ben warns listeners in 2020 against treating them as a current recipe: the easy value in those forms of advertising had long since been competed away.

By mid-2010 the platform had booked 700,000 nights. Greylock’s Reid Hoffman led a $7.2 million Series A at roughly a $60 million–$70 million valuation, about a tenth of the company. David says Airbnb then had more money in the bank than the total seed dollars it had raised. That is a statement about cash in the bank, not proof of accounting profit. The hosts’ deeper point is that the business did not need repeated, heavily dilutive financing just to keep its basic transactions moving. And beyond cheap accommodation, something more durable was appearing: families and groups sometimes wanted a home, a neighborhood and space together rather than hotel rooms.

Chapter 04 · 2011–2014

The network crosses the Atlantic

A traveler could become a host at home. A local listing could serve a guest from almost anywhere.

Airbnb reached a million cumulative nights in early 2011 and made the milestone national news. That summer it raised $112 million at a valuation above $1 billion, with Andreessen Horowitz leading the Series B. The urgency was Europe. Rocket Internet and the Samwer brothers were cloning internet companies and had launched Wimdu as an Airbnb competitor. The hosts argue that Airbnb could not treat Europe as a market it could safely concede. Travel crosses borders: the same guest who books in one country wants choice in the next, and guests returning from trips can become hosts in their own cities. The network was global in a way a city’s food-delivery or ride-hailing network was not.

Airbnb acquired smaller European competitors, opened offices and pursued listings aggressively. Wimdu assembled a Berlin operation to call hosts and property managers; Airbnb undertook similar work. David does not think listing acquisition alone explains the result. A directory full of unreviewed properties has little life in it. Airbnb already had real stays, reviews and trust developing across the network. That history mattered when a traveler was considering a stranger’s house or a host was considering a stranger as a guest. His interpretation is that these living, reviewed listings were harder to reproduce than the listing count. Ben calls it a flywheel. The hosts credit Airbnb with winning this contest, rather than presenting mere fundraising as the cause of victory.

There was another unusually favorable mechanism. Guests paid when they booked, often weeks or months ahead. Airbnb paid hosts after check-in. In between it held cash that included both its eventual fee and money owed to the host. David gives roughly 12%–15% as a fee range in this discussion. The remaining booking money was not Airbnb revenue or profit. While bookings grew, new cash arrived ahead of older payouts. This negative cash conversion cycle helped finance expansion and reduced the need to sell more ownership. Ben emphasizes the condition: spending against this timing advantage becomes dangerous if the business shrinks.

Five million cumulative nights by January 2012 became ten million by June. The hosts briefly acknowledge serious failures along this growth path: the vandalism of a San Francisco host’s apartment, known as the EJ incident, prompted an insurance guarantee; accidents and crimes also occurred. New York and San Francisco challenged a service that could function like an unlicensed hotel business. The episode does not give detailed histories of those incidents, and its rapid growth narrative does not make them harmless. Trust was a product requirement with real consequences when it failed.

Founders Fund invested $200 million at a $3 billion valuation in 2013. TPG led $500 million at $10 billion the next year. Greg McAdoo left Sequoia and Alfred Lin, formerly of Zappos, joined the board. A powerful network, favorable cash timing and abundant financing had created the sort of business founders dream about. The hosts then turn the lens: those strengths also reduced the pressure to operate precisely. Airbnb could keep growing while developing expensive habits and pursuing ideas whose contribution was hard to establish.

Chapter 05 · 2014–2016

From a room to all of travel

The company’s vision widened faster than the hosts believe its evidence did.

In 2014 Airbnb moved into 888 Brannan Street in San Francisco, with a five-story atrium, a living wall and an elaborate free-food operation. It unveiled the Bélo brand mark and began Airbnb Open, a conference for hosts. Chip Conley, the boutique-hotel founder hired as head of hospitality, was quoted predicting a Nobel Peace Prize within a decade. Ben understands the wish to build a workplace and community that enabled creativity. David finds the ambition easier to question. Alfred Lin’s observation, quoted in the episode, captures their concern: spectacular growth can cover many sins.

The underlying numbers were large. In 2015 Airbnb generated almost $1 billion of net revenue on $8 billion of bookings. ‘Net revenue’ here means the company’s revenue from the transactions, not net income. In 2016 bookings reached $14 billion and net revenue $1.65 billion. The hosts describe growth around 80% from the prior-year base. Airbnb raised $1.5 billion at a $25 billion valuation in 2015 and another $1.5 billion across two rounds in 2016. Investors wanted access, terms were favorable and the company could accumulate a war chest without the dilution its earliest round had imposed.

At the 2016 Airbnb Open in Los Angeles, Brian presented what he called the most significant development in the company’s history. Homes would sit alongside Experiences and Places inside a broader product called Trips. Places would help with restaurants, activities and local discovery, while Trips would organize the travel experience. Car rentals, groceries, flights and even an airline appeared in the discussion of what might follow. Ben and David admire the scale of the ambition but, watching the presentation again in 2020, see little evidence of successful expansion. Places and the Trips concept were gone. Experiences remained, but the S-1 did not disclose its bookings or revenue separately; the hosts therefore treat its small size as an assumption, not a published segment result.

Their disagreement is less with the value of a home than with the premise that most customers want the full community vision. Management stressed belonging and relationships between hosts and guests. The hosts think many customers were making a transactional choice among places to stay. They describe their own progression: Airbnb first felt much cheaper than hotels, then became a way to find more interesting neighborhoods and homes at similar prices, and eventually became one option among several. For a couple on a short trip, a good hotel could be easier. For a family gathering, a group vacation or a place with poor hotel inventory, the home still had a powerful advantage.

That distinction matters to the story. A company can invent a valuable category without owning every reason a person travels. The hosts’ criticism is that Airbnb’s success at the initial product did not establish that it would execute well at every adjacent one. They later contrast it with Amazon’s record of testing, abandoning and scaling new businesses. Their 2020 assessment is severe, but they explicitly credit the original product’s integrated innovations: payment, messaging, reciprocal reviews and, after the beginning, Instant Book. Those made the core experience work.

Chapter 06 · 2017–2019

Growth slows; the bills do not

The wait to go public exposed different clocks for founders, employees and investors.

In 2017 Airbnb told investors it expected to go public within twelve months. Early in 2018 its CFO Laurence Tosi departed. Tosi had joined in 2015 after serving as CFO of Blackstone and COO of Merrill Lynch. Brian wrote about an infinite time horizon and building a 22nd-century company. The hosts hear a message that an IPO was no longer imminent. Ben speculates about tension over readiness and financial guidance, rather than claiming to know the private argument. David points out the structural tension: a founder can think indefinitely, but venture funds have life cycles and their investors eventually need liquidity.

The growth figures were still impressive in isolation, yet moving in the wrong direction for the valuation story. Booking growth slowed to about 50% in 2017, around 40% in 2018 and below 30% in 2019. David places particular weight on the dollar additions: Airbnb added roughly $8.5 billion in booking value in each of 2018 and 2019. The base had grown, but the annual increment had stopped growing with it. Ben notes that a mature public company growing 40% would not normally invite this degree of skepticism; the long wait had kept Airbnb framed as a high-growth private technology company.

More troubling to David, spending behaved as though rapid expansion would continue. In 2019 total expenses rose 46%, variable costs 41% and fixed costs 60%, against booking growth around 28%–29%. Ben floats the possibility that the company was shifting toward profitability for an IPO. David rejects that explanation because the costs were increasing faster, not being brought into line. This exchange preserves their question rather than smoothing it into a single retrospective judgment. Airbnb had a powerful product and a cost structure that was not yet converting its scale into bottom-line profit.

In September 2019 Airbnb formally announced a 2020 public offering. Early employee options were approaching expiration, and long-standing investors were well into their fund lives. The hosts do not know the precise option-contract mechanics and say so; the broader liquidity pressure is their point. At the end of 2019, Airbnb had $38 billion in gross bookings and $5.3 billion in revenue, with a $700 million net loss. A positive cash-flow story could coexist with that loss because receiving guests’ money before paying hosts changes the timing of cash. It does not erase expenses or turn all cash held into earnings.

Chapter 07 · March–December 2020

When the cash cycle runs backward

The advantage that helped fund growth became a liability when travel stopped.

COVID-19 arrived before the planned IPO. In March and April 2020, Airbnb’s gross booking value turned negative: it refunded more future reservations than it received in new bookings. David is explicit that this is negative bookings, not a claim that reported revenue itself became negative. The payment system had previously brought cash forward. Now cancellations pulled it back out just as incoming demand disappeared. Ben likens the mechanism to leverage: it amplifies good conditions and can rapidly amplify bad ones.

Airbnb raised $2 billion in emergency capital from Silver Lake and Sixth Street Partners. The hosts describe a combination of equity and debt, two debt tranches at interest rates of 11½% and 9%, and an equity valuation of $18 billion. They are uncertain about the exact equity-versus-debt split, so a neat billion-and-billion allocation would overstate their knowledge. Their interpretation is clear: a company accustomed to abundant, cheap capital had accepted distress terms and a substantial valuation haircut because it badly needed cash. Fixed operating costs added a second form of leverage when revenue fell.

In May the company laid off 25% of its staff and cut $800 million in marketing spending. Ben objects that the reductions fell heavily on customer service while research and development was largely protected. Brian called the reset a ‘second founding.’ The sprawling ambitions narrowed: Places, Trips, a film studio called Rausch Street Films and other projects disappeared; Experiences continued, including online offerings. Ben also recalls a magazine, which David regards more sympathetically as understandable travel marketing. The conversation is about where management chose to spend and cut, rather than a claim that every activity beyond bookings had identical merit.

Then demand partly recovered in a different form. The hosts had themselves used Airbnbs for longer stays and trips where a hotel lobby felt unattractive during the pandemic. Ben recalls a period when Airbnb was down roughly half while hotels were down far more, but cannot specify the month; his personal experience is an illustration, not a precise market series. Across the first nine months of 2020 gross bookings were down 39% from the corresponding 2019 period. August bookings were down 14% year over year and September 17%. These August and September comparisons are year over year. The recovery remained incomplete, and its post-vaccine trajectory was unknown.

Airbnb filed its S-1 on November 16. On December 9 it priced shares at $68, raising about $3.5 billion at a roughly $47 billion market capitalization. During the December 10 recording, the hosts watch an opening price of $146 and trading around $159, implying a valuation above $100 billion. The official episode was released December 11. These are successive IPO and live-market snapshots, not interchangeable company valuations. The founders retained about 31% collectively going into the IPO: Brian around 11%, Joe and Nate about 10% each. That ownership became enormous paper wealth, not cash earnings of the business.

Ben estimates that the company would have a roughly $7 billion cash chest after the raise, discussing existing cash and marketable securities as well as new proceeds. Sequoia’s early ownership and later investments produced extraordinary paper gains, with many other investors and employees also benefiting. The hosts do not know all lock-up or selling-shareholder details. Their astonishment at the trading price opens the question that governs the remaining discussion: a great company and an excellent historical use of capital can still be difficult to justify at a particular public-market price.

Chapter 08 · The IPO debate

An extraordinary brand, an uncertain runway

The bull case rests on a category Airbnb helped create. The bear case asks how much more of it Airbnb can capture.

Ben’s strongest bull case is Airbnb’s distinctive supply, built over a decade, and the brand that brings people directly back to it. The hosts cite 91% direct or organic traffic. That is not a claim that all traffic is costless: Ben acknowledges brand advertising. It is a remarkable contrast with online travel agencies that often pay again to reacquire a traveler on each trip. He cites Expedia and Booking together spending about $11 billion a year on Google advertising. Airbnb had established a repeated customer relationship while cultivating accommodation that people did not simply think of as a generic hotel inventory search.

David adds two possibilities. Pandemic habits might persist in ways favorable to homes, and travel is undeniably a large market. Airbnb’s cited total addressable market was $3.4 trillion: $1.8 trillion in short-term stays, $210 billion in stays longer than 28 days and $1.4 trillion in experiences, rounded figures that explain management’s interest in the latter. The hosts focus on short-term stays. Compared with that claimed opportunity, $38 billion of bookings left apparent room. Yet the opportunity figure was a company estimate, not a guarantee that Airbnb could access it or that all travel spending suited its product.

The bear case begins at exactly that tension. Why was growth slowing before COVID if the available market was so large? David thinks Airbnb had lost the large price advantage that once made it an almost automatic choice. At comparable prices, people could rationally alternate between hotels and homes. The reachable slice of travel might be smaller than the broad market implied; or Airbnb might be failing to reach people who could benefit. The hosts leave both explanations open. Post-pandemic preferences might improve the picture, but that was still a forecast in December 2020.

Supply could also become less unique. Ben had put his own listing on Vrbo that year. He found its host product markedly worse, yet once set up, it provided another source of demand. David had barely listed his own home during the year but expected that he too would use multiple platforms if he resumed traveling. Third-party host software made coordinating calendars and listings easier. Hosts wanted higher revenue, fewer empty nights and protection from a platform decision that might suddenly block their livelihood. Airbnb had helped create an ecosystem, the hosts acknowledge, even if some useful host tools came from companies outside Airbnb.

Their host perspective exposes an incentive conflict in smart pricing. Airbnb benefits from another booked night and another fee. A host may face cleaning work, labor, risk and disruption that make a very cheap reservation undesirable. Maximizing marketplace liquidity is not automatically the same as maximizing a host’s preferred income or experience. Ben also worries about increasingly impersonal service and policies. These are the hosts’ assessments of the product and their own use, not a measured finding that every host had become dissatisfied.

Finally, David compares Airbnb with eBay: a broad marketplace with powerful early network effects that can still lose categories to focused competitors. Hipcamp might serve camping better; Outdoorsy could supply RV-specific features. The question is how much new demand would remain with Airbnb rather than niche services. Ben imagines cleaning and check-in services that might make staying exclusively with Airbnb more compelling, comparing the potential with Amazon’s seller services and fulfillment. He presents those as missing possibilities, not features Airbnb already possessed. Their skepticism about adjacencies does not erase their admiration for the original system.

Chapter 09 · Power and financial comparison

What the network can—and cannot—protect

A strong marketplace does not make every listing exclusive, every traveler loyal or every dollar of bookings revenue.

Using Hamilton Helmer’s Seven Powers framework, David identifies network economies as the obvious source of durable advantage. Guests care about varied, relevant homes in the places and price ranges they want; hosts care about guests who might book. The value crosses geography. Unlike a network where only a small group of friends matters, a traveler can value many different listings without knowing their owners. David calls it among the strongest two-sided networks he has seen. The hosts’ argument is about choice and density that matter to participants, rather than the raw number of accounts.

Both also identify early counter-positioning against hotels, but say it has weakened. Existing homes could supply incremental nights without incurring the entire cost of creating hotel capacity. Even if a hotel operator did not own the building, somebody still bore the construction, lease and operating economics. This helped Airbnb offer cheaper stays without adopting that whole cost system. They do not award it a cornered resource or strong switching costs. Hosts can list elsewhere; guests can book elsewhere. David allows some lock-in from review histories, especially when the concept was unfamiliar, but sees it as less decisive now that travelers trust competing platforms.

Ben then distinguishes a marketplace that assigns a largely interchangeable provider from one that assists a customer in choosing a particular merchant. With Uber, a rider generally wants a suitable nearby car. On Airbnb, the traveler chooses a specific home and experiences the host as the merchant. Ben argues that the latter structure limits how much of the transaction Airbnb can take, relative to the 30%-plus rates he discusses for ride-sharing. David agrees. This is their explanation of marketplace economics, not a promise of an immutable ceiling or a claim that Airbnb supplies no services beyond discovery.

The 2019 comparison with Booking makes the financial distinctions concrete. Airbnb’s $38 billion of gross bookings yielded $5.3 billion in revenue: an effective take rate of 13.9% when simply dividing the annual totals. It nevertheless recorded a $700 million net loss. Booking had $96 billion in gross travel bookings and $15 billion of revenue, a 15.7% effective take rate, and about $5 billion of net income. Ben cautions that the Booking booking total may include flights, which contribute little commission compared with hotels. These are simplified company-level comparisons, not perfectly matched unit economics.

Booking was about two and a half times the booking scale and substantially larger in revenue, yet it also spent heavily to acquire customers and still earned substantial profit. Airbnb’s timing advantage could produce positive cash flow while its income statement showed a loss. Ben recalls an average lead time around 36 days before the pandemic and roughly 24 during it, as approximate figures rather than exact contract terms. The question is whether Airbnb could turn greater scale into Booking-like profitability when recent expenses had risen faster than its gross profit base. Having a better-known brand or more direct traffic did not answer that operational question.

In their playbook discussion the hosts return to the value created: people could afford trips they otherwise could not take, and hosts could cover rent or mortgages through difficult periods. David describes expanding the price-quality frontier, giving customers more quality for a given price. They also stress the international business: about 43% of nights were in Europe against 29% in North America, while revenue was roughly even between those regions. The cash-timing advantage belonged with these strengths, but it arose partly because Airbnb introduced a new category and could establish payment terms different from a hotel reservation.

Chapter 10 · The closing analysis

A valuable business, with work left to do

The final grade becomes more qualified as the hosts separate a historic investment outcome from a sustainable operating business.

Ben closes the playbook with vulnerabilities that direct traffic can conceal. Guest-cohort spending fell sharply after the first year, to roughly the thirty-something-percent range in year two, with the hope of recovering toward half of the original spend. He is discussing the spending of a cohort, not the percentage of users retained. Travel may happen only once a year, and Airbnb need not win every trip, but those patterns leave less room for expensive performance marketing than a cohort whose spending expands every year. The company had not developed the same precision in paid acquisition that competitors dependent on it had been forced to learn.

Reviews had enabled trust without eliminating inconsistency. Ben found searching through listings laborious and regarded Airbnb Plus and Superhost as inadequate signals of dependable quality. David similarly thought many reviews conveyed little. These are pointed personal judgments from the recording. Ben wanted host-churn data, which the S-1 did not provide, before deciding how much deteriorating host experience might matter. The missing measure remains missing here. The hosts had no evidence that would justify turning their concern into a quantified rate of hosts abandoning the service.

They then ask about value destruction as well as value creation. Ben quotes a finding reported in a Harvard Business Review article that attributed about one-fifth of the average annual rise in U.S. rents to Airbnb’s growth, through absentee landlords shifting housing from long-term rental or sale into short-term stays. This companion reports what Ben cited in the episode; it does not import an independent housing study. He explicitly declines to arbitrate the company’s total net social impact. His concern is that the benevolent brand may have outrun the evidence about benefits after housing costs are included.

David distinguishes people renting the home they actually live in from property managers converting residential stock into a hotel-like business. He finds it difficult to see comparable housing harm in the former case. The latter is much grayer. He had seen estimates above 50% for the property-management or hotel-like use case, but does not trust the interested parties’ figures enough to establish the share. Ben likewise notes how advocacy and company-funded sources can disagree. The episode leaves the proportion and the net impact unsettled. It recognizes income for hosts and opportunity for guests alongside costs that may fall on people who never use the platform.

For the final grade, David initially calls the use of capital an A+: a $585,000 seed investment had helped create a remarkably valuable company with favorable business dynamics. Ben shifts the test away from the day’s share price. Could the roughly $3 billion invested before the emergency financing eventually generate persistent returns and cash for shareholders? He thinks so. Despite slowing growth, competition and his skepticism about a $100 billion valuation, he can imagine a profitable steady state with less research, development and marketing expense. That earns an A in his view, rather than an A+.

David accepts the distinction and moves to A. The eBay comparison remains favorable as a description of a valuable, durable marketplace; it does not place Airbnb alongside the best executors of new businesses. He thinks the company should already have been generating far more cash with a leaner structure and fewer speculative projects.
