The missing browser after the perfect launch

Acquired reading companion · Spring 2024

Microsoft Volume IIThe enterprise beneath the headlines

Browser wars, antitrust and consumer setbacks obscured another transformation: Microsoft learned to sell an integrated enterprise system, then began building the cloud that would change its future.

Released July 22, 2024 · Created October 19, 2026
About 35 minutes to read · Complete episode history and concluding analysis
Based on Acquired: Microsoft Volume II, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal.

01

1995, looking back to 1993 · 00:05:00

The missing browser after the perfect launch

A celebrated consumer product was about to meet a different kind of platform.

Windows 95 had arrived with Jay Leno, the Rolling Stones and a Start button that helped make the personal computer approachable. Bill Gates was still Microsoft’s chief executive, Steve Ballmer was his indispensable business partner, and the company occupied the center of the PC industry. Yet the retail operating system initially lacked an internet browser.

For consumers only a little earlier, going online often meant AOL, Prodigy or CompuServe. These were managed services, with content and access controlled by the operator. Publishing something there was different from placing a website on an open network.

Microsoft had explored buying AOL in 1993. When that did not happen, it developed Project Marvel, a proprietary online service that became the original MSN. The later internet property reused the name after the strategy changed; the two should not be treated as one continuous conception of the web.

Ben and David begin with that uncertainty because the familiar rise-fall-revival narrative makes the period seem simpler than it was. Microsoft would lose important consumer opportunities, but it would also transform enterprise computing and begin Azure well before Nadella became CEO. The episode follows those simultaneous stories through the 2014 leadership transition.

02

1993–1994 · 00:09:25

The future seemed to belong to the cable companies

An existing network looked more plausible than an entirely new consumer medium.

Interactive television was the information-superhighway vision. Cable companies already had screens in homes, physical connections, subscribers and content relationships. It seemed reasonable that software and more powerful set-top boxes would make those assets interactive.

Microsoft explored an alliance informally dubbed CableSoft. It would provide software for consumer equipment and back-end servers, with a project called Tiger. Silicon Graphics and the media companies belonged to the same imagined future. Bill spent time around figures such as John Malone and Gerald Levin, while Microsoft considered how content fitted its ambitions.

This was not simply stupidity exposed by hindsight. Even Jim Clark and Marc Andreessen initially considered a television-centered opportunity when they began working together. The internet’s growth was visible, but translating it into a commercial future still required a conceptual leap.

The hosts use the changing editions of Bill’s book The Road Ahead as a time capsule. The first emphasized the information superhighway; the revised edition gave the internet far more attention. Publishing schedules had lagged behind a market moving at internet speed. Microsoft’s advantage would depend on whether it could change direction faster than its public description of the future.

03

January–April 1994 · 00:14:19

Two memos bring the internet into focus

A technical proposal and a snowed-in campus revealed the same growth.

J Allard’s memo, Windows: The Next Killer Application for the Internet, pointed to Mosaic and its rapid adoption. He argued that Microsoft should embrace the behavior and extend internet capabilities into Windows. The hosts distinguish his words from the later hostile phrase embrace, extend, extinguish.

Steven Sinofsky then visited Cornell, his alma mater, on a recruiting trip and was delayed by snow. Students were using the network for ordinary social and practical life: messages, flirting and registration, not only scientific exchange. He wrote Cornell Is Wired! to convey how different that was from the older academic internet.

Bill took the observation seriously. An executive offsite in April 1994 helped turn internet growth into a company priority. The hosts connect this to Microsoft’s original sensitivity to exponential phenomena: something small today could become the dominant environment sooner than ordinary intuition suggested.

The shift was not an edict followed by an otherwise idle organization. Teams were already investigating protocols, applications and interfaces. The memos made some of that work strategically legible. Their importance was in recognizing an existing change in behavior and giving Microsoft permission to reorganize around it.

04

1994 · 00:20:19

The browser company was not just a browser business

A free client could create demand for commercial server software.

Jim Clark contacted Marc Andreessen after leaving Silicon Graphics. Their initial television idea gave way to the web browser, and the company that became Netscape built a commercial product using the experience Andreessen had gained on Mosaic.

The hosts carefully separate code and names. The new company’s browser was newly written; it was not simply the university’s original Mosaic carried into a startup. The university licensed the older work through Spyglass, and disputes over naming and commercialization contributed to the Netscape name.

Giving away Navigator could bring users onto a common client while Netscape sold server software to organizations publishing on the web. That made browser adoption more than a consumer popularity contest. Control of the client could influence which server capabilities customers valued.

Microsoft, meanwhile, needed a way to participate quickly. A possible license from BookLink disappeared when AOL bought that company. Netscape was not offering the arrangement Microsoft wanted. Spyglass remained a route to code, but buying access to it would prove less straightforward than the neat story of purchasing a ready-made Internet Explorer.

05

1994–1995 · 00:29:57

Internet Explorer was built into a broader technical vision

The engineering and the defensive strategy grew together.

Thomas Reardon and colleagues looked beyond the Windows 95 release toward an internet-native successor. They wanted HTTP support, reusable HTML rendering and perhaps a shell whose visual surfaces behaved more like the web. A browser application would sit on top of building blocks other programs could also use.

That matters to the later legal story. The team was not only stapling a standalone application onto Windows. It was also integrating shared capabilities into the operating environment. At the same time, this architecture would make it harder to separate the browser cleanly when regulators demanded it.

The Spyglass license supplied material to work with, but the code had already changed substantially from the university version. Microsoft spent considerable effort undoing and rebuilding parts. The hosts compare it with the DOS starting point: purchasing a foundation did not mean purchasing the complete later product.

Bill’s Internet Tidal Wave memo in 1995 made the strategic urgency explicit. The internet might become an application platform in its own right. Microsoft had to make Windows useful in that world, while preventing the platform’s value from moving somewhere it did not control.

06

1995–1998 · 00:36:35

Make the potential rival’s distribution channel compete with it

Netscape reached users through the very platform it threatened.

Netscape’s rapid adoption and IPO made the web’s commercial importance unmistakable. Andreessen’s comment about reducing Windows to a set of device drivers described the threat: applications could run through a browser across operating systems, weakening the reasons users and developers needed Windows specifically.

Internet Explorer initially appeared in the separately sold Plus Pack. That could not achieve the ubiquity Microsoft wanted against a freely available browser. In December 1995 Bill announced IE would be free and bundled with Windows going forward.

Microsoft also secured distribution through online services. The hosts trace IE’s rising share and Netscape’s deteriorating position, while explaining why losing the client damaged the server business too. They describe a dependency becoming dangerous: a rival relying on Windows to reach customers now faced a Windows owner determined to compete.

The episode’s sweeping language about dominance is an interpretation of the period, not a precise claim that literally every browser user had switched. Microsoft had defended its existing licensing business rather than discovered the web’s eventual advertising economics. Winning this battle would not guarantee that it understood how the next industry would make money.

07

1997–1998 · 00:44:35

The Apple rescue also served the browser war

A famous investment belonged to a larger bargain.

At Macworld in 1997, Steve Jobs announced a partnership with Microsoft, with Bill appearing by satellite. The familiar pieces were a hundred-fifty-million-dollar investment and a five-year commitment to Office for Mac. Continued access to Office mattered deeply to buyers considering an Apple computer.

The other pieces were also consequential. The companies would settle patent disputes, and Internet Explorer would become the default Mac browser. The hosts see a negotiation in which Apple needed reassurance and software continuity while Microsoft wanted browser distribution and a more cooperative public posture.

They recount Jobs using the possibility of additional intellectual-property disputes as leverage. That account is presented as the episode’s explanation of the deal, not independently reconstructed private dialogue. The bargain served both sides rather than being only a charitable rescue or only a browser transaction.

AOL acquired Netscape in an all-stock deal in 1998. By then the hosts see Microsoft near its peak of consumer platform power. The same behavior that looked like effective defense to Microsoft could look like an incumbent cutting off competition to everyone else. The episode now turns from commercial success to what the law actually required.

08

1990–1997 legal background · 00:54:56

A product or a feature?

An earlier settlement left a boundary that software kept moving.

The antitrust history began before the famous 1998 trial. An FTC investigation ended in a deadlock, after which the Justice Department pursued the matter. Microsoft settled in 1994 under a consent decree addressing restrictive practices and tying application products to Windows.

The hosts focus on a difficult boundary: Microsoft could add features to its operating system, but could not make buying a separate application a condition of buying Windows. Was a browser a separate product, or a capability a modern operating system should include?

Both motives could coexist. Integrated internet functions were useful to customers, and Microsoft also wanted to preserve its platform against a threat. The hosts do not treat consumer benefit as proof that no exclusionary conduct occurred, even while they remain skeptical about some of the government’s case.

In 1997 the DOJ argued that bundling IE violated the earlier decree. Microsoft insisted the code could not simply be removed. An ordered browser-free version proved unusable in the account the hosts describe, provoking further conflict. The episode’s comparisons to double or triple jeopardy express Microsoft’s frustration; they are not adopted here as a legal finding that criminal double-jeopardy rules applied.

09

1998–1999 · 01:02:40

A new case and a disastrous deposition

Refusing to concede anything became a public-relations liability.

Microsoft appealed the browser order and initially won room to keep shipping integrated software. The DOJ then brought a much broader Sherman Act case in May 1998. The questions expanded beyond one bundle to whether Microsoft had monopoly power and had used it unlawfully to maintain that position.

Bill’s deposition was conducted under a pretrial expectation that testimony would appear in written form. The strategy was to give as little usable ground as possible. The hosts describe prolonged arguments over wording, distinctions between emails and memoranda, and answers that looked evasive when seen rather than merely read.

The judge subsequently permitted video excerpts. David Boies used them throughout the trial, affecting both how Microsoft’s witnesses were received and how the press portrayed the company. The hosts do not know every reason for the procedural change or why Microsoft did not challenge it more effectively.

This is a story about legal strategy and credibility as much as software design. Employees who believed they were making useful products now saw their company represented as an abusive monopolist. That did not answer the legal questions, but it changed the experience of working there and the public meaning attached to its success.

10

1999–2002 · 01:12:20

The breakup order did not become the final outcome

Liability, remedy and the cost of the process were different questions.

Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson found Microsoft held monopoly power, describing the barrier created by its users and applications. The later district-court judgment found unlawful conduct and ordered the company divided into an operating-system business and an applications business.

The proposed separation would also have forced Bill and Steve into different companies and required divestment of conflicting holdings. For a substantial period, employees and outsiders expected a breakup. A fall in market value overlapped with these developments, the dot-com collapse and a leadership change; it should not be assigned to one cause alone.

The appellate process undid the breakup remedy and removed Jackson after his contacts with reporters became an issue. A new judge pushed toward settlement. The agreement proposed in 2001 and approved in 2002 left Microsoft intact, with restrictions on exclusionary OEM agreements, interoperability and documentation obligations, and independent technical oversight.

That outcome was not a declaration that every accusation had been false or that Microsoft had never violated antitrust law. The hosts’ provocative suggestion that Microsoft effectively won concerns the limited final remedy and the continued Windows and IE businesses. They distinguish that result from the immense disruption, reputational harm and subsequent litigation the company endured.

11

2000 onward · 01:25:47

Ballmer inherits the company in the middle of the storm

His first task was to keep people believing there was a company worth building.

Bill handed the CEO role to Steve on January 13, 2000, remaining chairman and chief software architect. He did not immediately disappear from product decisions. The transition happened before the final district-court breakup order and within a period of extraordinary uncertainty.

People interviewed for the episode repeatedly described Steve as the emotional anchor. The famous stage performances and declarations of love for Microsoft took place while people thought it might be split apart. The hosts find that context changes the meaning of behavior often remembered only as spectacle.

Steve also backed Brad Smith’s effort to settle disputes and rebuild relationships. Smith’s message to the board was that it was time to make peace. Moving forward required accepting a new legal and political reality rather than repeatedly litigating whether the old treatment had been fair.

David argues that the antitrust ordeal permanently damaged the consumer-technology company and Bill’s relationship with it. Ben agrees the distraction mattered but resists making it a complete explanation for everything that followed. They did not interview Bill for this episode, so judgments about his internal experience remain interpretation. Alongside the damage, Steve was beginning to build a much larger enterprise business.

12

Late 1990s–2000s · 01:36:10

The enterprise customer wanted a system

Winning the desktop created a route into the server room.

The old product categories were already visible in Bill’s 1996 description: Windows, Office and NT/back-office products supplied most revenue. What changed was Microsoft’s understanding of whom it could serve and how.

Enterprise computing was not simply many people each using a PC. IT departments needed databases, messaging, identity, access control and support. Microsoft could combine relatively inexpensive x86 hardware with server software rather than require the mainframe economics associated with older suppliers.

SQL Server strengthened the database offering. Exchange and Outlook linked email and calendars; Active Directory organized the people, devices and permissions other applications needed to reference. SharePoint, Dynamics and other products extended the system. The episode treats each as a substantial business, not a minor feature attached to a PC license.

The products worked better together, and each additional dependency made the relationship harder to replace. An individual component did not always need to be the best specialized tool if the customer valued an integrated whole from a familiar vendor. The hosts see this as business-model and product innovation, even though it looked less exciting than consumer hardware.

13

The enterprise-agreement model · 01:45:37

Turn the next software purchase into a continuing agreement

A broader bundle made adoption easier and the relationship stickier.

The enterprise agreement offered access to a broad collection of Microsoft software for recurring payments tied to the size of the deployment. The hosts initially say per employee, then qualify that the relevant measure was often devices; they note that in this era many employees used one device.

If a company used only part of the bundle, an additional need could be met with another already included Microsoft product. The extra adoption increased the customer’s dependence without a new standalone procurement. Microsoft amortized selling costs across many products in the same institutional relationship.

Three-year agreements fitted the rhythm of major Windows and Office releases. A customer could expect an upgrade opportunity within the term, while Microsoft gained a more predictable stream than occasional retail purchases. Expanding customers could also expand the relationship.

The low reproduction cost of software helped make bundling attractive. That was not the same as having no development, support or distribution costs. The hosts’ larger argument is that Microsoft had found a way to keep collecting from the value of an integrated system, rather than sell another box whenever someone decided to upgrade.

Explanatory interlude

Three parts of the enterprise relationship

Client applications

Windows and Office give employees a familiar working environment.

Institutional systems

SQL Server, Exchange and Active Directory support shared data, messaging, identity and control.

The agreement

A recurring bundle connects the products, spreads selling costs and encourages further adoption.

Qualitative explanation from the episode, not an architecture specification or universal contract. Device and employee measures are not treated as interchangeable.

14

The 2000s workplace · 01:48:59

The buyer and the person using the computer diverge

A restriction could be a feature for IT and a frustration for the employee.

An employee might want the newest application, easy installation or freedom to browse and run macros. An IT administrator might want to prevent exactly those actions, protecting data, compliance and a stable network. Microsoft increasingly optimized for the buyer responsible for the institution.

The distinction helps explain why a product could sell well while users disliked it. Big interface changes created retraining and sales objections. Backward compatibility protected workflows and customizations that the institution depended on, even when it made a cleaner redesign harder.

David’s banking experience illustrates the result: older Office and Windows versions remained in use on tightly controlled machines. That could be a rational choice for the company rather than simple technological backwardness.

The hosts do not claim users’ preferences ceased to matter. They argue that the economic relationship had shifted. This worked powerfully while IT could determine the environment, but later phones and personally chosen devices would force new expectations into workplaces. The same compatibility promise that built trust could become a constraint when Microsoft needed to reset.

15

By 2007 · 01:53:54

The quiet financial transformation

Most revenue now arrived through two exceptionally effective channels.

The episode cites analysts’ estimates that in 2007 forty percent of revenue came through multi-year enterprise agreements and another fifteen percent through single-year agreements. OEM Windows contributed about thirty percent. The remaining fifteen percent covered everything else.

That meant the majority came from the enterprise motion built under Ballmer, while much of the rest used the long-established PC-manufacturer channel. The familiar consumer retail box had become a relatively small part of the overall picture.

The hosts contrast their cited economics for boxed Windows with OEM distribution. Retail involved physical media, packaging, shipping and retail-channel costs. An OEM could receive the software and reproduce it across its own growing shipments. The precise channel comparison is the episode’s illustration of economics, not an independently reconstructed margin model. In their cited gross-margin comparison, they put boxed Windows at 29 percent and OEM distribution at 75 percent.

This is why the larger story cannot be inferred solely from the consumer products people remember. Microsoft was changing how software was purchased, deployed and paid for. The strength of those channels would sustain the business through releases that consumers and even many Microsoft employees found disappointing.

16

2001–2003 · 01:58:29

XP unifies the two Windows lineages

A difficult legal period still produced an important technical success.

For years Microsoft had maintained consumer Windows and the enterprise-oriented NT implementation. Developers could target shared interfaces, but the systems underneath differed. XP put the more robust NT foundation behind an approachable consumer experience.

That unification helped make XP an unusually durable release. Digital photography, music and home computing were becoming ordinary uses, while businesses could build around the same underlying architecture. Office released alongside it, reinforcing the familiar strategy of supplying useful applications immediately on the new platform.

The launch in New York a month after September 11 had a very different atmosphere from Windows 95. Bill remained the public figure onstage even though Steve was CEO, a reminder that the leadership transition did not cleanly divide business and product authority.

The market still had room to grow. The hosts cite PC ownership in only about sixty-two percent of American households in 2003. Windows remained a way to participate in that expansion, and XP demonstrated that the company could deliver a broadly successful consumer product during the same years its legal and enterprise stories were changing.

17

2001–early 2010s · 02:08:04

A consumer success with a contested opportunity cost

Gaming created capabilities whose value was not captured by one profit line.

DirectX had helped make Windows a more capable gaming platform before the Xbox arrived in November 2001. Moving into consoles expanded the company’s consumer presence beyond the PC, but it did not immediately create an enterprise-sized profit engine.

The hosts examine Entertainment and Devices reporting as an imperfect window into the economics. They cite substantial revenue with losses or relatively modest operating income, not a clean standalone lifetime Xbox profit calculation. The division contained more than one product, and the discussion does not establish a definitive audited break-even history.

Their disagreement concerns talent. Xbox attracted highly capable product designers and engineers. Ben wonders what those people might have contributed elsewhere when other consumer efforts were struggling. Money was comparatively plentiful; attention and skilled people were not.

David emphasizes Xbox Live’s experience running an always-on network service and its relevance to consumers who otherwise had little reason to care about Microsoft. They converge on those benefits, which later reappear in the Azure story. A business could have modest direct profits in this period while producing technical capabilities useful somewhere else.

18

2002–2006 · 02:15:12

The small follow-up becomes the enormous unfinished project

Security work, ambitious architecture and shifting scope consumed the schedule.

Microsoft initially imagined a relatively quick successor to XP and a more ambitious release afterward. The ski-resort code names captured that sequence: Whistler, Longhorn and Blackcomb. In practice the boundary between a modest update and a grand redesign kept dissolving.

XP’s security problems required far more work than a short service-pack effort. Service Pack 2 tied up significant talent, delaying what could be done next. This was a substantial improvement effort, not a guarantee that any release was immune from malware.

Longhorn accumulated major architectural ambitions. Avalon addressed graphics, Indigo web-services communication, and WinFS a more structured and searchable way to organize information. The hosts are particularly skeptical about whether application developers had actually asked for WinFS’s complex new foundation.

Different teams could work on their parts while the integrated product remained elusive. Dependencies, reviews and redesigns multiplied. The episode describes the original effort failing to come together and a later reset onto a more shippable foundation. It is a story of scope and integration failure, not evidence that every intermediate developer build in the project’s history literally could not compile.

19

2006–2009 · 02:23:56

The release arrived after its promise had consumed the company

The cost was years of engineering attention as much as the product’s reception.

Vista offered striking visual effects, but the hardware requirements clashed with what many OEMs wanted to sell. Security prompts irritated users. Enterprises hesitated to upgrade, and Microsoft extended opportunities for manufacturers to keep selling XP.

The hosts contrast the Windows organization with Office’s stronger shipping discipline, acknowledging Ben’s bias as a former Office employee. A culture that had once enabled technical breakthroughs could now justify too many delays in the name of difficult systems engineering.

Brian Valentine helped force the project toward release, then left for Amazon. Other strong engineers also departed. Ballmer’s reflection in the episode places Vista among the worst moments of his tenure because so much of the best talent had been taken off the field while other markets were changing.

Apple’s Mac-versus-PC advertising hurt beyond any immediate market-share shift. It expressed frustrations people recognized and strengthened expectations that technology should simply work. The hosts see a loss of consumer and developer excitement, while explicitly retaining exceptions: enterprise developers and established desktop applications still had reasons to target Windows. The web was attracting many of the new ideas.

20

Late 1990s–2000s · 02:38:39

Winning the browser had not revealed its business model

Search monetized the attention passing through the browser.

Microsoft had used Internet Explorer primarily to defend Windows. Google found that the same user attention could be monetized through search advertising. A browser or device owner could also earn by directing traffic to a search provider, even without operating the search engine itself.

The hosts explain the reinforcing scale: more queries supply more data, more advertisers improve auction liquidity, and greater revenue funds infrastructure and development. Being second could therefore be much less lucrative than simply taking a proportional slice of the leader’s business.

Microsoft’s search efforts sat within shifting MSN and Windows Live strategies. Meanwhile IE had stagnated and frustrated both users and web developers. The company did not treat search with the sustained priority the hosts think it required early enough.

An expensive aQuantive acquisition failed to solve the problem. The episode then turns to a much larger proposed transaction: Yahoo. The motivating question was no longer just how to distribute an application, but whether Microsoft could gain meaningful participation in a business model that monetized activity throughout the wider economy.

21

2008–2009 · 02:45:31

The forty-seven-billion-dollar deal that did not happen

The counterfactual looked attractive only if the assets and the integration worked.

Negotiations to buy Yahoo reached roughly forty-seven billion dollars. The hosts recount a meeting involving Yahoo’s founders and Ballmer at Boeing Field, while acknowledging conflicting versions of why the transaction ultimately failed. Concerns about the sellers’ commitment and integration belonged to the decision alongside the price.

Yahoo brought more than search share. Yahoo Japan and its Alibaba stake gave the hosts reasons to reconsider how expensive the operating search business really looked after accounting for other assets. But that is a counterfactual valuation exercise, not proof that Microsoft would have retained those holdings or built a Google-sized competitor.

Bing launched in 2009, and a commercial agreement put Microsoft’s search technology behind Yahoo. Yahoo initially retained most of the associated revenue. Microsoft gained query data, advertising-marketplace scale and experience operating a high-volume search service, rather than ownership of Yahoo’s direct user relationship.

Satya Nadella briefly led Bing. The hosts trace his earlier Microsoft work back through enterprise products and even the old Tiger project, rather than presenting him as someone who arrived only when cloud computing was already successful. The distributed-systems experience surrounding search would become one of several ingredients available for Azure.

22

2007–2008 · 02:56:52

An investment could also buy a commercial foothold

The strategic rights mattered more than hypothetical stock returns.

Microsoft also tried to participate in the emerging social market. The episode describes a complex proposed Facebook acquisition, reported at a much larger valuation than earlier offers, but the company did not sell.

Instead Microsoft invested two hundred forty million dollars for 1.6 percent in 2007, implying a fifteen-billion-dollar valuation. The investment came with rights to sell international banner advertising for a period. That supplied inventory and another way to build the advertiser side of Microsoft’s marketplace.

The hosts discuss possible investment returns but explicitly do not know when Microsoft sold the shares. The hypothetical appreciation should not be treated as a realized return. For a company already rich in cash, strategic access and operating capability mattered more than acting like a hedge fund.

Bill left full-time work in 2008 while remaining chairman. The hosts quote a contemporary dismissal of his relevance, then reject it as reductive. They use it to show the prevailing perception of Microsoft, not as their verdict on Bill. Beneath the criticism lay a real gap between the enterprise developer world and the web and consumer platforms attracting new energy.

23

Early 2000s–2009 · 03:04:21

The phone changed the workplace as well as the market

Windows Mobile had been designed for a different product and a different buyer.

Microsoft had pursued mobile and pen-based computing for years. Windows Mobile was an ingredient handset makers could adapt, often for business devices that fitted Exchange and the corporate network. The manufacturer, not Microsoft alone, determined much of the finished experience.

The iPhone reset expectations around a small, capable computer and a polished interface. Ballmer’s much-repeated price criticism also reflected his role selling against a competitor and a business model that initially looked expensive. The hosts qualify the familiar quotation without pretending Microsoft had understood the change correctly.

Personally chosen devices began entering workplaces. Employees expected software to work on what they wanted to carry, weakening IT’s ability to dictate every part of the environment. The enterprise model remained financially powerful, but it could no longer assume that user preferences stayed outside the network.

Windows Phone attempted a stronger Microsoft promise, with tighter hardware requirements and Metro’s distinctive interface, which had roots in Zune. Its problem was not only matching Apple’s product. Microsoft was still trying to charge manufacturers for an operating system just as another company discovered reasons to give one away.

24

2009 onward · 03:13:04

The competitor arrived from a different profit pool

Google could subsidize the software Microsoft wanted to sell.

Android gave handset makers an operating system without Microsoft’s license fee. Google wanted distribution for search and other services, with advertising supplying the economics. The hosts describe this as a competitor entering from the side with a different business model.

They distinguish the open-source system from the package of proprietary Google services, including the app store. The availability and terms of those pieces varied; the argument is not that every part of an Android phone was open source or unconditionally free.

A small fee can matter greatly in a low-margin device. Google could also support distribution and marketing because it expected to monetize the resulting users. Microsoft would have needed a major internal change to treat Windows Phone as an advertising-distribution vehicle rather than software to license.

The hosts see Verizon’s backing of Droid as a moment the competitive field narrowed. They also carry the logic into other software Google could subsidize, while preserving a limit: Microsoft’s enterprise lock-in remained formidable. Explaining the mobile failure does not require claiming all Microsoft products would lose to free alternatives.

25

After Vista · 03:22:02

Windows 7 repairs the ordinary product

Reliability and an organization able to ship became the achievement.

Steven Sinofsky moved from Office into Windows after Vista. The hosts use his car analogy to explain the job: Windows did not need another exotic engineering vehicle. It needed a dependable mainstream product that people and institutions could use without surprises.

Windows 7 restored that feeling. Ben and David struggle to name a spectacular feature, which is part of the point. It was fast, familiar and compatible enough that the computer did what users expected.

The organization was also a product of the effort. A slimmer structure, clearer responsibilities and stronger shipping discipline mattered as much as the visible interface. The hosts admire the ability to coordinate a large release rather than treating all Microsoft software of this era as uniformly bad.

That recovery made it possible to attempt something more ambitious next. Tablet computing was becoming plausible, and the iPad’s arrival seemed to validate a direction Microsoft had explored for years. It also threatened the PC position just as the iPhone had overturned the assumptions behind the old phone business.

26

2010–2012 · 03:24:57

The tablet future is imposed on the existing desktop

A new interface and a new developer platform became one risky release.

Windows 8 pursued touch devices and an application environment meant to attract developers back from the web. HTML5 technologies and ARM-based tablets belonged to that plan. Microsoft also entered hardware through Surface, reflecting the realization that mobile profits might sit with integrated devices or advertising rather than an independent software license.

The hosts describe an early vision in which touch tablets and conventional desktops could receive different experiences. The eventual requirement that Windows remain one platform put the new full-screen Start experience and the familiar desktop into the same product.

Users had to move between different metaphors. OEMs had not all prepared to sell the touch-first devices that the software emphasized. Surface RT and the ARM effort also exposed how hard it was to turn a scaled-down PC into the tablet people wanted; this does not mean every Windows 8 PC used ARM.

The app ecosystem did not generate the hoped-for enthusiasm. The hosts allow that the vision might look different with later hardware, while emphasizing that it failed in its own market and time. The company had again anticipated a broad direction without finding the right implementation, timing and relationship to its existing users.

27

2004–2006 · 03:35:28

The chief software architect is asked for a new future

Ray Ozzie’s services vision began before the apparent turnaround.

As Bill prepared to leave full-time work, Microsoft needed technical leadership alongside Ballmer. Ray Ozzie, known for Lotus Notes and later Groove, had longstanding relationships with people inside the company. Microsoft acquired Groove and brought him into the role, with Craig Mundie taking another part of the broader succession.

Ozzie’s 2005 Internet Services Disruption memo described improving networks and the appeal of services that simply worked. Businesses might rent capacity or buy hosted applications instead of purchasing and operating every server themselves.

The obstacle was inside Microsoft’s success. Server and Tools sold software for customers’ infrastructure, working through hardware makers, consultants and implementation partners. A service hosted by Microsoft could bypass parts of that chain and generate usage revenue instead of Windows-license metrics.

The new effort therefore began outside the normal business-unit structure as Red Dog. That was not evidence that the enterprise engineers lacked capability. It was a recognition that a business rewarded for selling on-premises software had incentives to resist a model that could replace it. Ballmer supplied the organizational protection while Ozzie shaped the technical direction.

28

2006 onward · 03:41:56

The skills were already scattered across the company

The incubation combined enterprise engineering with live-service experience.

Ozzie recruited Dave Cutler and Amitabh Srivastava, with experience in core systems and enterprise software. The hosts credit Cutler’s hypervisor work: the software that virtualizes hardware so multiple workloads can share the underlying machines.

Other seemingly separate businesses had supplied related skills. Bing taught distributed systems and low-latency service operation. Xbox Live supplied an always-on consumer network. Hotmail and MSN had operated at large scale, while SharePoint, Exchange and Windows Server brought enterprise application and infrastructure knowledge.

The hosts see this as a reason Microsoft could attempt the transition with unusual credibility. It had technical ingredients and relationships with the institutions that would migrate. That does not mean each earlier investment was justified solely because Azure later succeeded; they have already debated the opportunity cost of talent.

Capital expenditure was still substantial. Ballmer committed resources to data centers and capacity rather than only shipping another copy of software. The economics had real operating costs, including energy and equipment. Moving into cloud meant accepting a model that could initially look less attractive than Microsoft’s existing high-margin licensing business.

29

2010 onward · 03:44:14

Windows Azure was not yet the entire cloud vision

The hosts disagree about when the commitment became a bet on a different company.

Early Windows Azure emphasized platform as a service. It fitted Microsoft’s own technology more closely than a fully open infrastructure service on which customers could run whatever they chose. Microsoft had not yet broadly embraced the open-source world it had long viewed as a threat.

That qualification matters to the hosts’ disagreement. David sees the resource commitment and willingness to move toward a different model as a major company bet. Ben argues that spending cash was less scarce than assigning Ozzie and Cutler, and that the initial product did not yet overturn the Windows-centered approach. They agree the eventual transition was profound.

As Ozzie left in 2010, Azure moved into Server and Tools. Ballmer publicly declared the company all in on cloud, sending a message internally as much as to customers. He replaced the division’s leadership despite its success in the old model and selected Satya Nadella from Bing to lead the transformation.

Nadella’s execution deserves substantial credit in their account. The chronology also matters: development and sponsorship had been underway under Ozzie and Ballmer for years. The mature infrastructure and open-source offering arrived through an evolution, not a single launch in 2014 by a new CEO.

The hosts’ cloud assessment · 03:48:16

The old enterprise business becomes part of the cloud advantage

The financial segment was broader than Azure alone.

The hosts cite eighty-eight billion dollars of Intelligent Cloud revenue in fiscal 2023. They immediately qualify it: the segment includes large businesses such as SQL Server and Windows Server. That figure is not standalone Azure revenue.

The reporting helped the company describe its cloud scale, but the old products were also strategically useful. An enterprise could move gradually, combining its existing Microsoft environment with hosted services. Microsoft’s promise of a hybrid path drew on relationships and knowledge that a new supplier had to earn.

The opportunity reached beyond software firms. More consumer services and ordinary businesses depended on rented computing infrastructure. The hosts compare that broad expansion with the PC growth wave, while acknowledging Microsoft’s cloud share was much smaller than its old operating-system share.

Their enthusiasm about a decades-long opportunity and AI is a view from the 2024 recording, not an updated market forecast. What makes it consequential to this history is that a company perceived as missing the exciting consumer era had already developed the foundation for participation in the next computing wave.

31

2012–2014 · 03:55:57

The shelf party and the later qualification

Ben’s personal experience captures both the cost and the logic of protecting an incumbent.

Ben worked on Office for iPad, a product users wanted in a multi-device world. His team prepared to ship, then was told to hold it while Microsoft promoted Surface and Windows 8 as the place to get real Office on a tablet. The planned ship celebration became a shelf party.

At the time he saw the decision as an example of a company trapped by its Windows strategy. In the episode he still believes Microsoft stayed Windows-centered too long, but he also reconsiders the specific choice.

If the iPad might replace the PC, management could see significant downside in helping it immediately and little incremental near-term revenue from doing so. The hosts leave open whether delaying Office for a year actually cost Microsoft money. That is not resolved simply by how frustrating the experience felt to its developers.

The product shipped soon after Nadella became CEO. It supplied a tangible example of a new message: serve people across devices, with cloud and mobile at the center. The contrast matters, but the hosts retain the less comfortable conclusion that protecting an incumbent can be rational even when it looks stubborn from inside one team.

32

2011–2013 · 04:00:09

Nokia ends the old leadership era with an unresolved negotiation

A strategic dilemma, internal opposition and an uncertain decision process.

Nokia adopted Windows Phone as its main smartphone platform in 2011. Its existing system was losing momentum, and the company had not chosen Android. Former Microsoft executive Stephen Elop was leading it, giving the partnership deep personal connections as well as product logic.

By 2013 Nokia was considering Android unless something changed. Buying its mobile business could preserve Microsoft’s phone route. Ballmer framed the cash commitment as comparatively small beside Microsoft’s resources, while the hosts distinguish a financial loss from the more consequential loss of focus and talent.

Many senior leaders opposed the acquisition, including Nadella. The sequence is clear in the episode: Ballmer’s retirement plan was announced on August 23, 2013, and Microsoft agreed to buy Nokia’s mobile unit about ten days later for roughly seven billion dollars. The hosts heard different accounts of how the board and executive decisions connected and do not claim to know exactly who made the decisive bargain.

The uncertainty should not be converted into a confident story that one person bought the business as the price of another person leaving. The commercial failure belongs in the record, alongside the company’s ability to make a costly bet without threatening its financial survival.

33

February 2014 · 04:06:24

A new leader inherits more than a broken company

The cultural reset and the valuable underlying businesses were both real.

Satya Nadella became CEO on February 4, 2014. Ballmer stepped down, Bill left the chairmanship and John Thompson became chairman. The original leadership’s long shadow could recede enough for the company to frame its future differently.

The hosts see a genuine need for a reset after old battles, internal politics and product disappointments. They also insist that the new leader inherited major strengths: durable enterprise relationships, substantial operating profit and a cloud business already being built.

The record does not support replacing one simplistic narrative with its opposite. Ballmer’s era had real strategic and execution failures; Nadella’s execution and cultural changes mattered. It is equally wrong to imagine that Azure and enterprise success appeared only after 2014.

The later stock and market-value comparisons in the recording illustrate how much perceptions changed, not a claim that all the future value was already certain. This companion keeps the main chronology at the leadership handoff and treats those comparisons as the hosts’ retrospective context.

34

The hosts’ assessment of 2000–2014 · 04:09:28

The operating company grew while the stock went sideways

Starting valuation and business performance are different measurements.

Across Ballmer’s tenure, the hosts cite revenue rising from twenty-three billion to eighty-four billion dollars and operating income from twelve billion to thirty billion. Those are their chosen endpoints; operating income is not net profit, and their conversational shorthand about tripling should not replace the actual figures.

The stock had begun near a technology-bubble valuation. They cite a price-to-earnings multiple falling from roughly seventy-five times to fourteen times by the end of the period, helping offset the effect of operating growth on market value. A flat stock did not mean the enterprise itself had failed to expand.

They debate why investors continued to discount it. Consumer and developer relevance was legitimately in doubt, product strategy looked incoherent, and Microsoft did not explain the durability of its core businesses or the emerging cloud opportunity well. Their storytelling argument is a causal interpretation, not proof that communication alone determines a share price.

They also compare Microsoft with the large technology incumbents of its starting era, rather than only with the extraordinary new businesses Google and Facebook created. Preserving relevance, talent and growing profits was itself an achievement. Users’ dissatisfaction with many consumer products can remain true at the same time.

35

The hosts’ competitive framework · 04:19:11

The powers change with the customer

Scale and switching costs remain strong while other advantages weaken.

The hosts do not repeat Volume I’s broad case for all seven powers. Counter-positioning is mostly unavailable to the established company, though they see a narrower argument in Azure’s hybrid relationship with enterprises. In mobile, Microsoft was the company being counter-positioned by another business model.

Scale economies remain formidable: development can be spread over enormous numbers of customers, with the enterprise bundle making even small improvements valuable. Switching costs are particularly strong around the agreement and the integrated system. A competing point product must overcome both its own price and the customer’s existing commitment.

They think process power deteriorated in the Windows failures. Consumer branding weakened, while enterprise trust strengthened. Those are different audiences, not one universal judgment about the Microsoft name.

Network economies are less convincing in their analysis of this era. Interoperability and more portable file formats weakened the old operating-system-specific effects, and one enterprise’s purchase did not obviously make another’s more valuable. They find little meaningful cornered resource. The result resembles an enterprise incumbent built around scale and switching costs, rather than the earlier platform with advantages reinforcing it from nearly every direction.

36

The hosts’ cultural argument · 04:24:40

A stagnant stock can change what winning means internally

They look for incentives underneath the description of a bad culture.

The people interviewed described an environment where the stock seemed likely to stay flat. Employees could no longer assume that making the company broadly more successful would make their own equity dramatically more valuable. Competition for promotion and internal standing became more salient.

Stack ranking intensified that dynamic by forcing comparisons within groups. The hosts connect it to the familiar cartoon of divisions pointing guns at one another. Their explanation is not that one performance-management rule caused every problem, nor that the operating business had literally stopped growing.

They contrast fighting users’ preferences with finding a business that can serve them. Open source, web applications and personally chosen devices would not disappear because Microsoft preferred the old arrangement. The cloud could let IT and Microsoft support those choices while still selling something useful.

That is their return to embrace and extend: recognize what people are already doing, then find a sustainable way to improve it and earn money. The difficult part remains the economics. Giving users value for less than it costs is not by itself a durable business; the company must discover where payment and value creation can fit together.

37

The hosts’ execution analysis · 04:28:52

Correct direction, wrong implementation and moment

Seeing a future early did not mean knowing how it should arrive.

Microsoft anticipated touch computing, mobile work and interactive television long before their successful mass-market forms. The hosts argue that it often selected the wrong technical standard, entered at the wrong moment or carried the PC model into a setting that required another design.

A tablet could be a larger phone rather than a smaller PC. Touch could depend on a different interface technology. Interactive television eventually resembled services delivered through the internet, rather than a managed overlay on the cable system. These examples distinguish the broad prediction from the actual product and distribution model.

The earlier practice of bracketing possibilities had sometimes worked: develop approaches above and below current technical capabilities, then adjust as the market clarified. The hosts are less sure why that flexibility broke down. Some options collapsed into a single bet or could no longer flourish inside the organization.

They also reflect on Bill and Steve as complementary leaders. Bill combined technical judgment with a willingness to change course; Steve could align a huge organization and its partners behind a decision. Together those skills could exceed either person’s contribution alone. Separated or out of balance, theoretical ambition or rigid execution could become liabilities.

38

The final takeaway · 04:33:52

The missed waves were real; the missing story was larger

The episode ends with both a narrative critique and a disruption warning.

Microsoft’s partner network was both an advantage and a constraint. Hardware makers, software vendors, resellers and customers depended on continuity. Resetting a platform meant disrupting people outside the company as well as changing its own code. The hosts see that as a more precise explanation than simply saying that losing one generation makes a company ready to win the next.

David’s final realization is that Microsoft failed to tell a coherent account of what it was building. Other companies’ unsuccessful bets could fit a celebrated narrative of experimentation, while Microsoft’s became evidence that nothing worked. He does not deny the failures; he argues that the durable businesses and new cloud foundation disappeared behind them.

Ben’s original closing thought remains more skeptical about technology leadership itself. Even a dominant company can lose the next wave, and he thinks mobile might have been missed without the antitrust ordeal because Android’s economics attacked Microsoft from outside its familiar model. A strong old business does not guarantee relevance in the new one.

They finish by giving the era and Ballmer more credit for leaving Microsoft positioned for cloud while growing revenue and operating income. That is the episode’s qualified reassessment, not an acquittal of every product choice. Personal recommendations begin at 04:40:37, after this final exchange.
