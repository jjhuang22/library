Bitcoin

A paper became a network. The network became a monetary argument.

Based on Acquired · Bitcoin, with Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal
Released January 19, 2021 · Created Unpublished
Original episode: 3 hours 15 minutes · About 24 minutes to read

The story and analysis as told in the January 2021 recording, from the payment problem to the final debate over digital gold. Prices, estimates, legal circumstances, and predictions belong to that moment.

Chapter 01 · Before 2008

The number you had to give away

An ordinary tax payment opens a much older problem.

On January 11, 2021, David Rosenthal paid his taxes. The unsettling part came afterward: he had typed his bank routing and account numbers into government websites, and the money had been pulled from his account. He wanted that payment to happen. But he also gave those same numbers to vendors and business partners. Why did receiving money require sharing information that could also help somebody take it? The hosts dramatize the risk to expose the dependence on intermediaries; they are not claiming that every account number alone permits unrestricted withdrawals.

Ben Gilbert joined the thought experiment, then defended the system they were interrogating. Banks and card networks monitor fraud, reverse charges, and keep an economy operating. That continuity matters. Their infrastructure had grown through centuries of useful adaptations: banknotes standing in for metal, checks presented for verification, clearinghouses reconciling accounts, and computers processing payment batches. Credit cards made a purchase feel immediate by extending credit before the underlying money moved. The apparent speed concealed another layer of institutions and obligations.

The internet increased both the number of places people paid and the opportunities for information to escape. Stripe could keep a merchant from storing a customer's card number; Square and Venmo could make familiar rails easier to use. These were valuable improvements, but they still rested on the earlier system. PayPal had come closer to a vision of native internet money, yet its centralized operation had to investigate fraud and mediate disputes. It digitized the intermediary along with the payment.

Other experiments—DigiCash, e-gold, Bit gold, and Tencent's QQ coins—showed that digital value was hardly an unexplored idea. Public and private keys already offered an important component: people could publish an address while keeping the authority to act with a separate secret. What remained was the problem software normally solved in exactly the opposite direction. A digital file could be copied almost without cost. Money had to resist being spent twice.

The hosts begin here because their eventual subject was often explained backward. By January 2021, Bitcoin was widely discussed as an inflation hedge or a spectacular investment. Its original proposal addressed a more concrete frustration: how could people transact over the internet without requiring a trusted institution to stand in the middle of every payment?

Chapter 02 · 2008–2009

A paper, then a working system

A pseudonymous author combines existing ideas into a new arrangement.

On August 18, 2008, bitcoin.org was registered under the name Satoshi Nakamoto. Lehman Brothers failed the next month. On October 31, an account using that pseudonym sent a paper to a cryptography mailing list: Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System. The chronology placed it in a financial crisis, but Ben and David emphasize what its introduction actually argued. Commerce depended on trusted financial institutions; mediation and reversibility imposed costs; sellers had to gather information about buyers because they could not fully trust a payment.

The proposal was strikingly short—nine pages including references—and it did more than announce an alternative monetary philosophy. Satoshi would also write and release a working implementation. Earlier inventions supplied essential pieces. Public-key cryptography separated an address people could know from a secret they must not possess. Hashcash had used computational work as a way to make sending spam costly. Satoshi combined those ingredients with a distributed transaction history and incentives for the people maintaining it.

A signature could establish that a payment was authorized, but authorization alone did not resolve two attempts to spend the same money. The network needed a shared history. Instead of one central organization keeping that ledger, participants could retain copies, check transactions, and propagate the history they accepted. Blocks linked the records together; proof of work made producing a competing history costly. In the hosts' explanation, making work difficult to perform and easy to check was the essential asymmetry.

Ben detoured into the history of one-way functions, using multiplication and difficult factorization as an intuition, then described the independent development of public-key ideas in Britain and in the work publicly associated with RSA. These were explanatory analogies, not a claim that Bitcoin mining simply factors primes. He also acknowledged the underlying uncertainty: their confidence in difficult mathematical problems was not a universal proof that no efficient reversal could ever be found. A profound cryptographic break would threaten much more than Bitcoin.

There was another practical question: why would strangers pay for the machines and electricity? The protocol rewarded successful mining with newly created bitcoin. The initial block reward was 50 coins; by the January 2021 recording, it was 6.25. The declining issuance schedule, described by the hosts as halving roughly every four years, pointed toward a supply limit just below 21 million. Compensation for maintaining the system and the currency people might want to hold were part of the same design.

In January 2009, Satoshi booted up the software, mined the Genesis block, and began recruiting an open-source community. Cryptography researcher Hal Finney received the first transaction. At first the community was small enough for a researcher to run the experiment on ordinary equipment. The hosts' later argument about an entrenched network starts with how fragile that beginning was: an elegant paper did not automatically create a useful monetary system. People still had to join, work on it, and find something to do with it.

Authority, work, and a shared history

1 · AuthorizeA private key signs a proposed transfer. Others can check the signature without knowing that secret.
2 · Propose a blockMiners compete through computational work; the reward gives them a reason to participate.
3 · Check and shareParticipants check records and propagate an accepted ledger rather than ask one institution to keep it.

Conceptual simplification of the episode's explanation. Checking a transaction and mining a block are different tasks. This is not an exact protocol specification or a security guarantee; the hosts also discuss recent-block risks and unknown cryptographic breakthroughs.

Chapter 03 · 2009–2011

Two pizzas and an absent founder

The first uses were small, awkward, and necessary.

For much of its first year, Bitcoin circulated among people working on the project. On May 22, 2010, Florida programmer Laszlo Hanyecz offered 10,000 bitcoin to anyone who would get him pizza. Someone in England accepted, ordered two Papa John's pizzas with a credit card, and had them delivered to Florida. Hanyecz sent the bitcoin in return. It was an improbable bridge between the experimental ledger and an ordinary dinner: the restaurant itself still received a conventional card payment.

Ben found the exchange recognizably programmery. A participant could regard the coins as work he would otherwise have to do on a computer, while another participant wanted to see whether they could buy anything outside the experiment. Its importance was not that the initial price discovered a permanent value. The early market could barely be called a market at all. The hosts also recall a forum user's unsuccessful attempt to auction 10,000 coins for $50. A posted offer was no assurance of a buyer.

Satoshi eventually stopped contributing, handed control of the open-source repositories to other developers, and said they had moved on to other projects. No verified personal identity accompanied the departure. Ben marveled at the absence of familiar mistakes: no obvious personal email, phone number, or other breadcrumb that settled who was behind the name. A person, several people, or another identity altogether remained possible. The paper's use of 'we' did not resolve it.

The founder also left a much more consequential unknown: a large early holding. The hosts give differing estimates across the conversation, ranging from hundreds of thousands to roughly 1.5 million coins. Those are estimates discussed in the recording, not an established count. The larger point was concentration. Early participants could mine substantial amounts when little computing power was committed, and some large balances scarcely moved afterward.

By early 2011, the hosts place an indicative exchange rate around 30 cents; by the end of the year, they cite $5.27. But appreciation alone did not explain how a software experiment would cross into repeated use. A new service offered people a reason to acquire coins, transact with strangers, and keep returning. It would also make many potential users want nothing to do with Bitcoin.

Chapter 04 · 2011–2014

The first market, and its cost

Silk Road brings activity and a lasting association with crime.

In February 2011, a figure calling himself Dread Pirate Roberts launched Silk Road on Tor. The service borrowed the familiar structure of an online marketplace and applied it to illegal goods, especially drugs. Buyers paid in bitcoin, sellers mailed orders, and the platform brought distant parties together. The operator's stated ambition was ideological: give people an alternative to authorities he viewed as tyrannical. An initial seller-account fee later gave way, in the hosts' account, to a more familiar transaction-based marketplace model.

For Bitcoin, this was the first compelling repeated use case the hosts identify. People who did not want an ordinary financial institution attached to a purchase had a particular reason to use the new system. David cites more than 1.2 million transactions, nearly 150,000 buyers, and almost 4,000 sellers over the marketplace's roughly two and a half years. Ben sees the activity as helping Bitcoin get beyond its fragile beginning. That is their account of network development; it is not an endorsement of what was being bought and sold.

The government began investigating early. In October 2013, agents arrested Ross Ulbricht in San Francisco's Glen Park Library. David describes the operation: two agents staged a distracting argument while another seized Ulbricht's open computer before he could lock it. The detail mattered because control of the machine could expose the secrets controlling the money. Behind the pseudonym was a man who had grown up in Austin and been an Eagle Scout, an incongruity the hosts found hard to reconcile with the enterprise he built.

The discussion carefully separates Ulbricht's convictions from allegations that he had sought to arrange killings. David says he was convicted on seven charges involving matters such as narcotics conspiracy, money laundering, and computer hacking; he was not convicted of the murder-for-hire allegations, and the hosts say no killing occurred. As of their January 2021 conversation, he was serving life imprisonment without parole. That is the recording's historical vantage point.

The seizure made the US government a holder of bitcoin. The hosts describe about 144,000 coins seized and a subsequent auction of roughly 30,000, which venture capitalist Tim Draper bought for $17 million. A further episode in November 2020 exposed another 70,000 coins associated with Silk Road. According to David's account, an unidentified hacker had stolen them from the marketplace before its collapse; authorities later found the person, and the coins moved into federal custody. A ledger visible to everyone could reveal a transfer even while the circumstances behind it remained unknown.

Ben used this to distinguish two risks easily confused in a headline. Stealing someone's private key lets an attacker authorize a transfer. Losing the key can leave its owner unable to authorize anything at all. Neither is the same as rewriting the protocol's history. Yet he resisted treating poor usability as irrelevant to Bitcoin: if a complicated airplane is hard to fly, the consequences for the pilot are still real. Without a central operator, there is no ordinary password-reset desk. The hosts cite an estimate that roughly a fifth of the supply might be lost, but inactivity by itself cannot establish why an owner is not transacting.

Chapter 05 · 2010–2014

The exchange with a borrowed name

An easier way into Bitcoin creates another place to put trust.

While Silk Road grew, buyers needed an easier way to exchange dollars for bitcoin than asking around a research mailing list. One programmer had a spare domain. In 2006, Jed McCaleb had built Magic: The Gathering Online Exchange, hoping to create a specialized trading venue for cards. It did not take off. In July 2010, he repurposed mtgox.com for a Bitcoin exchange. The name that sounded vaguely like a mountain or a vault had come from a card game.

McCaleb recognized that operating the exchange would demand more time than he could give it. In March 2011, he sold it to Mark Karpeles, a French programmer living in Japan. McCaleb would later be associated with Ripple and Stellar; the hosts emphasize that he was no longer running Mt. Gox during its eventual collapse. The buyer inherited a service whose potential was enormous and whose responsibilities had become much closer to those of a financial institution than a casual website.

Warning signs arrived quickly. A security breach in June 2011 was followed in October by 2,500 coins sent to wrong addresses. Nevertheless, Mt. Gox became the dominant route in and out of Bitcoin, handling about 70% of exchange activity in the hosts' account. Ben remembers visiting the site and deciding he did not trust it enough to buy. For people outside the earliest community, the combination of Silk Road and a dubious exchange made the entire system look suspect.

In April 2013, volume overwhelmed Mt. Gox, it suspended trading, and Bitcoin's price fell sharply. Lawsuits followed. Dollar withdrawals stopped in June; in February 2014, withdrawals stopped altogether and the exchange filed for bankruptcy. The hosts describe approximately 750,000 customer coins and another 100,000 belonging to the exchange as lost in the collapse. Those are the loss figures and characterization given in this recording, not a later recovery accounting.

The paradox ran through both disasters. Bitcoin promised to reduce dependence on a trusted payment intermediary, but people still needed websites to buy it, somewhere to hold keys, and institutions they could understand. If an exchange controlled the relevant secrets, a customer could not necessarily recover money merely because the underlying ledger continued operating. The next phase would be built by people willing to treat those interfaces as serious businesses.

Chapter 06 · 2012–2016

Making custody respectable

Coinbase and Gemini pursue different customers with a common compromise.

In June 2012, former Airbnb engineer Brian Armstrong and former Goldman Sachs trader Fred Ehrsam founded Coinbase. Their ambition was broader than another trading screen. They wanted an exchange that people could trust, that worked with regulators, and that helped customers operate within the financial system rather than evade it. Coinbase went through Y Combinator that summer, raised from Initialized and angels, then from Union Square Ventures and Andreessen Horowitz. The money helped build infrastructure to make an intimidating product usable.

Its cloud wallet supplied the decisive compromise. A customer did not need to keep a private key on a hard drive, back it up without exposing it, or organize physical safeguards. Coinbase could retain custody while the customer logged in with a username, password, and familiar additional authentication. Ben understood why that made decentralization purists uncomfortable. David compared it to holding shares through a brokerage rather than keeping stock certificates. The convenience was purchased by trusting an institution again.

The hosts' own behavior illustrates its distribution advantage: both identify Coinbase as the place they first transacted. A technically capable person might manage keys directly; a large holder might arrange elaborate offline protection. Neither approach was a natural starting point for every ordinary user. The bearer-like nature of the asset also made visibly carrying valuable keys on a laptop an uncomfortable proposition. Accessibility was about operational habits as much as understanding the white paper.

Another route into legitimacy came from Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss. The hosts revisit their Facebook dispute because the settlement shaped what followed. After legal fees, the twins chose Facebook shares rather than cash; the hosts put the resulting stake's value around $300 million by the 2012 public offering. After competing as Olympic rowers, they heard about Bitcoin on a trip to Spain. What caught their attention was money with network effects.

They began buying in 2012 and accumulated more than 100,000 coins for under $10 million, according to David's account. They then funded Gemini with institutions in mind. Regulatory approval and a New York license to serve as a custodian mattered to asset managers who could not simply behave like an enthusiast with a laptop. David connects Gemini's infrastructure to the launch of Bitcoin futures in 2017. The point is the bridge into established markets, not that every institution would use the same exchange.

The price path remained anything but smooth. David cites roughly $13 at the start of 2013 and $770 at its end, followed by a fall toward $300 as Mt. Gox failed. By the end of 2016, Bitcoin was near $1,000. Infrastructure had kept developing beneath the volatility. The hosts compare its return pattern to venture investing: the earliest money could earn astonishing multiples, while a larger amount could only be deployed after the market had become bigger and less obscure. A currency and a company were different objects, Ben cautioned, even when the arithmetic looked familiar.

Who keeps the secret?

Hold your own keys

You retain the authority to transfer. Protecting, backing up, and recovering access become your responsibility.

Use a cloud wallet

A provider holds the keys and offers a familiar login. Ease of access depends on trusting that custodian.

Conceptual comparison from Ben and David's Coinbase discussion. Neither path removes the risks described in the episode.

Chapter 07 · 2017–2019

What the bubble left behind

Mania finances activity that survives its own collapse.

By 2017, the appearance of easy money attracted projects far beyond Bitcoin. Ethereum had launched in 2015, and new cryptocurrency ventures could sell tokens through initial coin offerings. In May 2017, the Brave browser project raised $35 million in 30 seconds. To founders, token sales appeared to offer financing without conventional equity dilution; to enthusiasts, they promised a way for users to share in the value of a growing network. The hosts acknowledge the theoretical alignment while describing how far the practice ran ahead of meaningful products.

Celebrity promotions and dubious ventures proliferated. People who had made money holding bitcoin could pour gains into new offerings. Bitcoin's own dollar price accelerated through the year, reaching nearly $20,000 on December 18. Ben remembers cashing out and believing the peak would never be surpassed. David had taken some money off the table earlier but left a remaining holding as an option on what might happen. They were not narrating a course they had both confidently foreseen.

By the beginning of 2019, Bitcoin was trading just over $3,700—about 81% below that December peak. Regulators pursued fraudulent offerings, speculation receded, and venture investors returned to more conventional ways of backing companies. Yet the bust did not remove everything built during the boom. Coinbase, Gemini, and other firms continued developing access and custody. Square added crypto capabilities to Cash App in 2018; Robinhood also introduced trading and expanded availability.

By summer 2019, the hosts place Bitcoin around $13,000 again. Ben later draws on an argument by Matt Huang that they discuss in the episode: bubbles could function as a go-to-market mechanism. Attention and rising prices attracted people and capital; the subsequent collapse hurt late speculators, but some infrastructure, expertise, and legitimate participants stayed. It was a way of describing the history they had just told, not a promise that every bubble would end with a higher floor.

The distinction between a growing network and any one use of that network would recur in their analysis. A system could begin with activities an investor disliked and later support others. Ben would push back on how easily this could become advice: telling a founder to do random things until users arrived was hardly a reliable plan. The history was stranger and less replicable than a simple growth formula.

Chapter 08 · 2020–January 2021

A different kind of buyer

Institutional demand arrives in a market with limited willing sellers.

The March 2020 panic initially contradicted a popular Bitcoin story. As markets sold off, bitcoin fell too, dropping below $4,000 on March 13 in the hosts' account. Ben remembers watching it move with the S&P 500 and questioning the claim that it was an uncorrelated refuge. David interprets the episode as a liquidity run: people holding several kinds of assets needed cash to meet other obligations. A long-run monetary thesis did not eliminate a short-run need to sell.

What followed changed the appeal of that thesis. Governments and central banks responded to the pandemic with extraordinary stimulus and low interest rates. The hosts saw investors being pushed away from cash and bonds in search of returns, while a fixed issuance schedule became easier to explain. Their discussion of money creation and dilution is an interpretation of the January 2021 environment; Ben explicitly says they are not equipped to settle the macroeconomics. It should not be mistaken for a mechanical rule that a given increase in money supply creates an equal immediate increase in prices.

The infrastructure built over previous years now let much larger pools of capital act. Paul Tudor Jones disclosed in May 2020 that roughly 1%–2% of his fund's assets were in Bitcoin, representing hundreds of millions of dollars. The hosts describe MicroStrategy's $250 million allocation and its treatment of Bitcoin as a treasury reserve asset. Square committed about $50 million, approximately 1% of its cash and cash equivalents. Guggenheim sought permission for one fund to gain up to $500 million of exposure. An authorization to invest was not itself the same as a completed purchase.

The significance was the size and motivation of these buyers. Only a portion of the total supply was actively offered for sale. Some balances were held for the long term, some were presumed inaccessible, and some belonged to early participants. The hosts estimate a relatively small trading float rather than treating all possible coins as available at once. New demand in blocks of hundreds of millions of dollars could therefore meet very few willing sellers. David's price explanation is the intersection of those forces, not a new production cost per coin.

Bitcoin passed its 2017 high in November, ended 2020 around $29,000, and briefly reached about $42,000 in early January before falling again. At recording, the hosts were discussing a price around $34,000–$35,000 and an aggregate value near $650 billion. David wondered whether deliberate institutional allocations might make this episode more stable than earlier retail manias. Both immediately left room for another collapse; Ben joked that the price could be $15,000 by release. Institutional ownership was a hypothesis about incentives, not protection against volatility.

The scale comparisons became a bridge into analysis. Bitcoin was far smaller than the US money supply or all global currency, and smaller than gold. But bank assets, company market capitalization, a currency's money supply, and gold's market value are different measures. The hosts used them to explore the possible scale of a monetary asset. None supplied a cash-flow valuation, and none told them which eventual role Bitcoin would earn.

Chapter 09 · The hosts' analysis · January 2021

A network facing an older network

Seven Powers works only if the actor and the comparison stay clear.

Network economies were the obvious power in Ben and David's account. A competing developer could copy software, just as someone could build another social website, but could not instantly copy the community, participation, infrastructure, and economic commitment around it. They also argued that miners with holdings had an incentive to preserve trust rather than damage the system supporting those holdings. Their technical language sometimes became absolute; the useful claim is the incentive and accumulated participation they identify, not a guarantee that attacks or cryptographic breakthroughs are impossible. Ben specifically cautions that recent blocks can be more vulnerable than older history. They speculate about quantum computing while acknowledging how little they know about it; the idea that incentives or software changes could address such a breakthrough remains speculation.

Ben identifies counter-positioning against government-issued money: a central monetary authority cannot adopt decentralization wholesale without giving up the discretion and power its system is built to exercise. David initially extends the argument to the old banking technology. Ben disagrees. Banks could adopt better authentication if sufficiently motivated or required; the coordination might be hard, but it need not destroy their business. David accepts the distinction between difficult modernization and a change that conflicts with the incumbent's fundamental interest.

Scarcity generated another qualification. Ben calls Bitcoin a cornered resource because its issuance is limited. David asks whose power they are discussing. A holder can benefit from a scarce asset; that is not automatically a distinct source of power for the protocol as an organization. They also distinguish industrial-scale mining from the whole system. Mining rewards and competition drive operators toward specialized equipment, low costs, and suitable data-center locations. Scale advantages there belong to mining businesses rather than necessarily to Bitcoin itself.

They find relatively little switching-cost power: exchanging currencies involves transaction costs, but not the deep organizational entrenchment of enterprise software. They identify no process power and little independent branding power. Ben would prefer Bitcoin because of its network, rather than its name. These exclusions matter. The framework does not become more persuasive by awarding every category to a successful asset.

There was also an incumbent network much larger than any rival cryptocurrency. Governments require taxes in their own currencies and pay employees, suppliers, and creditors in those currencies. Those obligations make people participate whether or not they are enthusiastic about monetary design. The dollar's acceptance was not a small defect Bitcoin would automatically overcome. It was an enormous existing advantage, reinforced by government activity throughout the economy.

The conversation then asks what money is supposed to do: provide a unit of account, store value, and serve as a medium of exchange. Ben quotes Huang's argument, within the episode, that money can be understood as a bubble that has not popped: collective belief is essential, even where government power, physical uses, or software help sustain it. Unlike a share, bitcoin does not grant a contractual claim to future company profits. A price cannot be tested against those profits in the usual way.

Scarcity, portability, divisibility, durability, and acceptance make the comparison more concrete. The hosts see strong digital properties and the possibility of permissionless, international transfers, while recognizing the broad acceptance Bitcoin lacks. Events in countries with unstable financial systems made independence from a state less abstract than it could appear to Americans. The hosts invoke the 2013 Cyprus crisis as a warning about access to bank deposits and the appeal of holding value outside that system. By this point they see gold as the nearer comparison. An institution could hold a volatile alternative asset alongside dollars without expecting it to replace every checkout payment.

Chapter 10 · The closing analysis · January 2021

A bank layer, with an unfinished bill

The original purpose, the human costs, and the investment thesis receive different verdicts.

The hosts' most consequential revision to their starting story concerns the layer Bitcoin occupies. A proposal introduced as inexpensive internet cash had become awkward for small, frequent payments. Ben describes it as better suited to moving large amounts of secure value infrequently. David reaches a similar analogy: Bitcoin resembles the bank or central-bank layer more than a credit card. Other systems might supply a faster transaction layer above or alongside it. They discuss Ethereum and DeFi as possibilities, leaving their detailed mechanics outside this episode.

That conclusion changes the meaning of the invention without diminishing their admiration for its construction. Ben sees a few clever ideas combined on the shoulders of earlier cryptography and proof of work. He also explores a 'why now': computing had become abundant enough to spend enormous resources redundantly checking and propagating records. Deliberate computational inefficiency might enable something previously impractical. But calling that elegant did not answer whether its costs were justified.

David returns to the network's evolution. Silk Road brought activity that later participants did not resemble, just as early Facebook or Airbnb uses differed from what those networks became. He explicitly does not condone the illicit activity. Ben challenges the leap from observation to a startup playbook; David replies that deliberate steps can bring successive sets of users. They agree on the importance of participation while leaving the ease of engineering that progression unresolved. David's other theme is the self-contained incentive: the network rewards work with value inside the network itself.

Ben then puts environmental damage alongside value creation. He cites a 2019 study estimating Bitcoin mining at about 0.2% of global electricity consumption, with other estimates nearer 0.4%, and approximately 23 megatons of annual carbon emissions. These are historical estimates discussed in the recording, not measurements for the present day. Renewable power did not settle the question for him, because using it in a mining facility could displace another use. David asks about the energy cost of traditional finance but acknowledges he has not done that comparison. Their exchange does not establish that either system is environmentally preferable overall.

The political bill is equally unsettled. Money independent of a government could help people whose assets or lawful opportunities were threatened by that government. It could also weaken a service bound up with social stability. Ben describes the tension between people protected by an existing order and people oppressed by it. They leave the consequences as philosophical and historical questions, not a claim that unbundling monetary authority must improve every society.

One distributional benefit did strike them as unusual. Ordinary individuals could participate in Bitcoin's early upside when institutions largely could not. The hosts contrast that access with venture returns usually reserved for founders, accredited investors, and institutional funds. This observation sits beside the losses and operational risks in the story; it is not a claim that everyone who could participate made money.

Their grades therefore depend on the job being graded. For the retrospective investment return over the previous decade, they are emphatically positive. For the white paper's original promise of everyday internet transactions, Ben says D or F so far. David points to Stripe ending Bitcoin support in 2018 because speed, usability, and costs did not suit frequent payments. Ben leaves open the possibility that another decade of development could improve the wider system, while David doubts Bitcoin itself will supply that rapid payment layer.

As a store of value, the answer depends on when the money is needed. A person saving for taxes next quarter cannot comfortably accept a possible 60%–80% drawdown. Ben would not tell his parents to move their retirement into it. David is much more enthusiastic about the long-term comparison with cash in a zero-rate environment, but neither removes the volatility. Their nearest shorthand becomes gold with substantial additional upside and downside, rather than an interchangeable substitute for a dollar bank balance.

Finally they ask whether a venture-style investment could still have enough room to matter. Ben considers a possible 20-fold outcome, which would require a vastly larger total value, rather than treating an arbitrary coin price as self-explanatory. Gold supplies a scenario, not a forecast. They differ over whether to count jewelry in that comparison: Ben describes roughly another 10-fold opportunity excluding jewelry, while David suggests about 15-fold using the broader gold comparison. These are their January 2021 scenarios, not return forecasts. David sees a further, less certain possibility beyond digital gold: Bitcoin could take on some reserve-currency role. There are two layers of hypothetical upside, with execution risk between the present and either one.

Even the stage analogy produces disagreement. David calls January 2021 a growth round, like Stripe in 2017; Ben prefers the later-stage comparison of Stripe in 2020, with substantial growth still possible after broad acceptance. David treats reaching the gold thesis as something like an IPO and going beyond it as the next act. Ben thinks gold with greater utility is imaginable, but neither knows what will happen. Their shared enthusiasm ends with different estimates of how much remains unproven.

The recording closes with an invitation to economists and people with missing context to offer feedback. That is the fitting endpoint for a story that moved from a tax payment to cryptography, crime, exchanges, institutional portfolios, energy, and the state. In January 2021, the hosts had an account of how Bitcoin had reached this point and several competing ways to judge it. The future remained outside the conversation.
