Berkshire HathawayPart III · The changing chessboard

A bigger balance sheet meets a faster world. Warren Buffett’s old strengths, expensive mistakes, and extraordinary Apple investment lead to an unfinished question about succession.

Acquired · Ben Gilbert & David RosenthalReleased June 7, 2021Created Unpublished

A companion to this Part III recording only · about 26 minutes to read.
The hosts’ perspective and figures are preserved as of 2021. Chapter navigation; the official transcript has no reliable complete cue set.

Chapter 01 · 1991–1992

The helicopter leaves without Bill

A friendship exposes two different ways of seeing the future.

On July 5, 1991, Bill Gates did not want to leave Microsoft. His mother, Mary, wanted him to meet Warren Buffett at the Gates family’s Hood Canal home. Buffett was visiting the Northwest with Katharine Graham and was hardly more eager to attend. Gates planned an escape: arrive late by helicopter, eat dinner, and return to work.

Buffett immediately asked about IBM. Gates’s answer was blunt: buy Microsoft and Intel instead. As David Rosenthal tells it, Microsoft was then worth about $10 billion and Intel about $3 billion. Ben Gilbert points to the underlying industrial change. Making the computer did not mean keeping the profits; the operating system and chips could capture them. Buffett’s familiar name was already the wrong place to look.

Gates asked about newspapers and got a surprise. Buffett, whose career had begun delivering papers and whose holdings included newspaper franchises, was worried about their future. He feared television and cable rather than the Internet, but he could see a threat to a business he loved. The two fell into conversation and largely ignored the other guests. At dinner, Bill Gates Sr. asked what had most contributed to everyone’s success. Buffett and Gates each answered with the same word: focus. The helicopter departed; Gates stayed.

Buffett bought 100 Microsoft shares for his personal account so he could follow his friend. Berkshire bought none, and he bought no Intel. Ben finds that tiny personal position confounding: it gave almost none of the economic benefit while complicating the information Buffett could discuss with an insider. Friendship brought him close to technological change without making Berkshire an investor in it.

Gates also joined Buffett’s circle of business friends. When Bill Ruane floated Kodak as an investment, Gates pronounced it toast. But he distinguished television networks from photographic film: networks owned content they could reuse as delivery moved onto the Internet; knowing how to make film might become irrelevant. David marvels at the foresight, while Ben adds Buffett’s own distinction between predicting rain and building an ark. Seeing a future is not the same as capturing it.

The episode opens after Buffett’s Salomon rescue has made him a public hero, with arena-sized shareholder meetings and Berkshire shares above $10,000. It deliberately steps back to this 1991 meeting. Its central tension is already visible: Buffett’s fortunes grew by recognizing durable economics; Gates’s world demanded continual adaptation.

Chapter 02 · The 1980s → 1997

Cherry Coke and the ham sandwich

The classic moat works—and becomes the lens that limits its owner.

To explain the contrast, the hosts return to Don Keough, Buffett’s old neighbor on Farnam Street in Omaha. Keough sold coffee and had six children. According to the story David recounts, he declined to invest with Buffett because his children said Mr. Buffett was always at home; a man apparently working so little was an unlikely steward of college savings. Coffee acquisitions carried Keough first to Houston and then to Coca-Cola in Atlanta. By the mid-1980s he was president and chief operating officer alongside CEO Roberto Goizueta.

Reunited at a White House dinner, Keough found Buffett still drinking Pepsi with added cherry syrup. Cherry Coke supplied a conversion opportunity. Buffett began studying Coca-Cola, liked the success of Diet Coke, but found the stock expensive. Then management replaced the original formula with New Coke. Taste tests had suggested customers would prefer it; customers’ attachment to the old product proved stronger. Pepsi pressed its advantage, bottlers fought with Coke, and the shares fell.

David places Buffett’s $1.2 billion open-market purchase, roughly 6% of Coke, in 1987 and describes takeover rumors involving Ron Perelman as part of the opportunity. Buffett joined the board at Goizueta and Keough’s invitation. The following year’s shareholder letter supplied the enduring formulation: for outstanding businesses with outstanding management, the favorite holding period was forever. This companion follows the episode’s dating and account rather than importing a separate history.

The recovery was more than restoring a recipe. Coca-Cola expanded internationally with a model that shipped concentrate while local bottlers handled water, carbonation, and distribution. The brand could travel without the company itself shipping every finished drink. David describes more than tenfold gains in the first decade, followed by only about three and a half times over the next quarter century. A wonderful early purchase did not imply an unchanged future rate of return.

Ben uses Buffett’s 1995 description of a moat to explain the purchase: a low-cost position, natural franchise, service capability, place in the consumer’s mind, technological advantage, or another durable protection for profits. Coke’s brand made that abstract idea concrete. It also encouraged Buffett’s preference for a business so strong that, in his phrase, a ham sandwich could run it.

At Sun Valley in 1997, Keough moderated a panel with Goizueta, Buffett, and Gates. Gates invoked the ham sandwich comparison and contrasted it with Microsoft’s high-wire act, which he thought would require a younger leader before he turned 60. In the hosts’ retelling, the remark deeply offended Goizueta. David reads the awkward social moment as a turning point: the most valuable future businesses might be those whose leaders could navigate change, rather than those insulated from it.

Ben gives Buffett’s position its strongest explanation. Valuation is sensitive to confidence in future cash flows. Buffett preferred a modest outcome with a high probability to a huge outcome whose probability was unknowable. David, quoting Andrew Marks’s characterization, calls him the greatest status quo investor. They mean that he could value expansion on a reasonably stable chessboard, not that his businesses never grew. Their argument that the Internet changed the chessboard is the hosts’ interpretation, made in 2021.

Chapter 03 · 1996–1998

A second currency

Protecting shareholders creates cash; using shares creates a much larger risk.

Berkshire’s expensive, unsplit shares created a market for intermediaries. Some pooled Berkshire shares and sold smaller interests; others imitated its reported securities portfolio. Buffett disliked people being sold a delayed imitation without Berkshire’s insurance float and other advantages. Yet he also liked his existing shareholder base and resisted a conventional split.

In 1996 he offered a new class of Baby B shares, initially with one-thirtieth of an A share’s economic value and greatly reduced voting rights. The supply would meet demand, rather than allowing a scarce allotment to become a speculative prize. Berkshire itself received the offering proceeds. Ben cautions that this was not free money: issuing shares diluted existing ownership.

The timing mattered. Ben says Berkshire ordinarily traded around one to one and a half times book value, while the B offering came near twice book. Buffett preferred buying businesses with cash because he prized the businesses Berkshire already owned; richly valued stock could nonetheless become a useful acquisition currency. The prospectus was unusually candid: Buffett and Munger did not consider the A shares undervalued and would not buy them, or recommend them to family, at that price. Ben insists on the distinction between that statement and declaring the stock overpriced.

The much larger application came in 1998: roughly $22 billion of Berkshire shares for General Re, about 20% of Berkshire. It was an enormous leap in acquisition size, paid entirely in stock. Charlie Munger’s memorable response was that Buffett had called him very late in the process.

David attributes an important motive to the account discussed in the episode: Buffett feared an equity-market crash and wanted a more conservative investment mix without publicly selling his celebrated stock holdings. Gen Re brought a large bond portfolio as well as insurance float. Buying it with Berkshire stock changed the mix indirectly. But bonds and premiums came attached to an operating insurer’s promises and liabilities. The clever financial structure could not make bad underwriting harmless.

Chapter 04 · 1985–2000s

The cost of staying hands off

Ajit Jain prices unusual risks; General Re conceals risks it has not priced.

Ajit Jain had arrived at Berkshire in 1985 after growing up in India, attending IIT and Harvard Business School, and working at IBM and McKinsey. The hosts describe a rare combination of analytical skill and appetite for unusual insurance business. Jain did not need the same policy repeated thousands of times. He wanted large, difficult risks that he believed he could price better than other insurers.

His advertisement sought casualty business with premiums above $1 million. These super-catastrophe policies could be frightening in size; the opportunity depended on understanding what was being promised and charging enough for it. David recounts long-running late-night calls with Buffett to review the business, and describes Jain writing terrorism cover after September 11 as demand and premiums rose. The point is selective underwriting, not the absence of risk.

General Re supplied the opposite example. Rather than immediately put it under Jain, Buffett promised its existing managers that he would remain strictly hands off. That familiar acquisition promise fit founders who cared deeply about their businesses. Here the managers were not founders, and the promise deferred intervention in a troubled institution.

David recounts an immediate roughly $300 million underwriting hit associated with Unit Cover, about another $1 billion from film-receipt insurance, and close to $2 billion of September 11 losses. Ben emphasizes gaps in policies: Gen Re had accepted exposures for which it had not properly been paid. An AIG accounting scandal then brought regulators and reputational damage, precisely the danger Buffett most wanted to avoid after Salomon.

Buffett eventually replaced the old management with Joe Brandon and Tad Montross, whom the hosts credit with repairing the business. In 2016 Jain finally received responsibility for General Re too. The acquisition’s failure was not simply a wrong market forecast. It showed the limit of a management philosophy when a hands-off promise met weak incentives and poorly understood contracts.

In 1999 Buffett also bought MidAmerican Energy Holdings. Buying the electric company seemed painfully dull beside the technology boom, but the hosts regard it as a satisfactory investment. It unexpectedly supplied the real-estate brokerage business that later carried Berkshire’s name—and, more consequentially, managers David Sokol and Greg Abel. These were not yet famous succession names. They entered through an operating acquisition.

Chapter 05 · 1999–2006

Being right about a bubble

Avoiding the excess is different from understanding the winners.

At the 2000 annual meeting, shareholders demanded to know why Berkshire owned no soaring technology stocks. Buffett rejected speculation. Munger’s earthier answer separated good businesses from speculative rubbish, then warned that mixing them would not protect the good ones from a collapse. Ben gives the financial objection substance: some public companies had no profits, some barely had revenues, and some sold products with negative unit economics. Banks and early shareholders still had incentives to carry them into the public market.

David’s comparison makes the qualification unusually sharp. A dollar invested in Berkshire at the peak of the technology bubble would, by his May 2021 calculation, have beaten the Nasdaq, the S&P, and narrowly Microsoft. Change the starting date to 1997 or even 2001, however, and Microsoft wins. The hosts do not treat avoiding one terrible entry point as proof that technology was structurally uninvestable.

Buffett’s related argument was that innovation need not enrich investors. Hundreds of early car manufacturers dwindled to a few, and the airline industry’s extraordinary social usefulness had not translated into durable aggregate profits in his cited historical comparison. Ben stresses that a firm needs protection against competitors arbitraging away its gains. David agrees with the proposition and rejects the inference that technology could not supply such protection. Both the bubble warning and the missed opportunity can be true.

Then the story changes register. Susie Buffett died in July 2004. She and Warren had lived apart for years but remained married and emotionally close. Her death was personally devastating and undid his assumed philanthropic succession plan: she would outlive him and distribute the fortune through a foundation.

The hosts describe Buffett’s frustration with giving money away when he could not measure results as cleanly as investment returns, and his competing desires to compound wealth and be admired. Those are their psychological interpretations. What followed was concrete. He invited Gates onto Berkshire’s board and, in 2006, announced plans to give away 85% of his Berkshire stock, then worth about $37 billion. Five-sixths of the gift would go to the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation; the rest to his children’s foundations and the Susan Buffett Foundation.

Rather than build an enormous institution bearing his own name, he trusted an existing one and delegated the work. Ben sees a meaningful sacrifice of a personal foundation’s legacy; David sees an ingenious alignment of generosity, public esteem, and relief from work Buffett did not want to do. The Giving Pledge, launched with the Gateses in 2010, extended the social example. The hosts celebrate the giving while retaining the tension between philanthropy and Buffett’s private scoreboard.

Chapter 06 · 2008

Cash becomes a contract

Berkshire lends credibility as well as money.

By the mid-2000s, buying family businesses for hundreds of millions or a few billion could no longer absorb Berkshire’s growing capital. Good managers could keep compounding their businesses, but there were not enough furniture stores and jewelry chains to move a company this large. The financial crisis briefly supplied a market suited to Berkshire’s scale, preparation, and speed.

Bear Stearns’s March 2008 collapse illustrated a familiar danger: losses mattered, but the immediate mechanism was counterparties ceasing to trust its paper. A business dependent on constantly renewed transactions could fail with astonishing speed. Berkshire had about $37 billion in cash. The hosts see a lesson from Salomon in Buffett’s response. He would provide senior or preferred capital, often with equity warrants, rather than simply become the controlling common shareholder called before Congress if the rescue failed.

April’s Mars acquisition of Wrigley offered the first opening. Mars was buying Wrigley for about $23 billion and needed financing when banks were constrained. Berkshire supplied roughly $6.5 billion: about $4.4 billion of debt at 11.45% and roughly $2 billion of preferred equity at 5%. The hosts report an eventual 14% internal rate of return. A stable candy business could still face expensive financing in a frightened market.

The crisis did not make every call attractive. In the Lehman anecdote, Buffett asked for details by hotel fax while on vacation; a related text message went unnoticed until his daughter found it much later. David explicitly refuses to make the missed text the explanation for Lehman’s failure. Buffett had already studied Lehman’s 2007 annual report after an earlier approach in March and declined the risk. He says he would not have invested in September either. He also passed on AIG.

Goldman Sachs was different. Berkshire invested $5 billion in preferred stock paying 10% annually. Goldman could redeem it for $5.5 billion; separately, Berkshire received warrants to buy $5 billion of common stock at $115 a share. David reports about $3 billion in profits overall, with much of the money coming back within a few years. The preferred dividend and redemption protected the economics differently from the warrants, whose payoff depended on common-stock prices.

On October 1, Berkshire put $3 billion into GE with a similar 10% preferred coupon and warrants struck around $22.25. The equity upside did not resemble Goldman’s: GE shares were about $13 when the hosts recorded. Other crisis-era financing included Swiss Re at 12%, Harley-Davidson at 15%, Tiffany at 10%, and Sealed Air at 12%. Ben also notes Berkshire’s later GE rail-car purchase, which he regards much less favorably.

In the episode’s aggregate, about $18 billion deployed produced roughly $25 billion returned—about $7 billion of gains, not $25 billion of profit. David calls it good rather than miraculous, with the timing of repayments varying. Ben praises preparation and rapid action. The hosts argue that Buffett’s name could reassure counterparties while allowing Berkshire to obtain terms ordinary providers of capital could not. Downside protection reduced exposure; it did not turn troubled-company credit into a guarantee.

Chapter 07 · 2009–2011

The last crisis grand slam

Bank of America and the search for people who could follow Buffett.

The biggest follow-on success came in 2011, not 2008. During the renewed financial anxiety around Europe’s debt crisis, Berkshire put $5 billion into Bank of America preferred stock paying 5%, with warrants for common stock at a $7.14 strike. At the episode’s roughly $42 share price, David puts the cumulative profit near $26 billion. That is profit, unlike the roughly $25 billion total returned on the earlier crisis investments. It dwarfed their combined gains and helped offset the disappointment of Wells Fargo’s scandals.

The hosts also briefly credit the 2009 railroad acquisition as a good investment. But even this strong stretch made the succession question urgent. Buffett was around 80 and had long joked about retiring five years after death. His plan separated the operating-company CEO from one or more investment chiefs. Ben clarifies the division: wholly owned businesses on one side, the securities portfolio on the other.

An open invitation in the 2006 shareholder report had sought a younger investor capable of eventually managing a very large portfolio. In 2010 Berkshire hired Todd Combs, age 39, from the small Castle Point Capital. He had worked for Florida’s bank regulator and Progressive before managing a hedge fund. David describes its five-year cumulative return as 34%, respectable but far from Buffett’s early annual returns. The surprising appointment was not simply the anointing of another young Buffett.

There had appeared to be a different candidate. A Wall Street Journal story that summer identified Li Lu, the Tiananmen Square participant who came to America, founded Himalaya Capital, and became close to Munger. He had introduced Munger to BYD. Munger had described Li’s joining as a foregone conclusion. It did not happen. David offers compensation as unconfirmed scuttlebutt, and likewise labels talk of David Einhorn as another candidate a rumor. Neither is presented here as an established explanation.

Ben describes the investment managers’ compensation as tied to outperformance of the S&P over a rolling period with a clawback characteristic. For an already successful independent manager, surrendering a fund’s economics might be unattractive; for Combs, managing billions inside Berkshire could be an exceptional opportunity. Incentives mattered even at an institution built around personal admiration.

Ted Weschler arrived by an even stranger route. He paid about $2.6 million twice for charity lunches with Buffett, benefiting San Francisco’s Glide Memorial Church. Weschler had run Peninsula Capital Advisors for 12 years, multiplied its capital more than twelvefold, and held a concentrated portfolio including DaVita and DirecTV. The conversations impressed Buffett enough to produce a job. The hosts do not know exactly where the managers’ autonomy ends and Buffett’s influence begins. Each began with about $1 billion and received more capital as trust grew.

Chapter 08 · 2011–2020

An envelope, and expensive mistakes

A succession scandal coincides with a run of disappointing capital allocation.

David Sokol seemed the likely operating successor. Then Berkshire’s $9 billion Lubrizol acquisition in 2011 exposed his personal trading in the target. In the hosts’ account, he bought shares and proposed the acquisition to Buffett; the troubling issue was trading with knowledge of the acquisition process. Sokol left Berkshire. David notes that he was not prosecuted or sent to prison, and the episode does not supply grounds to turn the story into a conviction. The succession focus shifted to his former deputy at MidAmerican, Greg Abel.

Buffett still controlled most of the investment money. In November 2011 he finally invested in IBM: $10.7 billion, based on what he regarded as strong, defensible client relationships. To the hosts, buying that legacy franchise while cloud computing was already changing enterprise technology was a particularly costly misunderstanding. Berkshire exited by 2018, with David reporting roughly $2 billion of losses. Ben and David emphasize the opportunity cost of committing so much money to IBM in that period.

They are similarly critical of the approximately $10 billion committed alongside 3G to the Kraft-Heinz combination. The stake was worth roughly $11 billion at recording: avoiding a nominal loss did not make years of tied-up capital successful. Precision Castparts, acquired for about $37 billion in 2015, produced a roughly $10 billion write-down in the year before the episode. These are distinct disappointments—stock losses, stagnant value, and an accounting write-down—not interchangeable cash losses.

Airlines brought an especially pointed reversal of Buffett’s earlier industry critique. Berkshire began buying them in 2016 and sold during the pandemic collapse. Ben faults the purchase more than the sale: without government support, airlines could have failed, so exiting in 2020 could be reasonable even if prices subsequently recovered. David mentions suggestions that Ted first developed the airline idea, but treats the attribution as scuttlebutt rather than a known fact.

Ben adds omissions to these commissions. Buffett understood American Express but passed over the Visa and Mastercard public offerings; Google, Facebook, and Amazon also went largely unexploited during their rise. Those comparisons express the hosts’ frustration with the available alternatives. They are not a claim that any particular counterfactual portfolio was inevitable.

The criticism is severe, but not absolute. The hosts credit the railroad, crisis financing, and Bank of America, and quote Buffett acknowledging that Ted and Todd had outperformed him. Berkshire’s problem was no longer a shortage of cash or legal flexibility. It was choosing investments large enough to matter while the economic landscape was moving.

Chapter 09 · 2016–2021

Apple, seen through an old lens

A technology investment becomes the largest answer to Berkshire’s technology problem.

In May 2016, one of Berkshire’s investment managers bought about $1 billion of Apple. Buffett called him one of the fellows in the office. The episode never establishes whether it was Ted or Todd. David guesses Ted from their perceived areas of focus, and Ben discusses conversations he had heard; this companion keeps that attribution uncertain.

The initial position helped persuade Buffett to commit much more. Over two years Berkshire invested about $36 billion, almost the purchase price of Precision Castparts. David cites an Apple holding worth about $120 billion in the preceding annual report and about $89 billion of gains. Those approximate figures, as spoken, do not reconcile simply by subtraction. They are the hosts’ reported figures, not a newly audited return calculation. His further suggestion that the gains approached the absolute gains of Buffett’s entire earlier career is explicitly an estimate.

Ben sees more than irony in the result. Late years dominate the absolute dollar gains of a portfolio that has compounded for decades. Still, an enormous portion of Berkshire’s recent success came from a technology company bought when Buffett was in his mid-80s.

The sales pitch, as Ben understood it, was a consumer brand with deep loyalty and little propensity to switch. Buffett did not need to take apart an iPhone to notice consumer behavior. Ben returns to the 1995 moat description and finds Apple almost a perfect fit: place in the customer’s mind, technological advantage, and protection against losing the customer.

David is less convinced that Buffett understood the machinery behind Apple’s success. Yet he refuses to withhold credit. Borrowing his father’s baseball analogy, someone who can hit .300 may use an unconventional swing; the result matters. Buffett built an organization and made a decision that put Berkshire in a position to be right. Ben’s brand interpretation and David’s skepticism about technical understanding remain different judgments, joined by agreement about the investment’s extraordinary result.

In January 2018 Abel and Jain formally became vice chairmen for noninsurance and insurance businesses. In 2021 Munger let slip at the annual meeting that Greg would preserve Berkshire’s culture, effectively revealing the operating successor. Meanwhile the younger managers were making investments such as Amazon and Snowflake. Ben is tentative about the precise authorship of those positions, but sees evidence that a future Berkshire could operate outside Buffett’s old boundaries.

Chapter 10 · The hosts’ 2021 assessment

What comes after the halo?

The bull and bear cases depend on what Berkshire can change—and what it loses.

Buybacks offered one answer to the shortage of attractive investments. If private investors were paying too much and Berkshire looked cheap, repurchasing its own shares increased each remaining owner’s claim on businesses already understood. Ben quotes Christopher Bloomstran’s argument in favor of that use of capital. He then supplies the complication: Berkshire’s publicly traded holdings are marked to market. Buying more Berkshire also means owning more exposure to those existing companies at their current valuations.

The optimistic succession case is therefore not simply continuity. Abel might hold less cash, act less conservatively, and give good investors greater scope. Ted and Todd had already helped produce Apple. Ben wonders whether Buffett’s recent disappointments had left him gun-shy and unusually dependent on the familiar. A successor might improve returns rather than destroy them.

The opposing case begins with humility about cycles. Buffett had compounded through many environments. Perhaps the Internet-driven period had made a sound method look foolish and changing it too far would sacrifice its eventual return to favor. Ben does not make that his central view, but explicitly preserves it as a risk to the optimistic story.

David adds the halo. Buffett’s reputation created real access and contract terms in the crisis, as well as the less measurable loyalty of shareholders who tolerated weak periods and attended meetings. Ben calls Berkshire both a religion and an investment. Without Buffett it might become merely an investment. A new management team inherits assets and a legal structure; it cannot assume that every benefit attached to his name transfers intact.

Another constraint is the lack of a large internal growth engine capable of absorbing capital at exciting rates. Jain’s underwriting had once supplied an entrepreneurial expansion story; GEICO remained important, but the hosts did not regard it as an adequate destination for all the money Berkshire generated. The company increasingly had to compete with outside investors for opportunities rather than fund its own new growth.

Valuation offered no easy escape. A sum-of-parts argument that Berkshire was cheap depended on valuing its cash fully and accepting high market prices for holdings such as Apple, as well as expanded industry multiples for the railroad. Ben worries that cheapness can be manufactured by generous assumptions about every component. David answers that selling creates the next question: where is the better place for the capital? Both treat alternatives as essential. Their discussion is a dated debate about 2021 asset prices, not an updated valuation or a recommendation.

Chapter 11 · Structure & incentives

An infinite horizon needs someone at the desk

The flexible company survives its original playbook; authority is harder to divide.

The hosts’ strongest playbook conclusion is that strategies must change with scale. Cigar butts gave way to enduring franchises; buying small family businesses could not absorb the mature company’s capital. Berkshire’s achievement was to build a structure flexible enough to change again. It was an operating company, not a fund with a termination date. It could buy a whole company, own a minority stake, lend, wait, or repurchase shares.

That structure also avoided a fund manager’s fee drag. A shareholder received the investment result without surrendering a recurring management fee and carried interest. If Buffett held cash, he was not simply collecting fees on idle assets. Ben sees aligned incentives and an indefinite horizon as major contributions that could outlast Buffett’s own investing style.

But dividing the job creates politics. One person had once compared acquisitions, internal opportunities, securities, and cash across the entire balance sheet. A successor organization has separate pools of money, managers whose compensation depends on their portfolios, and operating leaders with different expertise. Ben calls them potential fiefdoms. Capital that belongs economically to the same shareholders may be harder to move if doing so reduces an individual manager’s opportunity or reward.

Abel would presumably have final authority as CEO, but the hosts question whether an experienced operator could arbitrate investments with the same judgment, speed, and conviction as Buffett. The famous requirement that his successor have experience running a large operating business did not resemble Buffett’s own background. They leave the institutional answer unresolved rather than assuming four capable executives automatically recreate one exceptional allocator.

Ben also distinguishes subsidiary cultures from a universal Berkshire culture. A jewelry-store employee need not share a chemical company’s daily working habits. What he sees the center demanding is narrower and more concrete: protect Berkshire’s reputation, defer taxes and keep money compounding where possible, and send cash to headquarters for reallocation. Decentralization worked; preserving coherent capital allocation above it was the open question.

Chapter 12 · Closing judgments

A lifetime, a period, a portfolio

The grades differ because the questions differ.

For the whole career, David combines the partnership years and Berkshire years into an estimated 22.3% annualized return across 63 years. His illustrative $100 invested in 1959 and carried through the whole path becomes about $26.2 million by the episode’s endpoint. This is the hosts’ stitched historical comparison, not an available fund offering or a promise about future compounding.

They give the lifetime an A. David initially withholds an A-plus because he expects future investors may do better in a faster-changing world. Ben pushes back: outperforming over that duration today might require more leverage, more risk, and more luck among a much larger field of competitors. David proposes Sequoia as a possible institutional comparison, while expressly admitting he has not calculated its unavailable aggregate fund returns. They recognize different asset classes and a firm rather than one individual. The comparison remains a hypothesis, not evidence that Sequoia beat Buffett.

Ben ultimately settles on A too, because the finish had weakened. For January 1993 through the recording, he calculates about 13.5% annualized from Berkshire’s stock price, over roughly 28 and a half years. Both award that period a B: it beat the market but fell well below Buffett’s earlier standard. They do not dismiss nearly three decades as one bad cycle.

For the five years beginning around the Apple purchase, David says Berkshire’s overall stock performance roughly matched the S&P despite Apple’s enormous gains. He estimates that removing Apple leaves Berkshire behind by about half a turn of the return multiple. That is his analytical comparison, not an independently investable Apple-free Berkshire index. They give the recent period a C, noting again that shareholders were not also paying hedge-fund fees.

The final question is personal, and the hosts disagree in action while largely agreeing in rationale. Ben reveals that he has never owned Berkshire. He sees a conservatively managed way for already wealthy people to preserve and compound wealth, with no dividend forcing annual cash distributions and shareholders able to sell shares when needed. He is less persuaded that it is the right growth vehicle for someone early in building wealth.

David owns Berkshire and intends to hold. His other assets are heavily weighted toward growth technology, digital assets, and real estate. He treats this smaller allocation as a safety and liquidity sleeve, replacing money he had previously held in cash. That is his portfolio choice, not proof that an equity is cash or cannot lose value. Both repeatedly label this conversation as personal analysis rather than investment advice.

Ben closes the assessment with Ho Nam’s observation: Buffett was an investor who built a company worth more than $500 billion, an achievement unlike merely selecting a winning stock. David’s final learning is different but related. Buffett preached faith in America as a place where capital could grow; David sees the Internet as the expanding place for his own generation. His phrase, never bet against the Internet, is his 2021 conviction, not Buffett’s lesson and not a certainty the companion adopts.

The episode ends beyond the financial debate. David recommends Phil Fisher’s Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits while admitting he has not finished it, and praises the Xbox Series S and Game Pass. Ben offers Goodfellas and its soundtrack; they joke about securing Layla for the fade-out, then concede they probably will not. After thanking listeners and inviting them to share the show, they sign off late at night. The journey has changed how they think, even as its final investment judgments remain qualified.
