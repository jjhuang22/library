Hermès

The time inside the object

A harness maker survives the automobile, finds a global audience, and learns to grow by teaching people to make things slowly.

Released February 20, 2024Created October 12, 2026About 19 minutes to readAcquired · Hermès · Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal

Scope: origins through the February 2024 episode, including its concluding business analysis. Later company pages are used only to check enduring history and processes. Timeline cues follow the archived original YouTube upload, as indexed by uListen, with a duration of 4:11:09. Current podcast editions have different advertisements and clocks. The timeline is a reading guide, not an audio player.

01
1801–1880

A craft before a brand

The customer needed a harness that worked before the world needed a symbol of luxury.

Thierry Hermès was born in Krefeld in 1801, far from the Paris address that would eventually define his name. He trained as a craftsman and opened his own harness workshop in Paris in 1837. His work belonged to the infrastructure of a city moved by horses: leather had to hold under tension, fit correctly, endure weather, and look appropriate on the carriages of customers who cared deeply about appearances. The original product was useful equipment, with failure carrying consequences beyond an unhappy shopping experience.

That beginning matters because luxury was not an escape from performance. Beautiful finishing and reliable construction were part of the same commission. A harness was visible, but it also had a job to do. The workshop could not build a durable reputation by ornament alone. Thierry’s work received an award at the 1867 Universal Exhibition, an external recognition that helped a small maker communicate quality beyond the people who already knew his hands.

Paris supplied a favorable stage. Its growing wealth and increasingly public social life made private possessions legible to other people. A well-equipped carriage signaled the owner’s position while giving the maker an introduction to the next wealthy customer. In this setting, distribution began with relationships and observation. The business did not first create a mass audience and then search for something to sell it; it earned credibility through a narrow, demanding use case.

Thierry died in 1878. His son Charles-Émile moved the business to 24 Faubourg Saint-Honoré in 1880, bringing workshop and shop into the address that became the house’s enduring center. The move strengthened both sides of the enterprise: making and selling could remain connected. My interpretation is that this proximity created an early discipline. The person accepting a customer’s request could see what fulfilling it required, and a maker could understand whose life the finished object would enter.

Business lesson

Make quality observable

A difficult, practical commission can create trust that later products inherit.

02
1880s–1920s

When the customer leaves the horse behind

The family had to decide which part of its identity was portable.

Equipment for riding suggested an adjacent product: a large leather carrier for saddles and boots. The Haut à courroies brought handles, straps, a generous body, and a distinctive closure into a form that could move with its owner. It is a useful place to see the family resemblance to later handbags, without treating the original equestrian carrier as a modern Birkin.

The automobile then threatened the economic center of the house. Keeping the same product would not preserve the same business. Adolphe and Émile, the third-generation brothers, differed about the company’s future; Émile ultimately carried it forward. The consequential choice was to follow the changing habits of wealthy customers, using leather expertise to make objects for travel, sport, and everyday movement rather than binding the firm’s future to carriage transport.

Émile’s curiosity extended beyond leather. Hermès’ own history records that he encountered a closure on a military vehicle during travel in Canada, and obtained exclusive rights in 1922 to the system now known as the zipper. A borrowed invention could make the house’s products more useful. This is an important corrective to the picture of Hermès as a company that opposes technology: it could adopt a new mechanism while retaining its judgment about materials, proportion, and construction.

The transition also changed the meaning of the horse. An economic dependence became a source of visual language and accumulated skill. Saddlery could inform a bag even when its buyer never rode. The distinction is subtle but essential: heritage remained an input into creation rather than a requirement that the customer live in the past. A company facing a shrinking market can ask whether customers value the obsolete activity itself, or a capability demonstrated by serving that activity well.

Business lesson

Carry the capability forward

Preserve the material knowledge and customer trust; adapt the object to a new way of living.

03
1920s–1950s

A house with more than one material

Silk, silver, and a conspicuous orange box broadened the vocabulary.

The interwar expansion made Hermès more than a leather workshop. Its history dates jewelry to 1927, watches and sandals to 1928, and the first silk scarf to 1937. Robert Dumas, Émile’s son-in-law, helped turn this broader ambition into recognizable objects. His Chaîne d’ancre bracelet translated a working ship’s chain into jewelry; the scarf offered a different kind of canvas, where illustration and color could carry a story that leather construction alone could not.

The scarf changed the frequency and variety of creation. A durable bag can keep essentially the same outline for decades. Silk permits an ongoing succession of drawings and color combinations, with the customer choosing an image as well as an object. Hermès’ silk work is rooted in the Lyon region, with specialist production and printing. These are complementary businesses: one emphasizes enduring form, the other repeated creative renewal.

The orange packaging illustrates a different path to recognition. Hermès’ later account describes paper shortages around the Second World War that made its previous subdued colors unavailable; orange was what its supplier had. A constraint became a lasting signature. The useful business interpretation is not that scarcity automatically creates an iconic color. It is that a firm can recognize a practical solution worth keeping, then apply it consistently until customers recognize it without explanation.

Together these objects made the house easier to encounter without reducing it to one handbag. A scarf could be a first purchase, jewelry another expression of its design language, and packaging a reminder of a shared origin. Breadth has a cost: each category must earn credibility in its own medium. The house could transfer attention across categories, but attention alone would not make a scarf print well or a silver bracelet feel right. Expansion required capabilities as well as a label.

Business lesson

Broaden through distinct crafts

An adjacent category earns its place by adding a real capability to the house.

04
1930s–1970s

A bag acquires a public life

An existing design met a photograph the company could not have scripted.

Robert Dumas designed the structured handbag that became the Kelly in the 1930s. Its straps and closure belonged to a recognizable family of leather objects, but the scale and use were different from transporting riding equipment. In 1956, a photograph of Grace Kelly carrying the bag traveled around the world. The association joined an established product to the newly married princess of Monaco. Hermès’ account describes her holding it over her stomach to conceal her pregnancy.

The sequence matters. The product existed before the famous image; the image supplied a social meaning that went far beyond its construction. Hermès did not need to change the bag into a completely different object to benefit. For this companion, the 1930s design and the 1956 photograph are the dependable historical anchors; naming histories vary, so a precise formal renaming date is not necessary to explain the turning point.

An enduring design gives cultural attention somewhere to accumulate. When a company replaces its central products constantly, yesterday’s endorsement can expire with yesterday’s collection. A recognizable form allows several generations of customers to encounter the same object in different circumstances. That does not guarantee continued desire, but it gives the business a reservoir of recognition that new colors, materials, and sizes can refresh without erasing.

Robert succeeded Émile as head of the house in 1951. His period extended the range while maintaining a visible connection to drawing and making. There is a tension here that the later company never fully resolves: fashion rewards novelty, while a durable object invites repetition. Hermès’ answer was to let expressive details move faster than the core forms. My reading is that the company made room for change inside continuity, giving customers something new to discover without requiring them to forget what they already admired.

Business lesson

Let recognition accumulate

A lasting form can absorb new cultural meanings without resetting its identity.

05
1978–2006

Jean-Louis opens the world

The fifth generation scaled the house’s imagination and its reach.

Jean-Louis Dumas took over in 1978. His challenge was different from Émile’s: the market for luxury objects was expanding internationally, but a historic family maker could still become a provincial institution if it failed to meet customers abroad. Global stores required local relationships, product judgment, and a convincing physical setting. Expanding distribution was therefore an organizational undertaking, not merely a decision to ship more boxes.

The Birkin’s origin captures Jean-Louis’ attention to an individual customer. Hermès dates the creation to 1984, describing a Paris-to-London flight where Jane Birkin explained that she lacked a suitable bag as a young mother. He sketched a spacious rectangular holdall with room for her needs. The practical prompt became one of the house’s most famous products. It is a hand-carried tote, or carried in the crook of the arm; Acquired’s correction explicitly rejects its earlier suggestion that it should be worn over the shoulder.

Its celebrity name can obscure the original design problem: enough room, a usable opening, and an object appropriate to the owner’s life. The lesson is more specific than listening to customers. A leader with product knowledge could translate a complaint directly into a design proposal. The creative and commercial conversation occurred in the same encounter, reducing the distance between noticing a need and deciding how to respond.

Growth also required space and capital. Leather workshops moved to Pantin in 1992, and Hermès listed shares in 1993 while retaining family control. The house’s international presence expanded into major architectural statements, including New York in 2000 and Tokyo’s Ginza in 2001. These stores made a French origin available in other cities without reproducing the original address literally. Their challenge was to make the same house credible in a different place, with a local clientele whose tastes could not simply be assumed.

Business lesson

Give creation a wider stage

International growth requires institutions that translate the house into a local customer relationship.

06
2006–2014

The shares that could change the house

A takeover threat forced the family to put patience into legal form.

Patrick Thomas provided a leadership bridge after Jean-Louis, who stepped down in 2006. That transition mattered because the company needed professional coordination without abandoning the family’s creative authority. Public ownership meanwhile brought another complication: a minority stake could be purchased by someone with a very different idea of the company’s future. In 2010, Bernard Arnault’s LVMH disclosed a substantial Hermès holding, assembled partly through equity derivatives.

For the Hermès family, the issue was bigger than an attractive valuation. Joining a conglomerate could alter who decided the pace of production, the use of the brand, and the allocation of capital. The family’s response was to consolidate rather than disperse. In 2011, descendants pooled approximately 50.2% of Hermès shares in H51. Acquired’s published corrections establish that its rules restrict shareholders to descendants of Émile Hermès and Julie Hollande, and describe a lock-in running to 2041.

This was an expensive commitment in opportunity terms. A family member who could otherwise sell a liquid asset accepted constraints in exchange for collective control. Dividends gained importance because they supplied cash without requiring a sale of the underlying company. The structure made an operating preference enforceable: managers could plan around a stable controlling group rather than continually wonder whether the next relative to sell would change the balance of power.

The dispute ended with a 2014 agreement. LVMH’s official distribution documents recorded a 23.18% holding and proposed distributing Hermès shares to LVMH shareholders, rather than keeping that block inside the group. This defense did not create the craftsmanship that customers valued, but it protected the authority to continue investing in it. My interpretation is that governance was an enabling technology: the house’s production model needed an ownership model willing to wait for it.

Business lesson

Make the time horizon enforceable

A patient strategy is more credible when the controlling owners cannot casually abandon it.

Explanatory interlude · conceptual, not a legal organization chart

Three clocks, one decision

The owner’s clock

Family control and share-transfer rules support an investment horizon longer than a trading cycle.

The maker’s clock

Recruitment, teaching, practice, and inspection determine when additional capacity becomes trustworthy.

The client’s clock

Desire, availability, and confidence in service determine whether waiting remains acceptable.

Interpretation: ownership gives production time; production gives the customer a reason to value that time. Losing any one connection weakens the system.

07
2011–2023

The factory is a school

Growth depended on transmitting judgment, one person at a time.

The family’s next answer was expansion through training. Hermès describes a leather-school method developed as demand led it to increase production capacity from 2011. Many recruits arrived from other occupations, often without prior leatherworking experience. The task was to turn beginners into people capable of observing material, handling tools, and repeating exact movements while learning when a particular piece required a different response.

This makes the production constraint unusually human. Hiring more people is only the beginning; capable teachers need time to teach, and novices need enough repetition to build judgment. A building can be financed quickly, but its inhabitants cannot acquire skill on a financing schedule. Workshops and training make growth possible while also regulating how quickly it can happen.

Under Axel Dumas, executive chairman from 2013, and artistic director Pierre-Alexis Dumas, the house enlarged this system. The École Hermès des savoir-faire became a recognized apprenticeship center in 2021. In its 2023 results, the company reported two new leather workshops, in Louviers and Saint-Junien, alongside further expansion plans. Capacity was increasing; it would be misleading to describe the strategy as refusing all additional supply. The distinction is that the firm expanded the method rather than simply replacing it with a faster one.

The financial scale was substantial by the episode’s release. Reported 2023 revenue was €13.427 billion, recurring operating income €5.650 billion, and net profit attributable to the group €4.311 billion. The recurring operating margin was 42.1%. These figures describe different layers of performance; revenue is not profit, and neither is the stock-market value of the business. High profitability helped finance training and facilities, but the ability to finance them did not eliminate the time required to make them productive.

Business lesson

Scale the transmission

Invest in the mechanism that produces skilled people, then let capability govern output.

08
2000s–2024

More doors, one house

The final act asks how approachable Hermès can become without weakening its promise.

A globally recognizable handbag is only part of the business. Store managers select merchandise for their local clients through the house’s Podium buying process, rather than receiving an identical assortment everywhere. That discretion makes the store an interpreter of demand. It also means a traveler can discover a different expression of the house in another city. The commercial benefit is a reason to explore beyond the most famous products, provided the less familiar objects justify the attention they receive.

Petit h, founded in 2010 under Pascale Mussard, approached creation from another direction. Materials left unused by other workshops became the starting point for new objects. Instead of specifying a product and then sourcing its ingredients, makers asked what existing leather, silk, porcelain, or crystal could become. The exercise adds playfulness to a company whose severe quality standards can otherwise make it sound solemn.

New entry points widened the audience further. E-commerce, airport stores, perfume, and the 2015 Apple Watch partnership offered ways into the house that did not depend on being allocated a Birkin. Beauty arrived in 2020 with lipstick. These categories complicate the simple description of everything as handmade, scarce, and sold through one intimate channel. A customer buying a strap or a fragrance is having a different experience from commissioning or receiving a leather bag.

The hosts’ concluding debate centers on that tension. They ask whether more accessible products strengthen the relationship with future clients or compromise what the most demanding clients believe the name promises. Their broader conclusion is that customers value a combined experience: craft, identity, service, exclusivity, and the setting in which they buy. The following analysis builds on that argument but is this companion’s interpretation. The important question is whether each new door leads into the same house, and whether the house can still make its most demanding promise credible.

Business lesson

Audit every new entrance

Judge an accessible category by the relationship it creates and the expectations it changes.

Concluding analysis · this companion’s interpretation

The scarce resource is permission

Hermès earns money by selling objects whose price contains more than material and labor. It contains a recognizable origin, confidence in making, the experience of dealing with the house, and the social meaning other people attach to the result. Those elements interact. Fine construction without recognition belongs to a different market; recognition without convincing construction asks a different kind of customer to believe. The company’s achievement is to keep enough of the bundle intact while reaching far beyond its original circle.

Branding is the most visible competitive advantage, but process knowledge helps make it credible. Competitors can hire designers and buy good leather. Reproducing a large organization that recruits, teaches, evaluates, and retains skilled makers is harder. The difficulty is not a secret stitch that no one else can learn. It is maintaining consistent decisions across thousands of people while allowing those people to respond intelligently to varied materials. A rival needs a trusted name and a production system at the same time.

The ownership history adds another barrier. A strategy that declines some immediate sales opportunities can become fragile if owners measure success only by the next expansion. Stable control makes restraint believable, especially when the house has already demonstrated that it will defend independence. That is an interpretation of incentives, not proof that family ownership always produces good decisions. Families can disagree, select poor leaders, or become complacent. Here, the structure supports the particular production and creative choices the company has made.

Scarcity is economically useful only while people want what is scarce. Limited availability can reinforce desirability; it can also frustrate a customer into leaving. The same is true of allocation through stores. Personal judgment may help a finite supply reach long-term clients, but opacity can make the process feel arbitrary. A company that relies on discretion must attend to how that discretion is experienced. Otherwise the commercial mechanism starts to undermine the relationship it was intended to protect.

The resale market makes the tension visible. Some sought-after bags sell above retail, but that premium varies by model, condition, color, moment, and transaction costs. It is not a universal promise of appreciation. A premium tells the house that it could potentially capture more money from particular buyers; leaving part of it outside the company may support the perception that the original price remains defensible. The interpretation is that pricing has a reputational purpose as well as a revenue purpose. Raising every price to clear every shortage could change who the house becomes.

There are ordinary vulnerabilities beneath the unusual model. Wealth effects and regional demand still influence purchasing. Dependence on animal-derived materials creates sourcing and reputational exposure. Training takes time, so capacity can miss a change in demand in either direction. More accessible categories can create customers, but also create expectations that differ from those formed by the flagship bags. Global stores and digital channels add convenience while testing how much of an intimate service model survives at greater distance.

Repair offers a particularly concrete response to the question of value. A house that maintains objects over many years gives the buyer something beyond the moment of possession. That service requires people, parts, and memory: continued commitment to the objects already sold. My interpretation is that long-lived service makes the history more than decoration. The firm is still responsible for an old decision after the sale is complete.

The deepest limit on growth may therefore be permission rather than cash. How far can the company expand before clients, makers, and owners no longer recognize the same bargain? The answer cannot be set once for all generations. Émile had to leave carriage dependence behind; Jean-Louis had to take the house abroad; the sixth generation had to train on a much larger scale. Each preserved something by changing something else. The next successful decision will need the same specificity: identify what must remain credible, and build a new way to deliver it.
