Hermès

Six generations, one house

A harness maker survives the automobile, finds a global audience, and learns to grow by teaching people to make things slowly.

Released February 20, 2024Created October 12, 2026About 19 minutes to readAcquired · Hermès · Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal

01
1801–1880

A craft before a brand

An immigrant craftsman reaches Paris as the city becomes a stage for wealth.

The episode begins in Krefeld, Germany, where Thierry Hermès was born in 1801 to a French father and German mother. David describes a childhood overtaken by the Napoleonic wars and their aftermath. By twenty, Thierry had lost his family. In 1821 he moved to Normandy, abandoning the family’s innkeeping trade to apprentice in équipage: the craft of outfitting horse-drawn carriages. It would be sixteen years before he opened his own workshop.

In 1837, Thierry arrived in Paris and established a harness-making shop on Rue Basse-du-Rempart. Ben and David emphasize that this was not yet a saddle business. The customers were wealthy people riding in carriages; their equipment had to be finely made, but its visibility also mattered. Thierry became known for exceptional work. The hosts connect his success to the changing city around him rather than presenting craftsmanship alone as a complete explanation.

Under Napoleon III and Baron Haussmann, broad boulevards made a new public setting for carriages and their owners. David argues that social status was becoming less exclusively a matter of birth: an expanding class could display its position through possessions. Empress Eugénie became a customer of both Thierry Hermès and Louis Vuitton. The episode follows the parallel carefully. Vuitton’s trunks suited the coming age of modern travel; Hermès’ dependence on horses would eventually require a much harder change.

Thierry died in 1878. His son Charles-Émile, already a craftsman with years in the workshop, took over, added saddles, and moved the shop and workshop to 24 Rue du Faubourg Saint-Honoré in 1880. That apprenticeship becomes a recurring thread in the hosts’ story. They contrast a family learning to make objects with a family learning only to manage a company. For Hermès, they argue, the artistic and business traditions grew together.

From the hosts’ discussion

Craft and ownership grew together

David links the family’s authority over the business to successive generations learning the work with their hands.

02
1902–1920s

When the customer leaves the horse behind

Émile sees the automobile and chooses a different future from his brother.

Charles-Émile retired in 1902, leaving his sons Adolphe and Émile to run Hermès Frères. The hosts introduce Émile as the more adventurous brother, describing an earlier journey to Russia with miniature samples of saddles and harnesses that won business from the tsar. Around the succession, a practical accessory entered the range: the Haut à courroies, a large belted bag for carrying saddles and riding boots. Ben and David draw attention to its shape and closure as ancestors of the house’s later bags.

The decisive change came during the First World War. In David’s account, a military assignment took Émile to North America, where he met Henry Ford and saw automobile production in Detroit. Knowing that cars existed was different from seeing how quickly they could be made. Émile returned convinced that the horse-centered world was disappearing. He also encountered the zipper and secured a two-year exclusive license in France, applying the mechanism to products including a leather golf jacket. The hosts are uncertain about precisely where he first saw the fastening system, so the episode does not establish that detail firmly.

Adolphe wanted to remain in the horse trade. The hosts do not assume that he simply failed to understand the future; Ben allows that he may have preferred to remain a small specialist. In 1919, Émile bought him out. David relates the house’s story of Émile asking the artisans what they could make with their hands that would interest their customers now. It is presented as a remembered company legend, not a documented speech. Bags and leather accessories for automobiles supplied the answer.

The episode describes Émile’s wife asking for a smaller bag that could pass through car doors, followed by the development of handbags in the 1920s. Clothes, jewelry, and watches broadened the offer. At this stage, the company frequently collaborated with specialist craftspeople rather than building every capability itself. Stores followed customers to places such as Cannes, Deauville, and Biarritz. Ben contrasts this travel-destination strategy with the later policy of opening stores to serve a local clientele. The transition changed the objects and their distribution while keeping hand craftsmanship at the center.

From the hosts’ discussion

Adapt what the hands can make

The hosts return to Émile’s remembered question: how can the existing craft serve customers whose lives have changed?

03
1930s–1950s

Robert brings art into the house

The fourth generation adds warmth, humor, and a different kind of object.

Émile’s succession brought his sons-in-law into the business. The hosts focus on Robert Dumas, who added a creative character distinct from the original harness-making tradition. David describes craftsmanship, French heritage, and the transition to the automobile as already established. What Robert contributed was art and whimsy. Ben recalls the warmth and softness he felt in Hermès stores, contrasting that experience with the more forceful visual language of other luxury shops.

Robert redesigned the smaller handbag into the Sac à Dépêches in 1935. Later in the decade, a walk by boats in Normandy inspired the Chaîne d’ancre bracelet. The episode describes a notebook sketch becoming jewelry, an example of the owner’s artistic work entering the product range. The hosts explain métier as a craft or trade, the term Hermès uses for its business activities. They also qualify the idea of quiet luxury: the house’s goods were initially less visibly branded, but modern products sometimes use an H or equestrian motifs because clients want recognizable signs.

The silk scarf arrived in 1937. David describes the first design as growing from Robert’s woodblock artwork, while Ben supplies a revealing comparison: when Axel had his first internship in 1988, silk represented 55% of sales and leather only 9%. By the period discussed in the episode, those proportions had reversed, with leather at 43% and silk and textiles at 7%. The scarf was not merely an accessory to an always-dominant handbag business. For decades it was the major commercial expression of the house.

The hosts follow the work of printing scarf colors individually and describe the origin of orange packaging in wartime shortages. What had been an available substitute became recognizable packaging. They connect it to the horse-and-carriage identity and imaginative store windows, where Leïla Menchari’s displays created elaborate scenes. These were part of the dream customers encountered. In the hosts’ telling, Robert’s generation made the house more than technically excellent leatherwork: it created a visual world that people wanted to join.

From the hosts’ discussion

The dream is part of the product

Ben and David treat Robert’s art and playfulness as essential additions to craftsmanship, rather than decorative extras.

04
1956–1978

A photograph, then a difficult decade

Grace Kelly makes the bag famous, but recognition does not prevent stagnation.

In 1956, Grace Kelly was photographed carrying the Sac à Dépêches. The actress from Philadelphia had become Princess of Monaco, and the image joined the bag to the kind of French luxury dream the hosts have been describing. They discuss the familiar account of her using the bag to conceal her pregnancy. A product Robert had already designed gained a public identity that would endure as the Kelly. Leather goods remained expensive and difficult to make; the photograph did not instantly turn the company into a mass handbag operation.

The episode then moves into the problem concealed by today’s reputation for inevitability. Fashion changed in the 1970s. Brands such as Gucci, Yves Saint Laurent, and Chanel offered a more visibly contemporary approach, while Hermès’ careful stewardship of its established promise could make it seem old. Ben and David describe their conversation with former Gucci chief Domenico De Sole: protecting the mystique and refusing to cut corners could be a strength, but that same posture was difficult to reconcile with a fashion business organized around reinvention.

Toward the end of Robert’s tenure, consultants recommended closing the workshop above the Faubourg shop, outsourcing production, adding products, and lowering prices to reach more customers. The hosts do not identify the consulting firm with certainty. What matters to their argument is the proposed direction: a company falling behind was being advised to adopt the practices of competitors. It was an existential moment for the particular version of Hermès that the family had maintained.

Robert’s son Jean-Louis took over in 1978. Ben and David pause over the recurring pattern of a generational transfer happening just when the company needed to change. They do not attribute successful family succession to an exceptional bloodline. Instead, they discuss childhood exposure to the business, apprenticeship, understanding of the company’s place in the market, and the authority to rally people behind a change. They acknowledge the tension with nepotism: limiting the talent pool is a real drawback, even when growing up inside the business supplies knowledge that is hard to reproduce.

From the hosts’ discussion

A promise can become a constraint

The hosts describe the 1970s as a point when Hermès needed renewed relevance without following the proposed outsourcing strategy.

05
1978–2000s

Jean-Louis makes the old objects feel new

A scarf campaign and a slow-building handbag legend revitalize the house.

Jean-Louis had experienced American retail through work at Bloomingdale’s while his wife Rena trained in New York with architect I. M. Pei. The hosts describe him returning with an understanding of a broader audience, but rejecting the idea that Hermès needed to abandon its products. In 1979, a campaign showed young Parisian women wearing the house’s scarves with jeans. The object was familiar; its setting and the people wearing it were new. Ben and David emphasize how difficult it was to make the same products attractive to younger clients without insulting existing customers.

The episode interrupts the succession story to explain what handwork actually means. A saddle stitch uses one thread with a needle at each end, passed through the leather in opposite directions. Ben describes careful hole-making, the tight fit of thread and leather, and the stitch’s slight diagonal appearance. A Kelly is assembled by one artisan from 36 leather pieces, matched as closely as possible. The hosts give roughly twenty hours of work and disagree about how much training is needed before an artisan can work on the flagship bags. The disagreement remains a qualification, rather than becoming an exact training requirement here.

David’s conversation with former Hermès artisan Béatrice Amblard gives that process a human history. She trained in the Paris atelier and later repaired customers’ objects on the American West Coast. She remembered Jean-Louis knowing artisans personally and sending congratulations after she established her own business, April in Paris. The hosts use her account to discuss both the meaning of an object made by a person and the repair promise. They distinguish an independent maker’s excellent leatherwork from the wider experience offered by Hermès; they are not interchangeable purchases simply because both can use the same stitch.

The Birkin arrived in 1984 after Jean-Louis met Jane Birkin on a flight between Paris and London. The hosts recount her dissatisfaction with a bag that did not suit her everyday needs and his proposal for a better one. Crucially, they say it sold poorly for its first five years. The familiar image of an instantly coveted handbag obscures the time it took for its reputation to develop. Acquired’s later correction also matters: the Birkin is carried by hand or in the crook of the arm, not over the shoulder as the recording had suggested.

As its reputation grew, scarcity became inseparable from buying it. Ben and David explain the role of a relationship with a sales associate and the gap between a retail allocation and the secondary-market price. They discuss Veblen goods, whose price can contribute to desirability, but resist reducing Hermès to constant aggressive increases. They ask why the company leaves potential revenue uncollected. One argument they consider is that scarce bags lift the house’s status and encourage participation in its other products. They present that as a business interpretation under discussion, not a guarantee that any particular bag will appreciate.

Jean-Louis also expanded internationally and reorganized the company as a listed, family-controlled business in 1993. Production moved to Pantin in 1992. The hosts describe the public listing as supplying liquidity for family owners and funding growth, while creating a vulnerability that only became clear later. The same generation that made Hermès newly relevant opened its ownership to a market in which someone else could accumulate shares.

From the hosts’ discussion

Revitalize the setting, preserve the object

Ben and David single out the younger scarf campaign as renewal through presentation, while the product and craft remained intact.

06
2001–2014

The shares that could change the house

The family confronts a buyer who can make selling increasingly tempting.

After failing to acquire Gucci, Bernard Arnault began accumulating Hermès shares. David describes an initial 4.9% stake in 2001 followed by equity-swap arrangements that obscured the scale of LVMH’s position. He stresses that Arnault was both an investor and an experienced luxury operator. He saw more than an admired brand: a forthcoming generational transition and operating problems created an opening for someone who believed the business could become much more valuable.

The succession was not settled immediately. Pierre-Alexis Dumas became the artistic heir, but Axel Dumas had spent years away from Hermès in banking, including work in China and New York. The episode follows his return in 2003 through finance, jewelry, and leather goods rather than jumping directly to chief executive. Patrick Thomas, a non-family executive, bridged the generations. The hosts discuss the separation of artistic and business leadership after Jean-Louis had held both roles, and the need to improve management without losing the family’s creative authority.

LVMH’s stake became public in 2010 and reached 22.6% by December 2011 in the hosts’ account. The family still held roughly 73%, so a minority stake alone could not confer control. David explains the more subtle pressure: reducing the public float pushed up the share price, making a sale exceptionally attractive to individual relatives. With ownership spread across a large family, several departures could change everyone else’s incentives. Ben describes the fear of being the last person left after other holders had accepted an unusually lucrative exit.

In 2011, more than fifty family members contributed 50.2% of the company to H51 and agreed to lock up that holding. The hosts regard this coordination as the decisive defense. They discuss additional rights of first refusal over shares held outside the vehicle. Their recording speculates about documents they believed were unavailable; Acquired’s subsequent correction says the bylaws are public, limits shareholders to descendants of Émile Hermès and Julie Hollande, and identifies January 2041 as the date from which holders can ask the company to buy their B shares.

The episode ends the takeover story with the 2014 resolution and the distribution of LVMH’s Hermès holding to its own shareholders. The hosts emphasize that Arnault still benefited financially: shares reached his family holding company and helped finance later consolidation involving Dior. They do not portray his failure to gain Hermès as a simple financial defeat. The family, meanwhile, kept the independent company. For Ben and David, the extraordinary point is that owners facing a powerful offer coordinated to preserve their control.

07
2013–2024

The factory is a school

Axel and Pierre-Alexis expand the method rather than abandon it.

The sixth generation’s operating achievement, in the hosts’ view, was finding a way to increase handcraft production. They open this act with Axel’s discussion of strategy and trade-offs: doing one thing better means accepting that someone else will do another thing better. They also cite Patrick Thomas’ luxury paradox, where increasing sales can erode the desirability that produced those sales. Their question is how Hermès could serve global demand without surrendering what customers believed they were buying.

The answer was a network of relatively small workshops. David says each site is kept to roughly 250–300 people so that employees can know one another; beyond that, Axel regards it as a factory rather than a workshop. The hosts compare the approach to expanding through multiple smaller computing sites. Their analogy concerns the distribution of capacity, not mechanizing the work. A bag would still be made from beginning to end by an artisan.

The stated aim was to increase capacity by 7% annually. At the size discussed in the episode, that meant adding approximately 500 artisans each year, a dramatic change from a time when the company had only about 250 craftspeople in total. Competitors could not supply the necessary workforce because most had chosen different production methods. Hermès therefore built its own schools and training centers, including in French areas with high unemployment. The hosts describe a commitment to seeing learners graduate and offering them work, rather than assuming experienced artisans could simply be recruited.

Ben and David point to the 2021 opening of the École Hermès des savoir-faire as a government-recognized training program. They also describe a younger, largely female artisan workforce, unlike the older workforce associated with the earlier ateliers. Their central argument is that the company has not merely preserved a dwindling stock of skills. It has enlarged the community able to perform them. That is why they return repeatedly to transmission of know-how when explaining growth.

The financial discussion remains in the recording’s frame. The hosts describe 2022 revenue of €11.6 billion and anticipate roughly €14 billion for 2023; that last figure is an estimate in their conversation, not the final audited annual result. They also discuss the limits of reinvesting cash when training time and the desired image of the brand govern expansion. The remarkable growth they describe is presented alongside the constraints they believe made it possible.

Teach the scarce skill

The hosts identify the self-built artisan pipeline as the mechanism that lets Hermès scale its traditional production.

08
2000s–2024

More doors, one house

New products make the house approachable and give the hosts a question they cannot settle.

The Apple Watch partnership is the episode’s most pointed test of the modern strategy. David enjoys his Hermès watch strap, yet questions why a machine-stitched product should sit under a name he associates with the highest standard of handwork. Ben shares the concern. They consider whether a more accessible purchase starts a lifelong customer relationship, or weakens the expectations established by the flagship leather goods. Neither turns that discussion into a settled verdict.

The hosts connect the same tension to perfumes, beauty, airport stores, and e-commerce. These are entry points for people who might otherwise find an Hermès boutique intimidating or inaccessible. They discuss the principle that the house must serve contemporary clients rather than become a museum. They also note that an online purchase offers a different experience from visiting a store. Growth requires changing how customers encounter Hermès; the open question is how much change the meaning of the brand can accommodate.

Petit h supplies another expression of the house. The hosts describe Pascale Mussard’s 2010 initiative to use materials left over from other work. Its creative order is reversed: begin with available pieces of leather, silk, or other material, then decide what they could become. Small playful objects make the otherwise serious standards feel imaginative. Ben and David treat that fun as a substantial part of the culture, not just an amusing side business.

They also discuss Hermès Horizons, which applies the house’s expertise to bespoke commissions such as seats for a private jet. The point is not simply putting a large H on an expensive possession. The hosts recount the division explaining to customers that a conspicuous logo is not the service it is offering. This carries the old relationship between usefulness, craftsmanship, and status into unfamiliar objects.

The episode’s final operating discussion moves from products to the system around them: local store selection, long employee relationships, controlled production and distribution, and communications built heavily around events. The hosts argue that all of those contribute to what a customer buys. Their concluding analysis brings them together rather than treating a Birkin as an isolated high-margin leather object.

From the hosts’ discussion

Keep the accessibility question open

Ben and David debate whether Apple Watch straps and other entry products broaden the relationship or compromise the promise; they do not resolve it.

Ben & David’s concluding analysis

The bundle no rival supplies

Ben and David begin their concluding analysis with branding. Knowing that an object comes from Hermès changes the amount a buyer is willing to pay, even when the object does not display a large logo. They distinguish this from the group-level scale economies discussed in their LVMH episode. Hermès is not using the same portfolio model, and they repeatedly return to the accumulated history of the house as the principal source of its power.

They make two further cases. The first is a cornered resource: the company employs a large share of the craftspeople able to make its products in the traditional way, and a rival would need to train a workforce as well as create a trusted brand. The second is counter-positioning. A luxury competitor already serving a much larger customer base would struggle to change its production model to individual handcraft. The hosts qualify this second argument and debate how much of it is really part of branding. Their conclusion is not that every possible power applies; branding remains dominant.

The playbook then revisits the argument that handbags are particularly attractive products. In the LVMH discussion, ease of buying, manufacturing economics, brand recognition, and margins had worked together. Hermès complicates that story: customers cannot simply buy its best-known bags when they want, production is deliberately laborious, and visible logos are relatively restrained. Ben realizes that selling the same category does not mean running the same business. The high margins do not erase the fundamentally different system behind them.

The hosts also qualify the claim that Hermès has no marketing department. It still pays for communications, events, public relations, and some advertising. They cite communications spending of roughly 4.5% of revenue and discuss events as the larger component. These are figures from their episode comparison, not an updated company budget. Their argument is that concentrated experiences and word of mouth do much of the work. They connect the avoidance of paid celebrity endorsements to control over the house’s image: famous customers choose the goods rather than being appointed to represent the company.

Local store buying is another central playbook point. At Podium, store managers select from the house’s creations for their own customers, within allocation and assortment constraints. Ben and David call this a pull model: headquarters does not simply push an identical offer through every location. They compare the resulting discovery to a treasure hunt. They place this alongside the move toward more directly controlled stores and production, and the low employee turnover they discuss as evidence that the transmission of craft has organizational substance.

For the hosts, the product is ultimately a bundle: exclusivity, service, craftsmanship, shopping experience, and a powerful brand. Other makers can supply individual elements, but assembling them separately does not recreate the same purchase. An independent artisan can make excellent leather goods; a large brand can supply recognition. The hosts argue that Hermès combines those qualities with a repair relationship and a sense of continuity. This is their explanation for why comparing bags only by leather, stitches, or price misses the customer’s decision.

The value-creation discussion is more ambivalent than an account of a flawless luxury business. Ben and David recognize the argument that luxury is largely status signaling and captures more value than it contributes. They respond that Hermès guarantees a particular standard of craft and preserves an otherwise shrinking practice. They also see the resale premium as evidence that some economic surplus remains with clients. Neither point disposes of the ethical concerns they then raise.

They discuss exotic leathers, animal farming, and the destruction of imperfect products. Some details are explicitly uncertain, including their account of how Jane Birkin’s objection to crocodile sourcing was resolved. The hosts distinguish the argument for using hides from animals already raised for food from farming animals for their skins, and are dissatisfied with parts of the sustainability defense. They acknowledge why the house wants to protect quality, while criticizing the waste of usable objects. These remain concerns in the episode, not issues this companion independently adjudicates.

Their final personal takeaways return to culture and strategy. David is struck by a company that values enjoyment and remaining true to its identity more than winning a numerical contest. Ben is struck by how different the Hermès and Louis Vuitton businesses are despite selling ostensibly similar goods. The episode ends without grading the company or settling every tension. Its admiration coexists with questions about accessible products, limits to growth, value creation, and the costs of protecting the brand.
