Chapter 01 · 1950–1960s · Listen 00:05:52

An airfield, a championship, a dangerous trade

The hosts locate the beginnings in Britain’s engineers, Monaco’s glamour and Ferrari’s racing identity.

Formula 1 begins in the episode with a collection of European races, not a company waiting to be incorporated. Automobile clubs organised their own events, sold tickets and advertising, and attracted drivers and manufacturers with prize money. A common formula eventually acquired an international rule-making body, the FIA. But common rules did not yet mean a common championship. After the disruption of World War II, the FIA announced a seven-race world drivers’ championship, beginning at Silverstone in May 1950.

David identifies three foundations of this early sport. Britain supplied abandoned airfields, former military pilots and mechanics, and a need for entertainment and redevelopment. Colin Chapman embodied that combination. He founded Lotus with £25 in 1952 and supported his racing ambitions through a road-car business. When he finally entered Formula 1 in 1958, his emphasis was on weight, handling and cornering, rather than horsepower alone. He also helped turn the car itself into sponsor inventory, replacing national racing colours with the colours of Gold Leaf tobacco.

Monaco supplied a different ingredient. Prince Rainier’s marriage to Grace Kelly in 1956 brought Hollywood into an already prestigious European setting. The Grand Prix’s proximity to Cannes helped make it a gathering place for celebrities, while drivers increasingly became celebrities themselves. Enzo Ferrari connected this glamour with racing through the road cars he made for royalty and movie stars. The hosts argue that Ferrari did not simply gain legitimacy from joining the championship: Ferrari’s participation also legitimised Formula 1.

These strands developed together amid extraordinary danger. Ben and David describe fatalities as a recurring feature of the first decades, not an occasional disruption. Their opening establishes the combination that mattered to the business: inventive engineers, racing prestige, luxury and celebrity, and teams that were generally much better at spending money to win than at earning it. Ferrari had built a business around that combination. The championship as a whole had not.

Chapter 02 · 1930–1970 · Listen 00:30:43

The trader enters the paddock

The luxury-car dealer became an agent, then a team owner.

Bernie Ecclestone entered this world through dealing rather than engineering. Born in 1930 and raised in Suffolk, he traded surplus cars and motorcycles after the war, then developed a luxury-car showroom outside London. Calling him a used-car dealer misses part of the hosts’ description: he was the person who could find a desirable car for a wealthy customer. Financing and servicing those purchases helped him accumulate his first fortune.

Racing provided heritage for the cars he sold, and relationships in the paddock gave him another business. In the 1960s he became an agent for drivers, negotiating their pay. One of those drivers was Jochen Rindt at Lotus. They planned to leave Chapman’s team and acquire one of their own, making Rindt an owner as well as a driver. During the 1970 season, Rindt died in a practice crash while far enough ahead in the standings to become champion posthumously.

The episode presents Ecclestone’s next decision as carrying through the plan he had shared with his friend. In 1972 he bought Brabham for £100,000. The purchase placed him inside the Constructors’ Association, a loose gathering of team owners originally organised to coordinate transport to races. He soon saw that most of its members cared overwhelmingly about making faster cars, had little money, and had no sustainable commercial arrangement to support what they were doing.

Chapter 03 · 1970s–1981 · Listen 00:37:42

One negotiating table

Ecclestone made attendance dependable and bargaining centralised.

Each team negotiated separately with each Grand Prix. Payments varied, and spectators could not reliably know which teams would appear. The hosts describe this as bad both for bargaining power and for the sporting product. Ecclestone offered to take over negotiations, guarantee owners at least their existing income, and require them to attend the races. The owners could concentrate on competing while he dealt with promoters.

The improvement was immediate in the episode’s account. Average payments per team per race rose from about $10,000 before his intervention to $40,000 in his first year, then approximately $150,000 in the middle of the decade and $200,000 by its end. He also negotiated freight rates. The arrangement enriched him: the hosts say his fee eventually proved to be 8%, rather than the smaller percentage he had reportedly mentioned verbally. They nevertheless emphasise that he had made the teams’ situation dramatically better.

Promoters turned to the FIA for help against the new bargaining power confronting them. The resulting negotiations produced the first Concorde Agreement in 1981. The FIA retained authority over sporting rules. Teams committed to participating in every official Grand Prix. Fees and prize money flowed centrally through the Constructors’ Association, and the association controlled television rights for the next five seasons.

Television rights did not then look particularly valuable to the other parties. European public broadcasting offered little competitive bidding, and filming cars around a complex circuit looked difficult. Ecclestone had secured a commercial opportunity that still needed to be developed. Ben and David compare his approach with Pete Rozelle’s in the NFL: both centralised negotiations, but Rozelle acted for a league owned by its teams, while Ecclestone built businesses through which everyone else would deal with him.

Chapter 04 · 1980s–1990s · Listen 00:50:33

Sell the whole season

A cheap distribution deal developed the market before the rights became expensive.

Ecclestone’s first television move prioritised reach. He sold the European Broadcasting Union a very inexpensive package covering its public broadcasters, on the condition that they show every race rather than only a local Grand Prix. When the broadcasters lacked the ability or interest to produce the coverage, he financed a central feed through a new company, Formula One Promotions and Administration, or FOPA. Viewers could now follow the same championship across the season.

The distinction between FOPA and the Constructors’ Association became important when the next Concorde negotiation arrived. The hosts explain a television split of 30% to the FIA, 47% to the teams, and 23% to Ecclestone’s company. At the following negotiation in 1992, he persuaded the FIA to exchange its percentage for a guaranteed annual payment, starting at $5 million and rising to $9 million. He now held 53% of the television economics before his other fees.

As European pay television developed, he could auction rights to competing broadcasters. The teams did not all welcome his share, but their own sponsorships had become much more valuable. Television cameras made logos on the cars visible to an audience far beyond the grandstands. Tobacco companies, whose advertising opportunities elsewhere were narrowing, supplied substantial funding. The hosts’ next question is what the teams did with that money. Their answer leads straight back to the cars: they spent it trying to defeat one another.

Chapter 05 · 1960s–1994 · Listen 01:08:08

The laboratory and its limits

Faster cars, tighter safety rules and increasingly expensive searches for an edge.

Ben traces the technical race through three areas: aerodynamics, engines and electronics. Chapman’s early wings helped cars corner by pressing them toward the road, but also created drag. Lotus’s later ground-effect designs used airflow underneath the car to produce downforce. The Lotus 78 and 79 became decisive steps in this development; Mario Andretti and Lotus won their respective championships in 1978.

These gains also created hazards. If a skirt or the car’s relationship to the road changed, the grip supporting a high-speed corner could disappear. The rules eventually required flat-bottomed cars. Engines became more powerful and efficient, and some improvements reached road cars, but the hosts resist treating consumer innovation as the original purpose of every racing advance. Winning came first; road relevance also became a justification for manufacturers’ investment.

Williams brought a further transformation with electronic systems in the early 1990s, including active suspension and traction control. Its cars won in 1992 and 1993. Ayrton Senna moved to the team just as the FIA banned many of those aids, leaving him with a very different competitive situation. His fatal crash in 1994, during the same weekend that Roland Ratzenberger died, brought renewed public attention to safety after fatalities had become much less frequent.

The hosts describe subsequent changes to car speed, tyres, crash structures, cockpits and circuits, followed later by the halo. They also identify a financial irony: narrowing the rulebook did not remove the urge to win. It increased the incentive to search for smaller openings and spend heavily to exploit them. Their comparison is with semiconductor development, where progress becomes more demanding as the easy gains disappear. Meanwhile, Ecclestone’s commercial operation remained intensely personal, without the marketing, research and sales departments a modern media company would normally have.

Chapter 06 · 1990s–2009 · Listen 01:33:18

Selling equity, retaining the wheel

Liquidity changed the shareholders without immediately changing who ran the sport.

Ecclestone’s search for liquidity began with estate planning. The episode describes a proposed public offering, with his companies consolidated into SLEC Holdings, named for his wife Slavica. Bankers saw a business with roughly £250 million in annual revenue and EBITDA margins above 50%, but also found that much of his control lacked the formal documentation an offering would require. He obtained additional legal authority through a Concorde agreement; scrutiny from European antitrust investigators then helped derail the IPO.

Equity sales brought in a succession of owners without making him surrender day-to-day control. Hellman & Friedman assembled a large stake, then sold to the German media company EM.TV after roughly a month. Debt-financed expansion and the bursting technology bubble put EM.TV into trouble. Kirch took over, failed in turn, and left banks holding most of the equity. When those banks tried to assert control, Ecclestone negotiated with CVC, which bought them out, kept him as CEO, and allowed him to reinvest alongside it.

CVC and a newly incentivised Ecclestone pushed promoter income higher. Expensive new races could displace events that were neither especially lucrative nor indispensable to the sport’s heritage. The hosts argue that this produced a calendar built around fees rather than coherent viewing times. At the same time, leading teams were spending hundreds of millions each year. The 2008 financial crisis made some manufacturers’ support untenable.

A proposed cost cap provoked Ferrari and McLaren rather than resolving the crisis. In 2009 eight teams threatened to form a breakaway series through FOTA. They won concessions without leaving. Ben reports Zak Brown’s explanation of why such efforts repeatedly failed: the teams could agree that they wanted control, but not on how to divide the proceeds. Their different reasons for racing also made the coalition vulnerable to separate bargains. The hosts leave this period with relations and public trust badly damaged, then turn to two teams that helped create a different future.

Chapter 07 · 2004–2016 · Listen 02:05:07

An energy drink, a rescued team, a new dynasty

Red Bull and Mercedes brought different commercial ambitions into the paddock.

Red Bull’s Dietrich Mateschitz approached racing as an expression of the lifestyle associated with his drink. Sponsorship grew into ownership when he acquired Jaguar’s struggling team. In 2005 the renamed team brought an open-door hospitality venue, the Energy Station, into a paddock accustomed to tightly controlled access. The hosts describe DJs, parties and a conspicuously younger culture. What the establishment disliked, many drivers and employees enjoyed.

Ownership was not enough to produce a winning car. Christian Horner and Mateschitz recruited Adrian Newey from McLaren, bringing exceptional aerodynamic skill into the organisation. Red Bull began a four-year run of drivers’ and constructors’ championships in 2010. Its business purpose remained distinctive: the hosts describe a team prepared to leave little operating profit in racing so that it could create a larger marketing spectacle for the drink business.

Ross Brawn’s path began with Honda’s withdrawal during the financial crisis. He persuaded Honda to preserve the organisation while he sought a buyer, then bought it through a management transaction for £1 with temporary funding support. Honda would not supply an engine. Mercedes would, although adapting it to the existing chassis required an awkward compromise. The team lacked a secure sponsor base, and few expected much from the resulting car.

An aerodynamic opening changed everything. The double diffuser developed during the Honda period gave Brawn GP an early advantage. Jenson Button won six of the first seven races, and the team accumulated enough of a lead to win both championships as rivals caught up. Yet it could not fund the following season on its own. Mercedes acquired the team, initially without reproducing the extraordinary result. Further investment and the arrival of Toto Wolff and Lewis Hamilton eventually built a dominant organisation. Ben and David stress the change in leadership: these principals were running businesses, not merely pursuing race wins.

Chapter 08 · 2017–2021 · Listen 02:42:33

A media company sees unfinished work

The hosts describe a programme to repair relationships with teams, promoters and fans.

The asset Liberty acquired was a set of commercial rights, not ownership of every part of motorsport. The hosts explain that Ecclestone had secured a 100-year commercial-rights agreement from the FIA in 2001 for $360 million. Liberty’s transaction valued the acquired business at $8 billion including debt, with $4.4 billion of equity value. Chase Carey, with experience building Fox’s sports business, and Sean Bratches, with experience at ESPN, brought a different management background.

The initial plan left Ecclestone in place as CEO. That arrangement could not accommodate the changes Liberty intended, and he departed from the executive role in January 2017. The hosts frame the transition as the end of a 45-year run that had created the commercial sport but also held back its next phase. Digital access, storytelling, American market development and younger audiences had been systematically neglected.

Liberty’s first priority was the teams. It negotiated a cost cap covering spending on the car while leaving important categories, including driver pay, senior executives and marketing, outside it. Ben and David discuss the initial $145 million level and subsequent changes rather than treating it as a cap on the entire business. Combined with growing sponsorship revenue, the constraint made many teams viable and some highly profitable. Their example is Mercedes, which they describe as generating roughly $200 million in operating income while remaining competitive.

Next came promoters and fans. Liberty assembled promoters to share attendance information and marketing practices, and aimed to make each race weekend a major entertainment event. With fans, the contrast was illustrated by Hamilton’s Instagram account: Ecclestone’s operation had sent takedown demands, while Liberty encouraged the exposure. Video games, esports, preseason testing and Hollywood projects could all create engagement that had previously been left unused. The episode’s account is of repairing relationships throughout the system, not simply signing a larger television contract.

Chapter 09 · 2019–2025 · Listen 03:05:03

Let viewers meet the people

The decisive subject was human drama rather than the cars alone.

The original pitch for Drive to Survive was visually exciting racing. The hosts argue that its decisive subject turned out to be people: ambitions, insecurity, rivalry and office politics, set among fast cars and glamorous places. Even committed racing fans had been starved of access behind the scenes. The documentary could give existing followers something new while making the sport understandable to people without a technical background.

Liberty considered Amazon as well as Netflix. According to the hosts, Amazon offered more money, but Netflix declined to match the offer and argued that its larger global audience would better serve the sport’s growth. Liberty accepted the lower payment. Netflix selected Box to Box Films, and the production received creative control and final cut. It would tell an opinionated story with access, rather than a message approved in every detail by the rights holder.

Mercedes and Ferrari initially stayed out, leaving the production to work with the other teams. Daniel Ricciardo became an important point of entry. Ben describes an audience that expanded beyond its initially narrow target as the programme found viewers, including many women. Eventually sponsors at the absent teams wanted the exposure too. The series was available when pandemic restrictions kept people at home, and Formula 1 managed to resume racing with bubbles and repeated events at the same circuit.

The hosts draw a distinction that matters throughout their conclusion: fans need not watch live races to be commercially valuable. Someone following the documentary can still see a sponsor, buy merchandise and care about the championship. Ben’s story about accidentally revealing a real-world personnel change while watching with his wife illustrates the separation. To her, the programme’s unfolding story mattered more than news that had happened months earlier. Ben and David regard this form of fandom as one of Formula 1’s most unusual achievements.

Chapter 10 · 2018–2026 · Listen 03:26:45

Attention becomes a contract

New races, corporate hospitality and media partners created several routes into the sport.

The American expansion combined additional races, hospitality and broader media access. Miami followed Austin, and Las Vegas introduced a different business model: Formula 1 operated the event itself instead of collecting a promoter fee. Ben says the large investment had not yet become an obvious success and doubts that the model would be replicated widely. The episode therefore presents Vegas as a substantial bet, not an uncomplicated example of vertical integration paying off.

Their own paddock experience also reveals the business-to-business side of the sport. Sponsors can spend a weekend with clients in several countries rather than a few hours at a home stadium. Ben and David discuss the paddock as a travelling executive briefing centre and describe sponsorship deals that bundle branding with relationships, access and hospitality. A viewer’s interest can become value for a team even when that value arrives through a sponsor’s customer relationships rather than ticket sales.

Liberty initially gave ESPN the American rights for no fee in 2018 to build reach. The hosts contrast that with the reported $80–90 million annual value of a later deal. After the success of F1 The Movie, they discuss Apple’s five-year agreement and a reported annual price around $150 million, explicitly treating the price as a rumour. They likewise discuss the film’s roughly $630 million as global box office, not as income to Formula 1 itself.

The opportunity remains unfinished in their account. American race viewing is small beside the global audience, and greater competition among broadcasters could improve rights values elsewhere too. The hosts also examine the incoming Cadillac, Audi and Ford involvement, new regulations, and a possible American-time-zone spring run of races. They favour a calendar that avoids competing with the NFL and reduces unnecessary travel. Those are proposals and expectations raised in the episode, not outcomes established after its release.

Chapter 11 · As of March 2, 2026 · Listen 03:41:52

The championship is the asset

The episode separates the rights holder, the teams and the promoters.

For the central business, the episode uses 2024 figures: approximately $3.4 billion in revenue, with media rights contributing 33%, race promotion 29%, advertising and sponsorship 19%, and hospitality, merchandise and licensing making up the remaining 19%. These are the hosts’ figures for Formula One Group. They keep team sponsorship separate, because the teams own valuable inventory on the cars and drivers and sell their own access and hospitality.

Team payments are the central business’s largest cost. The hosts describe about $1.27 billion, or 37% of revenue, distributed through a formula combining equal participation, constructors’ results and historical involvement. They emphasise that the drivers’ championship does not directly determine these payouts. Ferrari retains a heritage-related advantage, though the arrangements are negotiated and not publicly available in full. Estimated front-to-back differences in payments also concern Ben, who argues that they reinforce the disadvantage of the slower teams.

The distinction between fans and viewers returns here. The episode’s roughly 830 million global fans are not 830 million people watching every race. Team values have also risen faster than their profits would alone explain. Ben and David discuss an average estimated valuation of $3.6 billion and describe ownership as a scarce asset that can have benefits beyond operating income. They do not present that valuation as annual revenue or a guaranteed investment return.

Unlike the NFL’s pass-through league structure, Formula One Group retains earnings and has its own enterprise value. The hosts ask whether teams would benefit enough from owning it to justify breaking away. Their illustrative calculation adds the central company’s roughly $492 million of operating income to existing distributions: teams already receive about 72% of that hypothetical total. They consider this a more workable balance than the Ecclestone-era relationship because Liberty is also investing in the sport. Separately, Acquired’s published correction clarifies that global transport is outsourced to Rock-It, rather than all logistics being supplied directly by Formula 1.

Chapter 12 · Concluding analysis · Listen 03:56:23

Why the system worked

Ben and David distinguish operational excellence on the grid from power at the league level.

Did Formula 1 require Bernie Ecclestone? David argues that it probably did need a forceful individual at the right moment. He contrasts the outcome with Pete Rozelle’s achievement in persuading NFL owners to share a league. That accomplishment was unusually difficult to reproduce, even among other American sports. Formula 1 added the complications of teams, circuits, broadcasters and an international regulator. In David’s view, coordinating those parties required an entrepreneur with Ecclestone’s incentives and negotiating temperament, rather than an employee who could simply be hired to build the system. Ben largely agrees.

Their next distinction is between a successful team and a defensible league. A technical loophole can create an advantage for a few races or a season before rivals copy it or regulators close it. Hiring a great driver does not itself constitute a durable strategic power, because another team can hire that person. Ben therefore argues that sustained team success often owes more to skill and operational excellence than to a structural advantage. David considers whether financial resources supply scale advantages. They ultimately narrow the operational-excellence argument to the front half of the grid, acknowledging negative feedback loops among the slower teams. Within that qualification, they agree that the sporting system ought to allow the strongest performers to win rather than make leadership permanently inaccessible.

For Formula One Group, they reach a much stronger conclusion about power. Custom engineering helps make the product distinctive. Its European prestige and Hollywood associations are difficult for another racing series to acquire, and the FIA’s designation of Formula 1 as the pinnacle of motorsport is a cornered resource in their analysis. They identify network economies linking teams and Grands Prix, branding that makes viewers prefer Formula 1 to a proposed replacement, switching costs for teams and circuits, and the scale needed to amortise the cost of a global series. The failed breakaway efforts are evidence of how protected the incumbent has been, even when participants resented its management.

The bull case begins with a striking comparison. Using their estimates of total sport revenue and fan counts, the hosts put annual revenue per Formula 1 fan around $7, against roughly $127 for the NFL. They immediately acknowledge an important limit: the NFL has far more events, and Formula 1 cannot simply multiply its calendar to a hundred races. Its opportunity is to make the existing weekends larger events, develop the American media market, improve scheduling and realise more value from European rights as new bidders enter. They also discuss an American champion or a champion-level woman driver as possible catalysts, without assuming either will appear.

The bear case is partly about the product on the track. Races can look like processions, while much of the important competition happens in engineering, qualifying, resource management and pit strategy. Bigger cars and limited overtaking can make the spectacle harder for a new viewer to understand. They no longer regard American interest as merely a pandemic fad, but acknowledge that Liberty has already used many of the easiest growth levers. Further progress may take longer.

Ben sees a gap between the people audiences meet in Drive to Survive and the live broadcast, where faces and personalities almost disappear inside cars. Better storytelling and visualisation could make strategy and an impending overtake clearer. David treats Apple’s technology and resources as a possible part of that solution. Their discussion is speculative about future presentation, not a claim that those improvements have already occurred.

They finish on two thoughts rather than a general management prescription. Ben is astonished by the durability of fandom among people who may never watch a race but know the drivers, buy merchandise and respond to sponsors. David is struck by the complexity of the business and the difficulty of assembling anything comparable. Formula 1 arose through decades of negotiation and improvisation; a challenger is more likely, in his view, to grow from something different than to reproduce the entire existing arrangement at once.
