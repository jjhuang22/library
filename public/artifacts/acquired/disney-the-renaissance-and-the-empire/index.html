A creative revival rebuilt the stories. Cable television financed an empire. Streaming made Disney question how those pieces belonged together.

Released August 10, 2026 · Created October 18, 2026
About 25 minutes to read · 1984 through the hosts’ 2026 analysis
Based on Acquired: Disney: The Renaissance and the Empire, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal. This covers Part II; the Walt-era companion covers Part I.

01

1984 · 00:05:07

A company worth taking apart

The stories had stopped renewing the business.

By 1984, Walt Disney’s company had lost the creative confidence that once held its businesses together. Epcot had opened as an expensive, debt-laden project far removed from Walt’s proposed city. Animation was struggling, and the company’s leadership was divided. Ron Miller, Walt’s son-in-law, was chief executive; Roy E. Disney, the son of Walt’s brother, had left the company and was preparing to challenge him. A falling share price made Disney vulnerable to investors who saw more value in its pieces than in its future.

The breakup proposals were stark: sell the film library to MGM and the parks to hotel operators. Management’s defense brought in the Bass brothers of Fort Worth and their investment manager, Richard Rainwater. A series of transactions left the Bass family with roughly a quarter of Disney’s shares. The company survived the immediate threat by giving these new shareholders substantial influence over its future.

Ben and David describe a business whose durable assets were carrying a failing creative center. In their account, parks and consumer products generated about $250 million of profit in 1984, while film and television contributed only about $2 million. Old stories still had value, but the company was not creating enough new ones. Nearby, another institution Walt had helped establish, CalArts, was training the animators who would eventually change that. Some went straight to Disney, only to leave or be dismissed before finding their way back into its wider story.

02

1984–1988 · 00:11:33

Three outsiders and a protected promise

A management reset began with a different idea of how to make a movie.

Roy E. Disney and Stanley Gold joined forces with the Bass shareholders to remove Miller in September 1984. Within two weeks, the board recruited Michael Eisner and Frank Wells. Wells had been president of Warner Bros. and had a trusted relationship with Gold. Eisner came from Paramount, where he and Barry Diller had overseen a run of popular films. When Diller left Paramount, Eisner did not receive the promotion he expected. His availability coincided with Disney’s crisis.

Their arrangement revealed the partnership’s character. Eisner insisted that a creative executive should lead the company. Wells accepted the presidency beneath him, with his own reporting relationship to the board. Eisner then brought Jeffrey Katzenberg from Paramount to run the studios. Eisner supplied creative judgment and public leadership; Wells handled operations and helped keep forceful personalities working together. That division would become important when it disappeared.

The Paramount approach favored clear story concepts, controlled budgets, and promising talent over expensive star packages. At Disney, live-action films such as Pretty Woman and Dead Poets Society made that approach productive. The hosts report that 27 of the first 33 films were profitable. They also explain that outside financing from Silver Screen supported the slate; higher park prices alone did not finance every production.

The parks supplied another immediate opening. Prices had barely moved while inflation had changed the economy around them. Raising admission and parking prices increased cash flow without requiring Disney to rebuild the attractions first. But animation initially looked less secure: the incoming team moved its staff off the Burbank lot to Glendale. Roy had extracted a promise that the division would survive and made himself its chairman. Protecting that option preserved the part of Disney that the turnaround would ultimately need most.

03

1989–1994 · 00:24:30

A song could carry the story

Creative renewal came from people, process, and technology together.

Katzenberg and animation executive Peter Schneider inherited a dispirited organization. Schneider questioned established production habits, from paint to animation pegs, and encouraged the use of computers. The point was broader than efficiency: old procedures were open to challenge, and the studio could improve. The Great Mouse Detective and Oliver & Company began restoring confidence before a more decisive change arrived.

Howard Ashman and Alan Menken brought musical theater into animation as a way to structure a story. A song could establish a character’s desire and make an audience care about whether it was fulfilled. The hosts give Ashman and Menken central credit for the Renaissance. Executives had created room for a revival, but the emotional force came from the people making the films. The Little Mermaid began that run in 1989. Its theatrical release was encouraging; repeated viewing on home video helped it become a much larger cultural presence.

Beauty and the Beast, Aladdin, and The Lion King followed. The episode gives original-release worldwide box-office figures of roughly $330 million, $500 million, and $750 million against production budgets of approximately $25 million, $28 million, and $45 million. Those grosses belong to the theatrical marketplace, not entirely to Disney: theaters, distribution, and marketing still had to be paid. Their importance here is the combination of comparatively restrained production costs and an expanding audience.

Technical investment helped make those economics possible. Disney and Pixar developed the Computer Animation Production System, or CAPS. The approximately $10 million investment digitized coloring and compositing while expanding the possibilities of the multiplane camera. The animators still drew the characters by hand. Software made richer scenes practical without repeating all the physical camera work. In the ballroom sequence of Beauty and the Beast, a computer-generated background supported hand-drawn characters. Technology served the storytelling rather than replacing it.

04

1985–1990s · 00:37:33

The vault came home

One story could support many different occasions to spend time and money.

Home video challenged an established scarcity strategy. Disney had reissued its classics in theaters to introduce them to new generations; family members worried that selling tapes would damage that cycle. Eisner won approval for a limited Pinocchio VHS release in 1985. The hosts describe 1.7 million copies selling at $29.95 retail, roughly $50 million of retail sales from a film that already existed. That is a measure of consumer spending, not Disney’s net receipts.

The experiment suggested that ownership at home did not necessarily eliminate demand elsewhere. A child could watch repeatedly, become more attached to the characters, and still want another Disney experience. The effect grew with new releases: the hosts report 30 million Aladdin tapes and 32 million Lion King tapes. Their estimates of the resulting cash generation are deliberately approximate. The important change was a second substantial commercial window after the theatrical release, with very little new filmmaking required.

Retail stores made the characters a regular presence in shopping malls. The company built more than 750 stores, turning affection for the films into visits, gifts, costumes, and toys. On Broadway, Beauty and the Beast was followed by The Lion King. Ben and David emphasize the extraordinary longevity of the stage production: they cite more than $11 billion of cumulative gross revenue across Broadway and touring productions. That is ticket revenue shared with theaters and other participants, not $11 billion of Disney profit.

Eisner also changed the scale of a park visit. Hotels, the Vacation Club, additional parks, and eventually cruises made Disney a destination for an entire family vacation. The expensive exception was Euro Disney, whose roughly $4 billion development struggled for years. The official episode correction adds an important qualification to this era: its debt burden helped produce a later period of cost-cutting, and several subsequent parks required substantial improvements. The achievements of the resort strategy do not make every investment or every execution successful.

05

1994–1995 · 00:54:32

The partnership broke before the numbers did

A successful enterprise lost the relationships that had helped it succeed.

The Lion King made 1994 a spectacular commercial year. Inside Disney, it was a year of loss. Frank Wells died in a helicopter accident on Easter Sunday. His disappearance removed both an operating leader and the intermediary between Eisner and Katzenberg. Months later Eisner required emergency heart surgery, leaving difficult decisions unresolved while he recovered.

Katzenberg believed he had been promised the number-two position if Wells departed. Others, including Roy E. Disney, questioned whether he was ready to run the entire company. The hosts preserve those competing accounts rather than treating a disputed promise as settled fact. Katzenberg left, pursued a compensation claim that reportedly settled for $280 million, and founded DreamWorks with Steven Spielberg and David Geffen. Its animation studio recruited Disney talent, creating a formidable competitor nearby.

Animation had also lost Ashman, who had died from AIDS. The succeeding film slate did not reproduce the same combination of creative momentum and commercial scale. That does not mean the films had no audience: Acquired’s official correction acknowledges the affection listeners have for such titles as Atlantis, Treasure Planet, and Brother Bear. The narrower business observation is that those releases did not match the Renaissance’s theatrical performance.

Eisner initially took on Wells’s responsibilities himself. At the same time, he pursued a different kind of expansion. After changes to television ownership rules, studios and networks could combine more readily. In 1995 he struck a roughly $19 billion deal for Capital Cities/ABC, whose largest shareholder was Berkshire Hathaway. Beneath the familiar broadcast network sat ESPN. That asset would change both Disney’s financial capacity and the difficulty of explaining what sort of company Disney had become.

06

1990s–2010s · 01:05:55

The other engine

A monthly cable payment behaved very differently from a beloved film.

ESPN’s leverage came from programming that cable subscribers were unwilling to lose. Alongside advertising, it collected affiliate fees from cable operators for each household receiving the channel. The operator paid even when that household did not watch sports. Threatening to withdraw valuable live coverage made those negotiations consequential for distributors trying to retain their own customers.

The hosts describe fees rising from cents to dollars per subscriber over time. Long sports-rights agreements helped ESPN sell programming while increasing what distributors paid for access. SportsCenter built a habit around highlights and personalities, extending the value of the rights. Disney could also negotiate for other channels alongside ESPN. The combination supported predictable cash generation on a scale far beyond an individual movie release.

This was an extraordinary acquisition outcome, but Ben and David resist rewriting it as a perfectly foreseen plan. ESPN was not the full rationale for purchasing ABC. Its subsequent pricing power became a much larger prize than the deal’s participants had expected. They cite the cable-networks segment contributing about 60% of Disney’s operating income during 2008–2011, while noting that estimates of ESPN’s share of that segment came from analysts.

The distinction matters. Disney’s characters could live for generations and feed parks, products, and repeat viewings. A live game was mostly perishable, but ESPN’s contracts could turn access to those games into steady cash. The practical relationship was capital allocation: the cable business helped finance parks and the acquisition of more stories. It did not need to turn SportsCenter into a fairy-tale franchise to be valuable. Disney now contained two powerful businesses with different operating requirements.

07

1995–2004 · 01:21:26

A second fight to save Disney

Commercial resilience and managerial legitimacy came apart.

The attempt to replace Wells with Hollywood agent Michael Ovitz illustrated the difficulty of managing the enlarged company. An agent’s work depended on relationships, dealmaking, and many simultaneous opportunities. Operating Disney demanded decisions, processes, and coordination across enormous organizations. The partnership failed quickly. Ovitz left after just over a year with a roughly $140 million severance package.

The company’s old creative weaknesses became more visible after September 11. Travel disruption damaged the parks, while declining animation momentum weakened consumer products. The Bass family faced a margin call elsewhere and sold about $2 billion of Disney shares. Eisner lost a major source of shareholder support just when the company was under pressure. ESPN remained a financial anchor, but it could not settle the argument about leadership or restore animation by itself.

In November 2003, Roy E. Disney resigned from the board. He and Gold launched SaveDisney.com, making a public case against Eisner’s management and succession planning. Their complaints included creative decline, weak relationships with partners, and inadequate park investment. The episode’s later correction is relevant here: although Eisner had expanded the parks dramatically, the hosts acknowledge legitimate criticism of the cost-cutting and weaker projects that followed Euro Disney’s debt problems.

Comcast then offered approximately $54 billion in stock for Disney in February 2004. To a cable distributor, owning ESPN meant owning a supplier with enormous negotiating leverage. The bid also amplified the public impression of vulnerability. At the March shareholder meeting, about 43% of shares voted to withhold support from Eisner. The board removed his chairmanship while retaining him as CEO temporarily. Comcast withdrew its bid; the succession question remained.

08

2005 · 01:41:58

A candidate argued for the future

The internal successor had to offer something more than continuity.

Bob Iger had entered Disney through Capital Cities/ABC and become president and chief operating officer. That made him the obvious internal candidate and connected him to an unpopular administration. His challenge was to persuade the board that he could change the company while having helped run it. He focused his candidacy on a program for the future.

Iger’s three priorities were high-quality branded content, embracing technology, and expanding Disney’s global reach. The technology commitment was meaningful in an industry that had watched digital distribution disrupt music and often treated Silicon Valley as an adversary. The international ambition also required more than translating existing releases: markets such as China and India offered enormous potential audiences but were not equally accessible.

The board selected Iger in March 2005. The episode dates Eisner’s departure and Iger’s transition to that September. One of Iger’s first calls was to Steve Jobs. Disney distributed Pixar’s films, but Jobs’s relationship with Eisner had become badly damaged. Iger had decided that repairing a distribution agreement would not be enough. He wanted Pixar’s leadership to help rebuild Disney Animation itself.

The hosts frame Eisner’s record with two truths. His first decade revived Disney; the following decade brought damaging conflict and creative drift. Yet the acquisition of ABC and ESPN created tremendous financial value throughout that second period. The company’s loss of strategic simplicity was real, and so was the capital that ESPN supplied. Iger inherited both.

09

1960s–1991 · 01:52:17

A future built outside Disney

Animation’s next method grew at the intersection of art and computing.

John Lasseter wanted to be a Disney animator, studied at CalArts, and joined the studio. His interest in computer animation eventually put him at odds with its management. In the hosts’ account, a proposed computer-animated approach to The Brave Little Toaster failed a test of immediate speed and cost savings. Lasseter was dismissed. The technology was not yet ready to make a feature in the way he imagined, but he continued looking for what it might make possible.

Ed Catmull approached the same ambition from computer science. At the University of Utah he worked on computer graphics, including a film of a modeled hand. He later built a graphics program at New York Tech before George Lucas recruited him to Lucasfilm in 1979. With Alvy Ray Smith and their colleagues, Catmull developed tools that expanded what filmmakers could show. Lasseter joined the group and supplied the animation and storytelling judgment it needed.

The group developed the Pixar Image Computer and pursued specialized imaging uses, but its business inside Lucasfilm was not the animated-feature studio its creative staff hoped to build. Lucas needed cash during his divorce and decided to sell it. Steve Jobs, after leaving Apple, became the buyer. In 1986 he paid $5 million to Lucasfilm and put another $5 million into the new independent company, initially owning 70% alongside employees.

Its first commercial relationship with Disney involved CAPS, the system that supported Disney’s own Renaissance. Short films demonstrated that computer animation could make an audience feel for objects; the company also developed RenderMan and made commercials. Those activities did not solve its business model. Hardware sales were limited, and a feature required financing. Lasseter’s renewed contact with Disney turned an idea for a television special into the possibility of Toy Story.

10

1993–2004 · 02:03:11

A hit became negotiating leverage

Owning the technology was not the same as owning the business.

The original film agreement reflected Disney’s financing and distribution power. Disney put up the production and marketing money, retained the intellectual property and sequel rights, and gave Pixar a small share of profits after relevant costs. It held options for additional films; sequels would not satisfy the original picture commitment. Pixar accepted because making a feature was the team’s dream, and Disney was willing to back it.

The production process made story revision possible before the most expensive work. Artists assembled drawings and temporary dialogue into reels that could be watched, criticized, and remade. Models, layout, animation, shading, lighting, and rendering followed. The technology did not remove the animator’s performance: bodies and faces still had to express character deliberately. The hosts describe a rendering farm of 117 computers processing only a few minutes of finished footage each week.

That iterative process also rescued the film. Disney’s notes pushed Woody toward an abrasive character the audience could not love. A disastrous screening in late 1993 stopped production. Pixar asked for time, returned to the story, and rebuilt the characters into a version Disney would continue funding. The lesson the hosts draw is specific to Pixar’s process: polished animation could not repair a story that failed in its rough form.

Jobs prepared an IPO for the week after Toy Story opened, gambling on a success that had not yet occurred. The film’s November 1995 release became a major hit, and Pixar raised about $140 million when it went public. Capital changed the next negotiation: Pixar could now pay half the production costs and seek half the upside. Disney still received distribution economics, and the new five-picture commitment did not resolve every disagreement.

Toy Story 2 exposed the problem in the sequel clause. Disney originally wanted a direct-to-video release. Pixar resisted creating a lower-status production alongside its theatrical work and pushed to make a film worthy of theaters. Disney approved that change in 1998. Jobs then wanted the sequel to count toward the five-picture commitment. Eisner refused: the agreement specifically excluded sequels. Toy Story 2 ultimately grossed almost $500 million, but its success did not reduce the number of films Pixar still owed Disney. Both companies earned from the film while the disagreement over control deepened.

Finding Nemo turned that tension into a more personal breach. After viewing an early story reel, Eisner wrote a board memo predicting a failure that would weaken Pixar’s negotiating position. The memo leaked. To Jobs, Disney’s partner appeared to be hoping for the film to disappoint, even though Disney shared substantially in its financial outcome. The hosts point out that rough reels were supposed to be revised; an unsuccessful early version was part of Pixar’s method. The finished film grossed about $871 million worldwide and became an enormous DVD success. Rather than giving Disney leverage, it strengthened Pixar’s conviction that it could bargain from the position of a successful independent studio.

Jobs demanded a distributor relationship with much more favorable economics: Pixar would finance production, retain the profits after a distribution fee, and have Disney relinquish its co-ownership of earlier films. The hosts also acknowledge why Eisner would reject those demands under the existing contract. Negotiations broke down in January 2004. Disney began Circle 7 Animation to develop sequels to Pixar-originated properties it controlled. A productive creative partnership had become a confrontation over rights, money, and trust.

11

2006 · 02:34:30

Buying the people who could rebuild the core

The acquisition preserved a creative culture and extended its influence.

Iger offered a different premise. Disney would buy Pixar, preserve its identity in Emeryville, and have Catmull and Lasseter lead both Pixar and Disney Animation. The acquired team would help repair the acquirer’s central creative operation. According to the episode, Iger even allowed for the possibility that they might conclude Disney Animation could not be rescued. They visited, found talent they believed in, and chose to rebuild it.

The approximately $7.4 billion stock transaction made Jobs Disney’s largest individual shareholder and put him on its board. Before the announcement, he privately told Iger that his cancer had returned and offered him a chance to reconsider. Iger continued with the deal. The hosts connect Jobs’s circumstances to a question they had pursued in their research: why had Pixar sold after proving that it could compete independently?

Their answer depends on the people’s goals, not just the franchise economics. Pixar’s filmmakers wanted to make films; building parks, merchandise, and an entire Disney competitor was a different vocation. Jobs had carried much of the business work himself. Disney supplied a permanent home for the creative studio while Pixar’s leadership brought a different process to Disney Animation. The hosts see this as protecting both companies’ futures, rather than a defeated studio absorbing a stronger rival.

Tangled, Frozen, Zootopia, and Moana became evidence that Disney Animation could flourish again, while Pixar continued creating important films of its own. Ben and David consider the revival of Disney Animation central to the deal’s value, beyond calculating returns from Pixar’s individual franchises. The iterative story process and willingness to rebuild an unsuccessful reel were themselves part of what Disney acquired.

12

2009–2015 · 02:46:37

A larger collection of stories

The next acquisitions expanded the creative center rather than adding another cable network.

Marvel cost about $4 billion in 2009. It was not an obvious purchase at the time: some of the best-known characters were tied up in other studios’ licenses, and the eventual scale of the cinematic universe was far from guaranteed. Disney and Marvel built on the films already underway, coordinating characters and releases into an interconnected franchise. The hosts call its early phases a remarkable act of world-building.

Lucasfilm followed in 2012 for another roughly $4 billion. Here the attraction included a mythology already established across generations. The subsequent results were less uniform. The hosts admire Rogue One and Andor while criticizing the lack of a coherent plan across the newer main trilogy. They distinguish acquiring a great collection of characters from reliably finding another great story to tell with them.

Alongside the acquisitions, Frozen renewed Disney’s own character pipeline. Merchandise, songs, and park experiences gave the films further lives. The episode cites annual retail merchandise sales exceeding $50 billion by 2015; those are sales through the retail ecosystem, not Disney’s reported revenue. The broader point is the strength of demand for the characters. Across Iger’s first decade, the hosts describe parks revenue nearly doubling and operating income nearly tripling.

ESPN and the other cable networks provided the financial backdrop. Ben and David compare the acquisition prices with the cable segment’s annual profits to show Disney’s capacity to invest. That comparison does not mean every purchase was literally funded from a separate ESPN bank account: Pixar was paid for in stock, and the other deals also had their own financing structures. Predictable cable cash made the overall company more able to pursue these ambitions.

13

2015–2019 · 02:58:01

The new distribution problem

Protecting access to audiences required building a business Disney had never operated.

In August 2015, Iger disclosed subscriber losses at ESPN. The company later reported a loss of about three million subscribers that year, leaving roughly 92 million. A business whose revenue had appeared reliably upward now faced a structural change. Raising affiliate fees could offset some cancellations for a time, but the cable bundle was losing households. Across traditional media, companies began searching for ways to respond.

Simply licensing Disney’s stories to Netflix looked attractive: Disney could collect high-margin payments and continue making money from products and parks. The hosts’ concern was control of discovery. Netflix could decide which films its recommendation system presented to which customers. Owning a service would let Disney place its stories directly in front of families, but would also require it to acquire and retain those families itself.

Disney briefly pursued Twitter as a possible foundation before abandoning the idea. The more suitable technology came from BAMTech, whose origins lay in Major League Baseball’s streaming operation. Disney bought a minority stake in 2016 and moved to control in 2017. It announced services for ESPN and Disney and the withdrawal of content from its Netflix agreement. Money that had arrived from a distributor would be replaced by the expense and uncertainty of building a customer base.

Fox expanded the bet. The announced $52 billion transaction for its entertainment and international assets rose to $71.3 billion after Comcast competed for them. The hosts subtract major divestitures to discuss a rough $44 billion hurdle for the assets Disney kept; this is their valuation framing, not a new purchase-price disclosure. The assets included more of Hulu and substantial international operations, while Fox retained its news, sports, and broadcast-network businesses.

The results were far less compelling than Pixar, Marvel, or Lucasfilm in the hosts’ view. A deeper library did not automatically create an equivalent number of new subscribers, and the Indian assets disappointed expectations. Hulu also pulled Disney toward general entertainment alongside its curated family franchises. The purchase increased its scale, but made the streaming ambition larger, costlier, and harder to distinguish from the rest of the market.

14

2019–2022 · 03:19:01

A spectacular launch hid a harder question

A permanent subscription needs a different cadence from an occasional exceptional film.

Disney+ arrived in November 2019 after a presentation built around the breadth of its library and an initial $6.99 monthly price. The hosts cite ten million signups in the first day and 26 million in the first quarter. Then COVID closed parks and kept families home. Disney passed 100 million subscribers within sixteen months, exceeding its original five-year target. Subscription growth seemed to validate the strategy just as the rest of the company faced an extraordinary shock.

But a service needs people to remain subscribed between its best releases. Production, engineering, marketing, and reacquisition all become ongoing responsibilities. A theatrical film could be an event followed by a distinct home-video window. Streaming offered neither the same event economics nor another sale of the same title every time it reached a household. Its revenue had to support the continual operation of the platform and its content slate.

That cadence strained the franchises. Marvel had just brought its major arc to a satisfying conclusion with Endgame; it was now asked to supply more stories for the service. Star Wars faced its own creative disagreements, while Pixar and Disney Animation were changing leadership. The hosts argue that multiplying releases can damage an existing universe when the additions weaken what audiences valued about it. They do not claim that every streaming show failed: their discussion includes affection for individual series.

ESPN faced pressure from both directions. Cable subscribers were leaving while sports rights grew more expensive. Technology companies could bid using economics beyond a single sports channel, and leagues could capture more of the value of their games. Ben and David still credit ESPN’s creative production. Their narrower argument is that highlights and commentary do not give it the same durable, repeatedly monetizable library as Disney’s animated stories.

Iger stepped down as CEO in February 2020, with parks executive Bob Chapek succeeding him. The pandemic made the transition far more difficult than a steady execution period. Streaming losses, organizational turmoil, and other operating disputes followed. The board removed Chapek in November 2022 and brought Iger back. The hosts interpret the succession as a mistake about what the next phase required: the streaming strategy was still being discovered, not merely administered.

15

2022–2026 · 03:42:15

The parks carried the company again

The source of Disney’s profits had moved back toward physical experiences.

Iger’s return brought more work on streaming economics, another shareholder challenge, and decisions about ESPN’s future. Disney began reporting sports separately in 2023. The episode describes the later NFL transaction for a 10% ESPN stake and the full direct-to-consumer ESPN service. Bundling ESPN, Disney+, and Hulu could attract casual customers, but the hosts emphasize a second purpose: a bundle can make households less likely to cancel than a single service.

The parks’ recovery was financial more than a new attendance record. The hosts cite roughly 145 million annual visits, below a pre-pandemic peak of 157 million, with higher spending per visit helping profits recover. That distinction matters when describing demand. A crowded park and stronger earnings do not by themselves establish that more people are coming than before.

At the recording, the hosts describe Experiences producing about $10 billion of operating income, compared with approximately $4.7 billion for Entertainment and about $3 billion for sports. These are rounded segment figures with different underlying businesses; they should not be confused with corporate net income or a clean sum of every reported revenue line. In their telling, parks and cruises supplied almost 60% of segment operating profit, reversing the earlier dominance of cable.

Streaming had also improved. The hosts describe approximately $13 billion of cumulative losses in building the direct-to-consumer business, followed by roughly $1 billion of annual profit. That recovery did not recreate cable’s margins. They contrast Disney’s scale with Netflix’s much larger streaming business to explain why owning a strong library alone had not produced the same operating leverage.

The next investment program put $60 billion over a decade toward parks and cruises, including new ships and domestic attractions. Physical capacity cannot expand like cable distribution once did, so higher prices also require better experiences. The episode closes its chronology with Josh D’Amaro becoming CEO in March 2026 and Dana Walden taking a new president and chief creative officer role. These statements describe the hosts’ recording-era picture, not subsequent updates.

16

The hosts’ concluding analysis · 03:59:22

Durable myths in a less forgiving market

The business can endure without recovering every advantage of its best years.

Ben and David ask what Disney could realistically have done instead of building Disney+. Staying with the old model was not a complete answer once theatergoing, home video, and cable economics changed. A smaller premium service combined with licensing to a larger platform might have preserved quality and reach. But it would also have left discovery partly in another company’s hands. The hosts disagree about how reliably excellent Disney films would rise through Netflix’s recommendations; the episode does not resolve that counterfactual.

They are also skeptical that a direct subscriber relationship has yet created all the promised synergies with the parks. Having an email address or a unified login is not the same as delivering a materially better vacation. The stronger strategic argument for the service is control over the presentation of Disney’s stories. The cost is the scale and content breadth needed to make a subscription product work.

Their bear case begins with weakened engines. Cable’s generous economics are eroding; streaming is expensive; and the next generation of enormously successful franchises is uncertain. Acquisitions may have supplied decades of excellent material without guaranteeing an inexhaustible supply. Repeated sequels can also draw down goodwill. Those concerns are about future renewal, not a claim that Disney has ceased producing work people love.

David’s bull case emphasizes the persistence of shared cultural myths. A disappointing sequel need not prevent the next generation from discovering an earlier film and caring about its world. Parks embed that attachment in family experience. Ben also points to financial recovery after the pandemic and streaming losses. The hosts discuss Bluey and Nintendo as speculative examples of stories Disney could steward; those are ideas and opinions, not announced acquisition plans.

They apply Seven Powers specifically to the streaming-era comparison. Disney has an unusually strong stock of controlled franchise intellectual property, which they describe as a cornered resource relative to smaller vertical services. Yet Netflix’s much larger revenue base demonstrates the importance of scale economies. Great characters do not automatically make Disney the most profitable streaming platform. Their discussion leans toward being a strong number two rather than pursuing every kind of content necessary to displace Netflix.

The final disagreement is one of emphasis. Ben sees an environment that has become less generous: the company must work harder to replace the surplus once supplied by cable and home video. David sees generational stories capable of surviving creative and economic cycles. Neither conclusion guarantees a return to the peak conditions of the 1990s or early 2010s. Together they leave a more precise picture of Disney: a valuable collection of stories and experiences whose ability to earn from those stories depends on distribution, management, and a changing market.
