Berkshire Hathaway
Part II

From the profit rusting in a tractor yard to the telephone call that could save Wall Street.

Released May 12, 2021Created Unpublished23 minute read

A reading companion to Acquired’s Berkshire Hathaway Part II, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal. This recording follows the change from bargain assets to enduring businesses, ending with the Salomon rescue and the hosts’ analysis. Chapter navigation has no invented listening times.

01 · 1924–1965

A second mind

Charlie Munger wanted independence, and a place in a growing city.

Charlie Munger was born in Omaha on New Year’s Day, 1924. His grandfather, a federal judge appointed by Theodore Roosevelt, supplied a demanding household ideal: concentrate on the task in front of you and control your spending. Like Warren Buffett, Charlie worked in Ernest Buffett’s grocery store and discovered that he disliked exchanging manual labor for a small wage. Wealth meant independence. But where Warren wanted freedom from other people’s instructions, Charlie imagined the intellectual independence of John Maynard Keynes.

Books offered another form of company. Benjamin Franklin became a lifelong hero, and Munger made what he called friends of the eminent dead. Ben Gilbert interrupts the romance of learning from famous lives with a qualification: those stories also contain survivorship bias and revisionist history. The episode introduces Charlie as extraordinarily intelligent and unusually confident, rather than treating his confidence as proof that every conclusion was correct.

At Michigan he studied mathematics and became interested in physics. The war took him through military engineering studies at New Mexico and Caltech. David Rosenthal recalls, with explicit uncertainty, a posting in Alaska as a meteorologist. Afterward Charlie entered Harvard Law without having completed an undergraduate degree. He chose Los Angeles for his career: the weather appealed to him, and the city seemed large and promising enough to become somebody without already being too established to enter its leading ranks.

Then his marriage fell apart, and his son Teddy developed leukemia. Teddy died in 1955 at nine. Munger’s later description of his response was severe: do not let one tragedy become two or three through a failure of will. Ben cautions that this was a prescription Charlie could give himself, not something to deliver to another grieving parent. Charlie set two goals: find a new spouse and expand his activities beyond law. A law partner introduced him to Nancy Borthwick, a Stanford economics graduate who would challenge him. Each brought two surviving children; together they had four more.

His clients seemed to possess the freedom he wanted. Mining magnate Harvey Mudd was among them. Charlie began accepting equity alongside legal fees, buying stocks, and developing Southern California real estate. By the early 1960s, the hosts put his wealth around $1.5 million. In 1959 he returned to Omaha to settle his father’s estate. The Davis family, early Buffett investors who thought Warren resembled Charlie, arranged a dinner. The two men were immediately absorbed in each other. David distinguishes the legend from its chronology: Charlie’s famous fit of laughter that put him on a restaurant floor came later that week, not at the first dinner.

Buffett described an occupation Charlie had barely imagined: investing full time through partnerships from Omaha. Asked whether Charlie could do the same in California, Warren considered the question and said yes. Charlie returned to law while starting an investment partnership. He also cofounded the firm that became Munger, Tolles & Olson, then left after only three years. In 1965, encouraged by Buffett and successful enough to make the choice, he became a full-time investor. The legal firm retained his name—and would return at the most dangerous moment in this story.

02 · 1960s

The profit rusting in the yard

A tractor dealership exposed the difference between earnings and spendable cash.

Munger learned Graham-style bargain hunting from Buffett, but he kept saying something that sounded almost beside the point to their circle: he liked great businesses. Warren was skilled at finding a price below the value of an enterprise’s assets. Charlie was increasingly interested in what happened after the purchase. A Southern California Caterpillar dealership made the distinction tangible. Tractors had to be bought before customers appeared. They sat on the lot, and every sale required another expensive tractor to replace the inventory. Growth demanded still more money.

A business could report a respectable return while leaving its owner with nothing to take away. In the passage the hosts quote, the construction equipment dealer surveys used machines taken in trade and sees his profit rusting in the yard. Charlie wanted to supply a small amount of capital once and receive much more over time. That led him toward competitive advantage: a reason competitors could not simply enter and compete away the attractive profits. A moat mattered because it protected cash generation, not because the metaphor sounded reassuring.

The friendship gave Warren an unusual critic. Growing success made other people deferential; Charlie’s high opinion of himself limited his deference. Warren could name an investment and have somebody try to find the holes. Their discussions continued by telephone and during California visits. Charlie argued that Graham’s caution had been shaped by the Depression: he saw a future more full of hazards than opportunities. Postwar California made that frame look incomplete. Buffett’s command of Graham could itself become a trap, like a Civil War veteran who could turn every conversation back to Gettysburg. The shift toward durable businesses was an argument between people, not a sudden discovery that bargains no longer mattered.

03 · 1963–1975

Stamps become a treasury

The storefront incentive carried cash that could be invested elsewhere.

Blue Chip Stamps was not a stamp collectors’ shop. Stores bought trading stamps, gave them to shoppers, and shoppers pasted them into books to redeem for goods. The arrangement had begun as an incentive to pay cash instead of buying on credit. A separate stamp company allowed customers to collect the same currency across many stores. That created two attractions the hosts identify: float between the store’s payment and the shopper’s redemption, and a two-sided network effect as more participating stores attracted more collectors.

S&H Green Stamps dominated nationally. In California, store chains had formed Blue Chip and excluded S&H. S&H and the Justice Department sued in 1963. Munger saw an opportunity in the falling stock: his legal judgment was that the operating company could survive while its store owners would be required to divest. A 1968 consent decree required the stores to sell 45%. Buffett, Munger and their friend Rick Guerin bought roughly that amount through their separate vehicles. They were not yet the familiar two-man Berkshire partnership on a stage.

After Buffett dissolved his partnership, he bought more of the companies whose shares he had distributed. His personal Berkshire ownership rose from 18% to 36%; Diversified Retailing from 20% to 39%; Blue Chip from 2% to 13%. Berkshire also owned Blue Chip, as did Diversified, Munger’s partnership and Guerin. These overlapping interests would become a regulatory problem. Meanwhile Blue Chip’s stamp business was deteriorating. The hosts say its core activity declined 90% during the 1970s as consumers moved on. The attraction was not a prediction of a stamp revival. They could redeploy the float inside Blue Chip into other businesses.

Bill Ramsey, Blue Chip’s president, was asked to look for acquisitions. In 1971 he brought them a California family business: See’s Candy. Its physical assets were worth about $5 million, while the family already had a $30 million offer. For a purchaser trained to pay less than liquidating value, that looked unacceptable. Charlie pushed the discussion toward earnings: roughly $4 million of annual pretax profit, growing around 12% without requiring more capital. Customers loved the product; perhaps that loyalty supported higher prices.

Warren still would not pay $30 million. He offered $25 million, and the family agreed. Blue Chip acquired See’s. In the hosts’ account, the little candy company subsequently sent more than $2 billion of free cash flow to Blue Chip and later Berkshire. It supplied lived evidence for the idea of a wonderful business at a fair price. Buffett would credit Charlie with understanding it early and himself with learning slowly.

Charlie was also learning from Warren’s decision to stop managing a partnership. His own first decade had produced about 28.3% annualized returns, but with much less steadiness. Losses of 31.9% in 1973 and 31.5% in 1974 scarred him. He worked toward recovery, earned 73.2% in 1975, then closed the partnership. A corporation offered a different relationship to fellow owners: they could sell their shares, while he could focus on enduring business value. The hosts do not suggest that shareholders ceased to matter. Buffett wanted Berkshire never to go out of business; he no longer organized his life around explaining one year’s investment result to limited partners.

04 · 1966–1974

An owner, without the fangs

The Washington Post required trust as well as a price.

Bill Ruane introduced Buffett to Capital Cities’ Tom Murphy in 1971. Murphy and Dan Burke ran the kind of lean media enterprise Warren admired. Murphy wanted him on the board, but Buffett wanted a large ownership position before accepting. Murphy disliked issuing shares as much as Warren did. They remained friends and advisers instead. Broadcasting rules also complicated board seats across television-owning companies, and Warren was interested in The Washington Post.

The episode briefly steps back to show what Warren had previously missed about media. In 1966 he bought 5% of Disney for $4 million, against an $80 million market capitalization. Mary Poppins had made about $30 million at the box office, but investors worried about difficult comparisons after a hit. Buffett saw a film that could keep earning from successive generations. Yet after roughly a year and a 50% gain, he sold. The hosts regard the premature exit as a major missed opportunity, while recognizing that the original purchase had made money. Their point is the changing understanding of duration, not a precise counterfactual net worth.

Katharine Graham had taken charge of the Post at 46, with four children and little professional preparation. By the time Warren approached, she was taking the company public amid the Pentagon Papers and then guiding it through Watergate. He proposed a joint bid for The New Yorker; she declined. The family’s dual-class control meant he could buy economic ownership without acquiring authority. Two years later he bought shares from the estate of chairman Fritz Beebe and in the market. At a 5% position he wrote that the purchase was a quantified compliment to the business and to Graham: writing a check separated conviction from conversation.

That did not automatically make an unfamiliar investor from Omaha welcome. At a later meeting in Washington, following a night in which Graham and Ben Bradlee had been making Watergate publication decisions, Buffett brought a contract. Berkshire would never buy another Post share without the Graham family’s permission. His ownership had already reached 12%. He voluntarily removed a possible threat to family control. Graham accepted his advice, but he still longed for a board invitation. Murphy carried messages between them; a weekend at Buffett’s California house finally brought the awkward courtship to a conclusion and Buffett joined the board.

The relationship lasted for the rest of Graham’s life, with Buffett serving on the board for most of the next 37 years. David explicitly leaves the personal nature of their closeness unresolved. Economically, the hosts describe the leading metropolitan newspaper as a local tollbooth: readers brought advertisers, advertising money financed stronger subscription offers, and the market could tip toward a dominant paper. This was an argument about that era’s business, not a claim that a newspaper franchise would remain insulated indefinitely.

Berkshire paid about $10 million for the 12% position. David follows it forward within this recording to a $1.1 billion exit from Graham Holdings in 2014, after the newspaper itself had been sold. He calls the capital appreciation about a 12% annualized return, but stresses that he does not have the dividend cash-flow data. Decades of distributions make that an incomplete measure of the investment’s return. The Post was also becoming a network of relationships: Graham would soon help Buffett meet the person who could rescue GEICO.

05 · 1970s–1983

The structure nobody could explain

Wesco brought an investigation—and the need to simplify.

The web connecting Berkshire, Blue Chip and Diversified drew SEC scrutiny. Around 1975 Chuck Rickershauser, a former Munger law partner who had handled See’s, warned that securities charges were being considered. He spent weeks drawing their ownership chart. His joke—that there had to be an indictment somewhere in it—captured how hard the structure was to explain. The episode’s central example is Blue Chip’s investment in Wesco Financial, a Southern California financial services company.

Wesco had agreed to a transaction with Financial Corp of Santa Barbara. Buffett and Munger considered the proposed deal unfavorable to Wesco, persuaded its controlling family and board to abandon it, and then invested through Blue Chip. When the merger disappeared, Wesco’s shares fell. They declined to exploit the lower price. David cannot recall whether they paid the old merger price or slightly more, and the companion leaves that detail unresolved. The investigators’ puzzle was why investors who had helped stop a merger would then voluntarily pay above the available market price.

Buffett’s testimony emphasized management’s feelings: controlling ownership would not make Louis Vincenti want to work for people he regarded as unfair. Munger invoked Franklin and said fairness was consistent with shareholder obligations. David sees a long-term bargain with managers. Ben pushes back against conflating that explanation with every Berkshire acquisition. Berkshire still wanted attractive purchase prices, and it also selected people who would keep working after selling because the enterprise remained their life’s purpose. A seller’s continued motivation and an acquirer’s generosity were different questions.

The investigation lasted years and burdened a business trying to present itself as a trustworthy partner. The hosts describe an agreement in which Blue Chip would not repeat the disputed conduct, without an admission of guilt, and the group would simplify. Diversified merged into Berkshire. Charlie became chairman of Wesco, establishing more distance from Warren. The intended Blue Chip merger took longer; the hosts do not know precisely why. It finally joined Berkshire in 1983. The familiar corporate shape emerged through this cleanup, rather than being the original master plan.

06 · 1976–1980

GEICO must survive three problems

A reputation helped raise money; an operator had to remake the business.

GEICO had expanded beyond government employees, its original and relatively safe driver pool. Underwriting and prices had not adjusted adequately to the broader population. Insurance float now worked in reverse. Premiums had already arrived, but they were too small for the future claims they had purchased. The company could not go back and collect more money for past coverage. In 1976 it announced a $190 million underwriting loss, eliminated its dividend and faced regulators. Its stock fell from $61 to $2.

Buffett did not simply assume an old favorite would recover. GEICO’s board brought in Jack Byrne, a seasoned Travelers executive who had left after being passed over for the top job. Byrne proposed persuading other insurers to reinsure some of GEICO’s future losses. He argued that a collapse could leave the industry with the problem anyway. Ben asks whether that consequence was certain; David presents it as the argument Byrne made, rather than supplying a detailed legal mechanism. Reinsurance would shift some risk to other insurers, but it would not itself provide all the cash GEICO needed for current claims.

Katharine Graham arranged a meeting at her Washington home. Buffett questioned Byrne for hours and came away convinced he had found an operator who could do the work. The next day he bought $4 million of GEICO shares at $2 each. David does not disentangle the exact ownership percentage of that purchase from the subsequent financing. GEICO still needed new capital, and most major investment banks would not take the reputational risk of an offering that might fail.

Salomon Brothers would. John Gutfreund led the bank’s involvement in a $76 million convertible financing, which David describes as debt after Ben asks whether it was preferred stock. Buffett offered to take the entire offering if necessary, but at a lower price. Salomon could tell prospective investors that he already owned shares and would stand behind a financing. The deal was oversubscribed. Berkshire nevertheless bought a quarter of it at the full offering price. The stock rose to $8 as survival became more plausible. Capital and part of the inherited risk had been addressed; future pricing still had to be repaired.

State licenses constrained what GEICO could charge. Byrne asked states to approve higher rates. When New Jersey refused, the episode’s quoted account has him surrendering the license, sending cancellation telegrams to 30,000 policyholders and firing 2,000 employees in an afternoon before an injunction could stop him. This was a brutal demonstration that he would leave a state rather than continue writing business at a loss. GEICO ultimately shrank to seven states and Washington, DC that allowed the repricing. Byrne also replaced management. The business stopped losing money and began growing again.

Across 1976–1980, Berkshire spent about $47 million, reaching roughly one-third ownership. GEICO’s later repurchases lifted that interest toward half without further Berkshire purchases. The hosts briefly carry the thread into the mid-1990s: buying the other half required $2.3 billion, with the Capital Cities/ABC sale to Disney helping release capital. David estimates GEICO’s value around $50 billion at the time of recording; it is explicitly an estimate, not a current valuation. They also reconsider the simple regret that Buffett once sold GEICO. It nearly failed in the intervening years, and his return at $2 was very different from passively enduring that history.

Ben distinguishes finding a security that is safer than the market believes from making it safer through one’s own involvement. Buffett’s name and backstop helped a financing happen. That could create value unavailable to a passive buyer. But it also planted a dangerous idea: if Berkshire’s reputation could save GEICO, perhaps it could save other institutions whose underlying behavior Warren did not control.

07 · 1980s

Enough capital to matter

The growing balance sheet widened the opportunities—and the temptations.

The hosts move quickly through the expanding group. Berkshire bought Nebraska Furniture Mart from Mrs. B. In their telling, she later quarreled with her children over the business and opened a competitor across the street at 95. Berkshire paid $5 million to buy it and obtained a noncompete. The Buffalo Evening News required a newspaper war, substantial capital and disagreements between Buffett and Munger. These are short examples in the recording, and this companion does not fill them out from other histories.

The episode places the era against inflation, Volcker’s response and the debt-driven activity of the 1980s. Buffett attacked the efficient market hypothesis in his “Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville” talk. The hosts frame his objection as a distinction between price volatility and the possibility of permanent failure. Borrowing does not merely amplify an abstract fluctuation; it can make an enterprise unable to meet obligations and end the game. Their account connects the decade’s takeover financing, junk bonds and mortgage securities to a willingness to treat leverage as a route to higher returns.

Berkshire also finally joined Murphy and Burke in the Capital Cities purchase of ABC. Buffett invested $517 million, an amount that the now multibillion-dollar Berkshire could supply. Equity allowed a smaller company to buy a much larger one without relying entirely on debt. David does not have the complete transaction structure in his notes: he believes there was convertible equity and some borrowing, at a reasonable rather than extreme level. The exact financing mix remains qualified. Buffett stepped away from the Post board to accommodate the broadcasting conflict. His balance sheet could now alter the terms of a major corporate transaction.

08 · 1987

The white knight enters the pit

Loyalty to Gutfreund overcame the warning in front of them.

At Salomon, a rising revenue line did not translate into comparable rewards for outside owners. Traders demanded enormous bonuses and threatened to leave. The hosts describe a firm in which employees captured much of the success while shareholders bore growing risk. Michael Lewis’s Liar’s Poker, intended as a warning about this world, became an inspiration to people who wanted to join it. Gutfreund had become CEO, and corporate raider Ron Perelman bought the largest existing shareholder’s position and threatened his control.

Gutfreund called Buffett. Salomon had stood behind GEICO when other banks would not; Berkshire’s loyalty and reputation as a management-friendly investor could now protect him. Over Rosh Hashanah weekend in September 1987, while Perelman was unavailable, they arranged a $700 million convertible preferred investment. The recording describes a 15% coupon. Both Buffett and Munger accepted board seats. On a tour above the trading floor, Charlie asked whether Warren really wanted to invest in this. Warren paused, then went ahead. Ben observes that Charlie followed him into it too.

Black Monday came on October 19. Salomon suffered $75 million in trading losses and its stock fell. At their first board meeting, Buffett and Munger faced a plan to reprice employee options. The employees wanted recovery opportunities at a lower exercise price while existing shareholders absorbed the decline. They objected, then acquiesced, partly because a public fight could further damage the investment. The hosts describe this as the start of a slippery slope, not an innocuous compensation adjustment.

Behind the board’s back, Gutfreund also promised John Meriwether’s fixed-income arbitrage group 15% of its trading profits. The traders had no corresponding personal exposure to the losses. This was a different 15% from the coupon attached to Berkshire’s security: one paid capital, the other rewarded employees for upside generated with the firm’s money. Berkshire had entered to support familiar management, but its board seats did not give it command over the incentives and conduct that were accumulating beneath it.

09 · August 1991

Four million dollars against the firm

The auction scandal became a crisis of disclosure and daily funding.

While Buffett vacationed in Reno, Salomon president Tom Strauss and general counsel Don Feuerstein called about Treasury auction violations by government bond desk head Paul Mozer. They implied that Charlie was comfortable with the response. He was not. The initial description understated what had happened. Mozer submitted unauthorized bids in real clients’ names and bids for invented clients, using them to evade the 35% limit and corner auctions. Others who needed the bonds then faced a squeeze. The hosts say several small firms failed amid the resulting volatility, while Salomon made only about $4 million of incremental profit.

Meriwether and Gutfreund had known about the problem four months earlier. The general counsel had identified criminal conduct but advised that there was no technical reporting obligation. Management neither properly informed the board and other regulators nor removed Mozer and imposed controls. When the story surfaced in August, counterparties and lenders began to hesitate. The episode cites roughly $150 billion in market exposure against $4 billion of equity and $50 billion rolling over daily. Its separate “60 times” leverage remark does not reconcile arithmetically with those rounded figures; the diagram below keeps the cited amounts separate rather than deriving that ratio.

On August 12 the Federal Reserve wrote that it was deeply troubled by both the firm’s actions and its inaction. It questioned whether the relationship could continue unless Salomon made substantial changes within ten days. Gutfreund and the general counsel withheld the letter. The Fed assumed the board knew. Buffett did not. A press release promising investigation could not satisfy officials who believed the firm was ignoring a specific warning.

By Friday the pressure forced Gutfreund out. He effectively handed Buffett a firm with no succession plan. Buffett flew to New York and met officials, still without understanding the concealed letter. They told him to prepare for all eventualities. Walking away meant losing $700 million, but the hosts think the larger danger was Berkshire’s reputation as a dependable partner. Remaining could also destroy that reputation if the rescue failed. Warren accepted the interim chairmanship and installed Deryck Maughan, head of the investment banking division and relatively distant from the trading culture, as CEO.

10 · 1991–1992, with the episode’s coda

The telephone and the open files

Saving the firm required public credibility and evidence against its own employees.

On Sunday a new notice threatened to end Salomon’s auction privileges before Tokyo opened. Buffett instructed the lawyers to prepare bankruptcy papers while trying every government connection he had. He reached Treasury Secretary Nick Brady, broke down and pleaded for more time. Brady consulted Alan Greenspan. A later call came from Treasury official Jerome Powell. In the episode’s account, Salomon could no longer bid for its own account but could continue bidding for customers. Buffett said that would do. The threatened immediate ending had become a narrow route to survival.

Even during that weekend, Gutfreund and his personal lawyer sought a $35 million severance agreement. Munger refused to engage or sign. The dispute later went to arbitration; Gutfreund received nothing. Buffett took responsibility for government and public trust while Maughan took the work inside the building. “Get it right, get it fast, get it out” was the instruction for discovering and disclosing wrongdoing.

Munger, Tolles & Olson returned to the story. Roy Olson proposed waiving attorney-client privilege so the firm could share what its lawyers found with the government. For the new leadership, evidence of past wrongdoing could demonstrate that the cleanup was real. Employees had to answer the investigators or lose their jobs, with their answers available to officials. Buffett’s congressional appearance supplied the public standard: losses of money could be understood; a loss of reputation would meet ruthlessness.

Salomon settled for a $190 million fine and a $100 million restitution fund. David assumes the fund would address injured institutions and does not specify its full distribution. Buffett left the chairmanship in 1992 as soon as he could, while remaining on the board and retaining the investment. The recording carries the financial story forward to the late-1990s sale, described by the hosts as a $9 billion transaction involving the Travelers/Citigroup lineage. Berkshire received $1.7 billion on its $700 million investment, plus coupon payments. Near the end Ben momentarily treats $1.7 billion as the profit; David corrects him: it was the amount out, before counting coupons.

Both hosts say the return did not justify the risk to everything Berkshire had built. The rescue nevertheless strengthened the very myth that had helped draw Warren into Salomon. Meriwether subsequently led Long-Term Capital Management; Mozer alone, in their telling, served four months in prison. David also qualifies the rescue story: officials feared the consequences of shutting such a large bank, not merely disappointing Warren Buffett. They see a rehearsal for systemic problems that would emerge later, without telling those later stories here.

11 · The hosts’ closing analysis

What belonged to Berkshire?

The enduring advantages were easier to see in acquisitions than in stock selection.

Before applying Seven Powers, Ben separates two activities. Owning whole businesses involves finding candidates, evaluating them, choosing or retaining managers, and deciding whether each subsidiary should consume capital for growth or send cash to headquarters. Investing in public securities involves a different daily practice. The episode’s inventory of acquired companies includes See’s, Wesco, the Buffalo News, Precision Steel Warehouse, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer, Fechheimer Brothers, Borsheims, H.H. Brown and Central States Indemnity, followed in the mid-1990s by the rest of GEICO, Helzberg Diamonds and RC Willey. The public positions discussed are the Post, Capital Cities and Salomon; Coca-Cola is acknowledged but reserved for another episode.

For whole-company acquisitions, Ben proposes counter-positioning against private equity. Berkshire invests its own corporate balance sheet. Buffett does not earn a management fee, a promote or a new fund from turning assets over. David agrees that this can focus attention on long-term value rather than a fund’s selling deadline. They test whether that difference actually wins deals, rather than simply making Berkshire unwilling to pay a competing price. The Mrs. B example provides their answer: a seller who values family management and the business’s legacy may accept less money for a credible promise that the acquirer will let the family keep running it. Ben qualifies the argument: retaining the family maximizes value only if they have acquired the right business.

Avoiding heavy acquisition debt strengthens that alignment because the enterprise has less chance of being forced into bankruptcy. The hosts do not call this market winner-take-all. Berkshire needs a niche among conglomerates, private equity and other buyers. They explicitly reject network economies as Berkshire’s own principal power even though stamps and newspapers had network effects within their businesses.

Branding is their firmest agreement. Equal dollars from Berkshire can be worth more to a recipient because Buffett’s presence changes what regulators, lenders, sellers and other investors believe. Salomon is their extreme example. Scale is disputed and then narrowed. David thinks the argument from insurance float to more investment is a stretch. Ben says the 1980s brought a useful middle size: large enough to influence boards, small enough that those successes materially affected Berkshire. Too much capital can reverse that benefit. David accepts the period-specific advantage while observing that it is not a sustainable power. They find no meaningful switching-cost case.

Process power remains unresolved. A system embodied across many employees is the usual model; Berkshire is a tiny organization whose final decisions are Warren’s, informed by Charlie. Is there a process inside one person’s head? Ben allows a liberal interpretation. David thinks process might be the only arguable power in ordinary public-market selection, but doubts Berkshire has a defensible exclusive capacity to see and exploit every inefficiency. Other investors can act on their own discoveries. Ben returns to investments made safer by Berkshire’s unique intervention. David replies that Buffett had to work hard to enter the Post at first; perhaps the mystique became a power after those relationships had been built. The discussion ends with that distinction open, rather than assigning Berkshire a clean score across seven boxes.

They add no separate playbook section because the narrative has already supplied it. On value creation, they see more than buying undervalued shares: combining insurance with operating businesses, financing GEICO’s survival, and preventing Salomon’s collapse. Byrne performed GEICO’s operating work. Ben asks about the harm to customers expelled from states; David thinks failure without repricing would also have harmed them, but they leave the consequences of crossing that boundary as a fair question. On Salomon they acknowledge the wider destruction and Berkshire’s earlier role in propping up corrupt management. Berkshire captured value effectively, including long tax deferrals when it held investments rather than selling.

For their final assessment they compare performance in 1970–1992 with the partnership era, rather than judging only the drama of the rescues. Ben reports 27.4% annualized returns, against 29.5% during the earlier partnership years, and A shares rising from $45 at the start of 1970 to $11,750 at the end of 1992. He treats this as another golden stretch and wonders whether Buffett could time markets. David counters that more recent timing had been less impressive; they reserve that argument for the next installment. The transcript does not supply a numerical S&P return here, so none is invented. Their enthusiasm for the overall record coexists with their judgment that Salomon’s investment-specific risk was unacceptable.

The conversation closes with personal recommendations: David recommends the Macklemore episode of Armchair Expert; Ben, nearing the end of his first viewing, praises The Sopranos. They invite listener corrections and ideas before the trilogy’s final installment, mention the community and Brad Stone book club, and say goodbye. This companion stops with that recording. Its bridge from bargain hunting to business ownership ends with a qualification: the reputation that helped Berkshire create value could also expose it to obligations far larger than the money it invested.
