Costco
The member’s bargain.

A warehouse club built an extraordinary institution by making a simple promise expensive to break: share the savings, earn the renewal.

Episode: Acquired · Ben Gilbert & David RosenthalReleased August 21, 2023Created October 12, 2026Scope: the history and business arguments in the 2023 episodeAbout 18 minutes to read

A narrative companion to the episode, revised against Acquired’s transcript. Figures and membership terms describe the 2023 discussion. Listening cues identify relevant passages; advertising and reissued audio may shift the clock.

01

1916–1954

Sol Price grows up in a labor movement

The episode begins with the upbringing that shaped Sol Price’s view of business.

Listening cue · 6:53 ↗

Sol Price was born in the Bronx in 1916 to Jewish immigrants from Belarus. His parents worked in New York’s garment factories, in conditions the hosts connect to the labor organizing that followed the Triangle Shirtwaist fire. They did not work at Triangle itself. David places Price’s later concern for workers and customers in this political environment, rather than treating it as a philosophy invented after he became wealthy.

An eye condition made Price self-conscious as a child, and the episode describes him directing that energy into school, where he skipped two grades. His family moved to San Diego during his high school years. There he met Helen Moskowitz, his future wife, whose family owned a scrap metal business. Shipbuilding and the Navy’s expansion during World War II helped both that business and the city grow.

Price earned a law degree at USC and returned to San Diego to practice. His clients brought him into the details of new retail businesses. One was a locker club for sailors: storing uniforms and personal belongings brought customers in, while laundry, haircuts, food, jewelry, and other services gave the business more ways to serve them. Another client, Four Star Jewelers, supplied an unusually large wholesale account in Los Angeles called Fedco.

Fedco was a nonprofit buying club for federal employees, started by postal workers pooling their purchasing power. Its membership cost $5 for life; the fee was not the large recurring profit stream that membership would become at Costco. Customers were willing to drive from San Diego to shop there. Price and the jewelers saw demand for the same offer closer to home, and Helen’s family had an empty warehouse that could house it.

02

1954–1975

FedMart turns a buying club into a retailer

A for-profit version of Fedco becomes the training ground for Costco’s future leaders.

Listening cue · 13:56 ↗

Before copying Fedco, Price tried to partner with it. He first proposed a joint venture, then offered a franchise arrangement in which Fedco would own the business. Its board declined both. In November 1954, Price and his partners opened FedMart in the San Diego warehouse. The episode emphasizes the crucial difference from Fedco: this was a for-profit company with an incentive to expand.

FedMart was a discount retailer, selling packaged food and general merchandise in ordinary quantities. It was not yet the bulk-pallet warehouse club familiar to Costco members. Membership initially restricted access to federal employees. The hosts explain that this restriction also let the business sell below manufacturers’ minimum retail prices, because it was not selling to the general public.

The first store’s sales reached $3 million against an expectation of $1 million. FedMart expanded to Phoenix and then Texas. Price left legal practice to run the company, and hired Jim Sinegal, a San Diego City College student, as a part-time bagger. Sinegal spent the next 22 years at FedMart and eventually ran its distribution and centralized warehousing operations. That particular experience would supply the idea for Price Club.

A 1959 public offering raised $2 million. FedMart used the capital for expansion and additional offerings, including gasoline, pharmacy, and its FM house brand. Fuel priced below nearby stations attracted shoppers who could also buy inside the store. The pharmacy challenged established margins; the hosts recount threats against the man who built that division. His successor later helped establish Costco’s pharmacy business.

Price taught four priorities in order: customer value, good employee wages and benefits, honest business practices, and returns for investors. The hosts stress the tradeoffs behind those commitments. Price opposed using a loss leader to draw people in and recovering the money through high markups elsewhere. They connect higher wages and benefits to lower turnover, lower training costs, and employee loyalty. At the time of the episode, Ben cites average hourly wages of $26 at Costco versus $19.50 at Walmart, and roughly 7% attrition among Costco employees after their first year.

03

The FedMart sale and its aftermath

A capital partner takes control

Sol and Robert Price seek expansion capital and end up losing control of the company.

Listening cue · 34:12 ↗

FedMart’s success did not give it the resources of larger competitors such as Kmart. Sol and his son Robert were strong at developing retail ideas, but the hosts describe them as less effective at operating at national scale. They needed either more capital or a sale. Their search took them to Europe, where Carrefour had pioneered the hypermarket: discount general merchandise combined with a full supermarket.

They partnered with German retailer Hugo Mann, hoping to fund expansion and turn FedMart stores into hypermarkets. Ben identifies two mistakes: failing to understand what Mann wanted from the deal, and selling him a majority stake while treating him as though he were a minority growth investor. David describes the result as a buyout where Price had expected growth capital.

At their first board meeting after the deal, Price and Mann fought. Mann removed Price and changed the locks on his office. Sinegal was left to tell the remaining organization what had happened. Ben’s interpretation is that Mann wanted FedMart’s valuable real estate, while Sol and Robert wanted investment in stores. The episode presents that motive as Ben’s reading of the situation, not as an independently established term of the transaction.

Instead of retiring, Sol and Robert leased a new office and began planning another business. They did not intend to repeat FedMart and compete directly with the expanding discount chains. They returned to the part of the business Sinegal had run: the central warehouse supplying the stores. Could the warehouse itself become the business, without the less profitable retail layer?

04

1976–1982

Price Club discovers its consumer business

A warehouse for small businesses finds a much larger market through a credit union.

Listening cue · 42:49 ↗

Price Club’s first warehouse opened in San Diego in 1976, in a former Howard Hughes aircraft hangar. Sol and Robert’s intended customers were small businesses such as restaurants, gas stations, and variety stores. Those businesses needed inventory but could not operate their own central warehouses. Suppliers would deliver directly to Price Club, and business owners would collect goods there to stock their shelves.

This removed much of the handling and transport required by the old store model. Goods could remain on pallets, and Price Club initially needed no separate distribution network. The founders also limited the assortment to about 3,000 high-volume items. A membership fee now paid for a useful wholesale service, rather than simply creating a legal distinction between members and the general public.

The opening was not the immediate success FedMart had enjoyed. Recruiting business customers was difficult, and after a few months the team worried it might have to close. A meeting with the San Diego City Credit Union changed the direction. The credit union did not need much merchandise itself, but saw a benefit for its members. CFO Giles Bateman negotiated a group membership that let those individuals shop at slightly higher prices than business members.

Consumers arrived, told one another about the bargains, and brought more business owners into the system too. The hosts treat this as a discovery, not the original plan: ordinary households would accept a rough warehouse experience and large packages in exchange for exceptional value. Growing traffic also attracted local hot dog vendors. In the episode’s account, Sol contacted Hebrew National, which offered both hot dogs and carts, beginning the familiar hot dog-and-soda offer. The hosts are uncertain whether that offer loses money.

Inventory speed made the economics different from FedMart’s. Suppliers generally invoiced when a pallet arrived, with about 30 days to pay; the goods were immediately available for sale. Ben reports that modern Costco turns inventory 12.4 times annually and describes roughly 26–27 days to sell against typical net-30 terms. Those are the episode’s approximate figures, not a guarantee for each product. The point is that customer cash can arrive before the supplier bill comes due without imposing unusually long payment terms on vendors.

Price Club’s shareholder count eventually crossed the threshold requiring public-company filings in 1979. David distinguishes that from an IPO: it did not need to raise new money, and the shares initially traded over the counter. It listed on Nasdaq in 1982. That year Sam Walton visited, learned the model, and soon launched Sam’s Club. The episode also traces Price’s influence on Bernie Marcus and Home Depot. Price shared his ideas even with people who might become competitors.

Explanatory interlude · conceptual model

Savings travel in a circle.

1. Select fewerConcentrate purchases in the products that deserve the floor.
2. Move moreHigher volume per item supports better terms and simpler handling.
3. Share savingsLower prices make the trip and membership more valuable.
4. Earn return visitsMore useful trips reinforce demand and the next renewal.

A schematic of the reinforcing choices discussed by the hosts; not a measured financial model.

05

1982–1993

Brotman recruits Sinegal to build Costco

A rejected franchise proposal becomes a new company led by an experienced operator.

Listening cue · 1:07:39 ↗

In 1982, Seattle retailers Bernie Brotman and his son Jeff asked to open a Price Club franchise in the Northwest. Price Club declined. The Brotmans decided to build their own version, repeating the pattern of Fedco’s earlier refusal to work with Price. Jeff first approached Price Club’s head of merchandising about becoming a co-founder. He declined because Sol was his uncle, but pointed Jeff toward Jim Sinegal, who was then doing retail consulting.

Sinegal moved to Seattle. He and the Brotmans raised $7.5 million in exchange for half the company, and recruited a small team of veterans, largely from FedMart and Price Club. The first Costco opened in Seattle in 1983, followed by Portland and expansion into other markets. The founders were reproducing a format they understood, with people who already knew how to operate it.

The hosts distinguish Price’s creativity from Sinegal’s ability to execute and scale. Sinegal had not merely observed the warehouse model: he had run the FedMart division that inspired it. Costco reached $1 billion in revenue in less than three years and $3 billion in less than six, and went public in 1985. The episode credits disciplined replication and attention to details rather than an entirely new retail invention.

Price Club remained a successful business, but it expanded less aggressively than Costco and Sam’s Club. This time, David says, lack of capital was not the problem: the warehouses’ cash flow and investment payback supported growth. The difference was pace and execution. Sol gradually stepped back, leaving day-to-day management to Robert. Competition made the relationship between the two related companies increasingly consequential.

06

1993

Costco and Price Club join forces

Sinegal becomes the natural operating leader of the combined business.

Listening cue · 1:14:22 ↗

Costco and Price Club combined in 1993 as PriceCostco. The hosts describe the deal as close to a merger of equals: Costco shareholders received 52% of the combined equity and Price Club shareholders 48%. Each business had roughly 100 stores and about $8 billion in revenue, producing a combined business of about $16 billion. Sinegal would run it.

Costco was growing faster, so waiting could have shifted the balance further in its favor. But Sam’s Club was also expanding aggressively. Even combined, the two businesses were only slightly larger than Sam’s Club by stores and revenue. The hosts see both respect for Price and competitive urgency in the timing. Price preferred joining Sinegal to ending up with Walmart.

The merger reunited people and operating ideas that had passed through FedMart and Price Club. Sol’s later years centered increasingly on philanthropy and politics, while Sinegal carried the retail philosophy forward. The episode connects their values closely without treating them as identical managers: its recurring distinction is that Sinegal was the stronger executor.

Costco’s Code of Ethics gives that philosophy a specific order: obey the law, take care of members, take care of employees, and respect suppliers. Shareholder returns are the intended result of doing those things. The hosts emphasize “respect” rather than generosity toward suppliers: Costco negotiates hard, but its enormous volume makes it especially important to be fair. They also recount an early Washington liquor-license review as a reason management learned the practical value of being beyond reproach.

07

The member bargain in 2023

Membership supports a low markup model

The annual fee changes both customer behavior and the economics of merchandise.

Listening cue · 1:18:48 ↗

At the time of the episode, a basic membership cost $60 a year. The hosts explore more than the fee itself. Paying in advance encourages members to use the bargain they have purchased. Membership and bulk buying also select for customers who can afford a larger upfront outlay and have space to store goods. This helps explain the apparent contradiction of exceptionally low unit prices and an affluent customer base.

Ben cites research putting a typical member household’s income near $125,000, versus about $80,000 for Walmart customers and $71,000 for the US median. These are figures discussed in the 2023 episode. The hosts also connect membership to low shrinkage: customers identify with the club, do not want to lose access, and many large packages are hard to steal. Employee loyalty supports the same outcome.

Costco caps product markups at 14% above supplier cost, with a 15% exception for Kirkland Signature. Some categories, including electronics, have lower markups. This is a ceiling, not a statement that every item earns that amount. The episode later discusses an approximately 11% gross margin for the retail business; markup on cost and gross margin on sales are different measures.

Low prices are not simply periodic promotions. The hosts connect the markup cap to Price’s opposition to making shoppers overpay on one product to fund a spectacular bargain on another. Sinegal passed the same philosophy to Jeff Bezos at their 2001 coffee meeting: work to charge customers less. The episode describes Bezos reversing Amazon’s price-increase policy after that conversation.

The fee and the merchandise business reinforce one another, but the hosts reject the slogan that all profit comes from membership and retail only breaks even. Their historical shorthand is roughly 70% of operating income represented by membership fees and 30% from retail, with retail’s share higher in recent years. That is their approximate framing, not a fixed segment split or a claim that fees equal net income. The detailed membership discussion returns in the operations chapter.

08

The 1990s and international expansion

Kirkland and the intelligent loss of sales

A trusted selection makes fewer products an advantage for the member.

Listening cue · 1:33:23 ↗

Kirkland Signature took its name from the Washington city where Costco had been headquartered. David thinks the company had moved to Issaquah by the time the brand launched, but preferred the Kirkland name. A single name also had to work as a trademark across countries as the company expanded internationally. The episode’s story is about a unified member promise, not a series of unrelated store brands.

That promise is good quality and value, not the most technically advanced product in every category. The hosts use a sweatshirt to explain the distinction: it need not equal a premium performance brand to be useful to members. Costco introduces a Kirkland item when it can offer a lower price or a better product than available branded alternatives. Wine, spirits, batteries, and mixed nuts illustrate the range.

By the time of the episode, Costco carried about 3,800 distinct products, or SKUs. A limited assortment gives Kirkland a stronger position than a conventional supermarket house brand competing with many alternatives. In some categories it is one of two or three choices; in others, the only choice. Ben cites $52 billion in annual Kirkland sales excluding gasoline, more than Nike’s revenue at the time. The institution’s purchasing judgment, rather than endless choice, helps members accept that concentration.

The hosts frame low selection as a bet on trust. Buyers have already selected one or two good products at attractive prices, so the member can rely on their work. Price called a related discipline the intelligent loss of sales. His example was carrying an eight-ounce can of lubricating oil while declining to stock a smaller size. Losing shoppers who needed only the small can was acceptable when fewer items improved the whole operation.

International expansion brought the model to the UK, Korea, Taiwan, Japan, and later China. David acknowledges why Japan might have looked unpromising: dense cities and smaller homes differ from American suburbs. But the hosts say the model worked because quality and value remained appealing.

09

The modern warehouse in 2023

Pallets memberships and employee productivity

The logistics and loyalty choices work together, rather than in isolation.

Listening cue · 1:45:45 ↗

Modern Costco needs distribution centers; not every supplier delivers directly to each selling warehouse. Its cross-dock system moves whole pallets from supplier trucks on one side of a depot to Costco trucks on the other. The hosts describe departures within minutes or hours, without unpacking individual boxes or keeping merchandise overnight. Ben reports that about 92% of Costco merchandise uses this system.

Bulk packages and fewer products reduce handling throughout the operation. Employees do not spend the same time opening cartons and arranging individual labels as in a conventional retailer. That productivity helps explain how Costco can pay higher wages while needing fewer workers per dollar of sales. The episode connects this to retention, low shrinkage, and promotion from within, rather than treating higher pay as a detached charitable expense.

The same scale explains why a low margin percentage can still produce substantial profit dollars. The hosts discuss roughly $230 billion in revenue and $7.5 billion in operating income, while emphasizing that the inventory can largely finance itself through normal supplier terms. Buildings and opening a warehouse still require investment. Their argument is about capital efficiency, not the absence of physical assets.

Executive membership adds another layer of loyalty. The episode dates its introduction to 1998 and describes the then-current price as $120, an extra $60 over basic membership. A 2% reward makes that increment break even at $3,000 of eligible annual spending. Ben emphasizes the option to refund the upgrade when it does not deliver value, rather than profiting from members who barely use it.

Ben says Executive members accounted for about 45% of paid memberships worldwide but 73% of sales. He explicitly leaves open how much of the difference is causation versus selection: frequent shoppers may be the people most likely to upgrade. He cites a US renewal rate around 93%, with higher renewal among Executive members and holders of the Costco Citi Visa card. The pattern is a set of voluntary steps toward greater loyalty.

Price Club initially accepted cash or checks instead of absorbing credit-card fees. The hosts argue that proving members would shop on those terms later strengthened Costco’s negotiating position with payment providers. Ben also speculates about income from the Citi/Visa arrangement, while acknowledging that he has not confirmed its economics. That speculation should not be read as a disclosed contract term.

10

The hosts’ concluding analysis

Scale culture and the limits of growth

Seven Powers, vertical integration, and the actual bear and bull cases from the episode.

Listening cue · 2:03:05 ↗

The hosts identify scale economies as the clearest competitive power. They use investor Nick Sleep’s formulation of sharing scale economies with customers: concentrate volume, negotiate better purchasing terms, keep overhead low, pass savings to members, and repeat as membership grows. Management could retain more of the benefit as margin, but the hosts argue that sharing it makes the business more durable.

They also identify process power in a culture and operating system developed over decades. Their counter-positioning argument is specifically against convenience-led commerce such as Amazon: a company delivering a huge assortment to the home cannot readily copy the overhead of a warehouse where members collect bulk purchases. They see some membership switching friction, but little network power and no clear cornered resource.

Brand requires a qualification in their Seven Powers discussion. Members trust Costco’s buyers, and Kirkland has acquired a following of its own. But the company does not use that trust to charge higher prices. Ben calls it latent branding power: the benefit may appear in purchasing volume and renewal rather than excess product margins. Treating this as ordinary premium-pricing brand power would miss their argument.

The treasure hunt adds changing merchandise to dependable staples. An unexpected bargain gives members another reason to visit and buy immediately, because it may disappear. The hosts also discuss Costco-specific product bundles and its role as a place where suppliers can sell discounted goods without presenting them exactly as they do through other channels. These choices depend on the warehouse retaining members’ trust in quality and value.

Low overhead does not mean refusing every complicated activity. The hosts say Costco vertically integrates when member value justifies the added work. With few major chicken processors, Costco first learned by renting the full capacity of an Alabama plant, then built its own facility in Fremont, Nebraska and worked with local farmers. Ben cites two million chickens processed there weekly. The purpose in his account is to improve value and keep supplier pricing competitive, not a general rule that every product should be made in-house.

Their management discussion centers on continuity and modest operating habits. Ben describes Kirkland coffee and water at headquarters and executives working in cubicles. Leaders built careers inside the business, many beginning in hourly jobs. The hosts connect that accumulated experience to consistent execution and the company’s willingness to leave substantial value with members and employees while still producing long-term shareholder returns.

The bear discussion starts with slow adoption of e-commerce. Ben calls it a miss that happened to work out well, not an entirely deliberate master plan. Matching the delivery infrastructure of Amazon or Walmart would add overhead that Costco’s margin structure cannot easily absorb. A second limit is physical growth: opening buildings, training the right people, expanding suppliers, and moving pallets cannot scale as instantly as software. Cash alone cannot remove those constraints.

The hosts consider and largely reject the idea that young consumers will abandon Costco. They cite TikTok enthusiasm and membership uptake consistent with earlier generations. On the bull side, they see a widening operating advantage and room for more warehouses both in North America and abroad. Adding a closer store can make the offer more useful even in a city that already has Costcos. They highlight China’s demand while acknowledging the complications of operating there.

E-commerce also appears in the bull case, but in a form suited to Costco. A $1 billion acquisition created Costco Logistics for difficult deliveries such as sheds and large appliances. Costco Next directs members to partner websites for discounts, adding value without Costco handling those orders’ logistics itself. The hosts’ final bull case is the culture’s ability to survive individual CEOs and economic cycles. Their enthusiasm rests on the coordinated system continuing to work, not on a claim that growth has no limits.

The hosts’ operating themes

The Costco playbook

  1. 01 · Legal clients lead to retail ideasPrice’s clients exposed him to the locker-club model and Fedco’s buying power.
  2. 02 · Values require operating tradeoffsCustomer value, employee pay, and honest dealing come before investor returns in Price’s priority order.
  3. 03 · Growth capital and control are differentThe hosts describe Price selling majority control while expecting a growth partner.
  4. 04 · A consumer market emerges from a business planCredit-union group memberships unlocked consumer traffic and word of mouth.
  5. 05 · An experienced team can replicate the formatSinegal and the Brotmans assembled veterans who already understood warehouse operations.
  6. 06 · Competition creates urgencySam’s Club’s growth helped bring Costco and Price Club together.
  7. 07 · Put limits on transaction markupMembership and a markup cap align the recurring relationship with lower prices.
  8. 08 · Accept the intelligent loss of salesTrust in buyers and fewer choices support volume, even when some shoppers need a different item or size.
  9. 09 · Align the logistics and loyalty choicesWhole pallets, rapid inventory movement, Executive rewards, and retention reinforce the model.
  10. 10 · Share scale and expand selectivelyThe hosts favor member value, physical operating discipline, and e-commerce that fits Costco’s strengths.
