Spring 2023 · Acquired · Season 13, episode 1

Nike

The shape of a dream

A runner began by selling someone else’s shoes. The company he built would change what people wanted to wear—and why.

A reading companion to Acquired, with Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal

Released July 25, 2023Created UnpublishedAbout 30 minutes to read

The full episode’s history and closing analysis, through its 2023 perspective. Official corrections are incorporated. Chapter navigation follows the story; no passage timestamps are asserted.

01

1948–1963

A runner with a different kind of ambition

Before the swoosh, there was a coach who kept taking shoes apart.

Bill Bowerman became the University of Oregon’s head track coach in 1948. A veteran with a formidable personality, he pursued advantages wherever he could find them. That included rest, which was less conventional in training than it sounds today, and equipment. He taught himself enough shoemaking to dismantle and rebuild the footwear his runners wore. Lighter materials might save weight; different construction might improve performance. Phil Knight, a middle-distance runner who was not at the front of Bowerman’s extraordinary team, became one of his test pilots.

Knight’s assessment of himself as merely an okay runner needs context: he could run a 4-minute, 13-second mile. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal see the same tension in the future founder’s personality. He was intensely competitive but profoundly introverted. David recalls a nervous Knight delivering an early version of Shoe Dog to a warmly receptive Stanford audience. The public image of the enormous brand obscures the private, driven person who assembled it.

At Oregon, Knight studied business, as Acquired’s official correction specifies; the conversation incorrectly calls him a journalism major. His father published the Oregon Journal, and photography provided an analogy for Knight’s final business-school paper at Stanford. German companies had dominated professional cameras until Japanese makers delivered good products at much lower prices. Athletic footwear was also dominated by German firms, especially Adidas and Puma. Could Japanese running shoes follow the same route?

Knight graduated from Stanford in 1962. In the hosts’ chronology, a subsequent trip around the world took him through Hawaii and on to Japan. There he watched runners and decided that Onitsuka’s Tiger shoes offered the best opportunity. He traveled to Kobe to ask the manufacturer for American distribution rights. The company he claimed to represent, Blue Ribbon Sports, was scarcely more than an idea. His proposed billion-dollar American running-shoe market was an unsupported pitch, not a researched estimate.

Even the name’s origin remains unsettled. Knight tells a childhood story about blue ribbons won in track; other accounts connect it to drinking and a Blue Ribbon brand. The hosts refuse to settle the competing versions. What mattered commercially was the meeting: Onitsuka agreed to send samples after receiving $50. Knight asked his father to wire the money, returned home, and waited. The shoes did not arrive for almost a year. He went to work as an accountant while the idea remained unresolved.

02

1963–1967

Fifty-one percent of a much better company

A coach’s involvement changed what the distributor could promise.

When the samples arrived near the end of 1963, Knight thought the shoes were good enough, and cheap enough, for his plan to work. Onitsuka gave him the western thirteen states; another distributor had the East. With no money for a substantial shop or wholesale inventory, Knight drove to track meets and sold shoes from his car. A man who had struggled to sell encyclopedias and stocks could sell something he understood and believed in.

He brought the shoes to Bowerman, hoping his old coach would buy them for Oregon. Bowerman wanted more: a place in the business. They ultimately agreed that Knight would own 51 percent and Bowerman 49 percent, each contributing $500. David describes the choice as keeping all of Phil Knight’s Blue Ribbon Sports or owning 51 percent of Bill Bowerman’s Blue Ribbon Sports. The latter had a very different standing with runners.

Bowerman also had reasons to say yes. As Ben explains through correspondence discussed in the episode, the coach had spent years trying to buy directly from manufacturers and interest them in his designs. Adidas had directed him back to American distributors and ignored his offer of product ideas. Blue Ribbon supplied both access to shoes and a route for experimentation. His contribution would be intermittent, but consequential.

The business sold $8,000 in 1964 and $16,000 in 1965. Its early economics explain why doubling sales did not create financial ease. A pair sold for $6.95 and cost roughly $3.50 landed. Once a sales representative received about $1.75, the remainder could not finance another $3.50 pair and support an expanding business at the same time. Profit existed, but cash was tied up in the next shipment.

Jeff Johnson, the first full-time employee, brought an almost unreasonable commitment to running. He sold from his car in California, designed shoes, opened an early Los Angeles store, and later moved across the country to help establish production. These were not separate departments. They were jobs an early employee kept taking on because they needed doing. The hosts emphasize how unusual his enthusiasm was in a country where motorists still mocked recreational runners and sometimes threw drinks at them.

Knight returned to accounting at Price Waterhouse rather than draw a salary that would slow inventory purchases. Later he taught accounting at Portland State. There he met Penny Parks, whom he hired as a bookkeeper and later married, and art student Carolyn Davidson, whom he engaged at $2 an hour for design work. Neither encounter looked like the beginning of a global marketing institution.

03

1967–1970

The market learns to run

Blue Ribbon helped supply a movement that Bowerman helped explain.

Bowerman’s product ideas began changing the relationship with Onitsuka. Ahead of the Mexico City Olympics, he proposed a breathable nylon upper in place of leather. Sweat made leather heavier, and weight was an obsession. Onitsuka could manufacture the design. What remained unclear was who owned it: had Blue Ribbon commissioned a product, or had a distributor merely suggested an improvement to its supplier?

The shoe became the Cortez. An earlier Aztec name ran too close to Adidas’ Azteca Gold; in the story the hosts recount, Bowerman asked for the name of the Spaniard who defeated the Aztecs. Naming aside, the new construction sold. The familiar later Nike silhouette began life with the Tiger design on its side, a reminder that Nike’s product inheritance preceded its own brand.

Bowerman was also widening the definition of a customer. After encountering recreational running in New Zealand, he organized jogging groups in Eugene and wrote Jogging: A Physical Fitness Program for All Ages, published in 1967. Life profiled the coach and the groups. His conviction was simple: people did not need to be elite competitors to count as athletes. Knight initially wondered who would want to read a book about jogging.

The hosts resist choosing between Nike creating the fitness wave and merely riding it. Bowerman helped popularize the activity, while Blue Ribbon’s growing network could put shoes on the new participants’ feet. Knight’s later formulation is more modest than claiming to have invented the movement: the company was there and rode it. By 1970, annual sales exceeded half a million dollars, and Cortezes sold as quickly as shipments arrived.

A renewed Onitsuka agreement promised three more years of supply, through 1973. Knight hoped that a real business and a multiyear contract would persuade his bankers to provide a $1.2 million credit line. Instead, the request alarmed them. The Oregon banks treated growth faster than the company’s equity as a danger. Knight treated growth as survival. He kept borrowing against almost everything available, selling inventory, repaying the bank, and borrowing again.

A proposed local offering, dressed up with the technology-sounding Sports-Tek name, failed to attract the hoped-for investors. Families of employees supplied money instead. Bob Woodell, another former Oregon runner who used a wheelchair after a college accident, became a crucial early leader; his family lent a large share of their modest resources. Some such loans later became equity. The hosts celebrate the eventual outcome while insisting on survivorship bias: other businesses using this much leverage disappeared.

04

1971–1972

A new name on a broken bridge

The logo came before the goddess—and neither began as the company’s name.

Knight’s escape from the credit ceiling came through Nissho Iwai, a Japanese trading company with a Portland office. The hosts describe a hybrid of financier and supply-chain partner. It knew manufacturers, could investigate them, and could make money financing an importer’s purchases. Tom Sumeragi offered to finance Blue Ribbon’s inventory at a scale the regional banks could not approach.

Onitsuka opposed the relationship. A trading company could introduce Knight to factories and make the Tiger supplier unnecessary. Onitsuka sought other American distributors and offered to acquire control of Blue Ribbon, reportedly at book value. Knight stalled rather than surrender the company or cut off the supply he still needed. The separation was not a clean exercise in contract management: the hosts recount a spy and Knight copying documents taken from an Onitsuka executive’s briefcase.

Meanwhile, he approached a Mexican factory for soccer cleats that Blue Ribbon could sell as American football shoes. Knight believed the restriction on importing competing track-and-field shoes left an opening. The factory needed a side design. Davidson drew alternatives, first with a structural-support requirement that was later dropped. Under time pressure, Knight and colleagues chose the checkmark-like mark he did not love but thought might grow on him. She charged $35.

The order of events matters. The mark was not inspired by a wing of the Greek goddess Nike: the name came afterward. The factory then needed a model name for the cleats. Knight favored Dimension Six; other suggestions failed to settle the matter. In the account the hosts regard as credible, Johnson arrived with Nike, the goddess of victory, after a dream. They were naming a shoe model, rather than deliberately unveiling a new corporate identity.

Those first cleats cracked in cold weather. The hosts distinguish that failure from a blanket claim of a poor factory: shoes made in a warm climate had not been tested for freezing American football conditions. The company nevertheless crossed into making its own line. Onitsuka stopped supplying Tigers, and Nissho financed the replacement while helping arrange direct factory relationships. The deal combined market-rate interest with a 4 percent shoe-sales royalty; the hosts are uncertain about its exact scope and how later terms evolved.

At Nippon Rubber, Knight asked how long a Cortez-like shoe would take. After lunch, the factory had produced an almost exact sample. He ordered running shoes, tennis shoes and basketball models, including names that endured, and asked for bright orange boxes. Nike Inc. began as a subsidiary of Blue Ribbon Sports in 1971. A separate manufacturing identity had emerged from the very relationship designed to supply another company’s product.

Davidson’s later reward should follow the official correction: she received 500 shares in 1983, not at the IPO. There were six subsequent stock splits by the episode’s timeframe, not the seven used in the hosts’ on-air calculation. The corrected chronology means their roughly $7 million calculation should not be carried forward as stated.

05

1972–1974

The sole, the runner, and the six-month order

A new brand had to prove that trust would survive a change of supplier.

With factories ready to make what he designed, Bowerman found inspiration at breakfast. His wife Barbara’s waffle iron suggested a sole that might grip the new artificial track at Oregon. Pouring polyurethane into it glued the first iron shut. Later molds made the idea practical. The hosts treat the breakfast story as supported, including by the eventual recovery of the discarded iron, rather than as a convenient corporate fable.

The waffle sole worked on artificial tracks and on Astroturf. Oregon’s football team wore the shoes, supplying conspicuous publicity. Blue versions with yellow markings also went with jeans. Performance equipment was beginning to travel into ordinary life. In 1972, the first full year selling the independent Nike line, revenue reached $3.2 million. Customers had followed the distributor and Bowerman, rather than insisting on the Tiger name.

Steve Prefontaine supplied another extraordinary convergence. Bowerman’s Oregon runner became the face of American distance running and held American records across distances from 2,000 to 10,000 meters. Amateur rules prevented straightforward paid endorsements, leaving an elite athlete working to support himself. Nike employed him as national director of public affairs at $5,000 a year. Knight’s explanation of the job, as quoted by the hosts, was essentially that he could run fast. Prefontaine’s early death in a car crash made his place in the story tragic as well as foundational.

The hosts approve of supporting the athlete against the amateur system, but place the maneuver inside a broader culture willing to push rules. That culture was not harmless in every setting. They later connect the same relentless competitiveness to conduct Nike deserved criticism for. It is a recurring qualification, rather than a license to treat every boundary crossed as justified by the eventual success.

Knight also introduced the Futures Program. Retailers willing to commit and pay six months ahead could receive a 7 percent discount. They initially resisted; demand for the waffle trainer made access valuable enough to change their minds. Financing shifted toward customers, and advance commitments made planning less precarious. This helped establish the wholesale relationships Nike would spend decades building—and eventually try to reduce.

The combination of product, athlete association, financing and outsourced production carried sales to $8 million in 1974. That year the Onitsuka litigation ended with Blue Ribbon winning and ultimately receiving a $400,000 settlement. More significant than the money was the lawyer the case brought into Knight’s orbit: Rob Strasser. The court battle over a supplier relationship had introduced the person who would transform Nike’s approach to demand.

06

1974–1980

Putting the swoosh where people look

Rob Strasser turned isolated endorsements into a system.

Strasser joined as in-house counsel, but Knight quickly found a wider use for him. A large man with an equally forceful personality, nicknamed Rolling Thunder, he differed from the former runners who filled the early company. Nike had already paid tennis player Ilie Năstase $10,000 to wear its shoes. Strasser made such deals systematic, signing NBA role players at modest cost so the mark appeared night after night on television.

He hired Sonny Vaccaro to build a college-basketball program. Rather than paying amateur players, Nike could pay their coaches as consultants and clinic participants. Coaches could then direct their teams to wear the shoes. The network grew rapidly through prominent programs, and the strategy moved into college football. A Washington Post article that mistakenly listed Iowa instead of Iona even prompted Iowa coach Lute Olson to call and ask to join.

This explains an otherwise puzzling expense. Most spectators would never buy football cleats. The athlete or team did not have to sell its exact equipment to justify sponsorship. It could make Nike mean authentic athletic performance, and a customer could buy that association in a running shoe worn with jeans. The hosts describe running, basketball and tennis as especially valuable because their shoes also work in daily life. The broader roster created impressions for the whole brand.

The product work continued. In 1977, former NASA engineer Frank Rudy brought air-sole technology to Knight. Knight was skeptical until the mention that Adidas had rejected it helped capture his attention. A test run persuaded him. Strasser negotiated a royalty of roughly 10–20 cents per pair using the technology; Nike later bought Rudy’s company. The cushioning was a nitrogen-filled bag inside the midsole, not merely a marketing use of the word air.

Strasser also typed a blunt set of ten principles that circulated internally. The hosts correct the mythology around later internet reproductions: the author was Strasser, not Knight, and the decorative wispy swoosh was not on the original. They admire the insistence on change, offense and finishing the job, but Ben rereads the language more uneasily after learning the company’s history. Fighting rules, personal ambition and treating resources as there to be taken have consequences as well as energy. The memo is evidence of a particular culture, rather than a timeless official values poster.

Revenue rose from $14 million to $29 million to about $71 million, reaching $150 million in 1979. Yet the commercial engine remained overwhelmingly running shoes, largely for men. The official company name became Nike in 1976. By its December 1980 IPO, in the same week as Apple’s, Nike had a market value of roughly $400 million and raised about $22 million. Knight owned 46 percent after the offering. Financial arrival coincided with a dangerous confidence that yesterday’s market would remain tomorrow’s.

07

1980–1984

The fitness boom changes clothes

Owning running did not mean understanding every athlete.

Nike had reached about half the American running-shoe market. Knight took a sabbatical, and the company could finally operate without the old capital starvation. The difficulty was that it had confused one part of a broader movement with the whole movement. Fitness continued growing; running no longer supplied the same extraordinary expansion.

Aerobics brought a different customer, especially women, and a different desired product. Reebok’s American business, led by Paul Fireman, sold soft white leather shoes that looked right with the period’s exercise clothes. Nike regarded them as technically inadequate and declined to take the category seriously. The hosts describe hubris rather than the sudden disappearance of runners. A company could retain its existing customers and still miss the growth that determined the industry’s future.

Reebok would pass Nike in sales by 1988. At the same time, Adidas became a tougher bidder for sports sponsorships. The cheap exposure Nike had assembled was no longer so easy to obtain. Futures orders, committed well in advance, cushioned reported revenue, leaving only a few quarters of decline. They could not repair the deteriorating demand underneath.

Knight acknowledged 1984 as a bad year in his shareholder letter. Nike needed another route into the culture, and a young basketball player from North Carolina offered one. Michael Jordan was drafted third, not universally understood as the inevitable transcendent figure he later became. Signing him was a substantial wager. The hosts insist that the entertaining film Air is unreliable about how it happened: Strasser was central to structuring the deal, and Vaccaro did not make the dramatized home negotiation with Jordan’s mother.

Jordan preferred Adidas. Nike had neither the prestige he wanted nor the leverage to dictate ordinary terms. That weakness created room for an agreement the incumbent brands would struggle to match. For the hosts, the story’s importance lies in the incentives and uncertainty of that contract, rather than in hindsight that makes finding Michael Jordan sound obvious.

08

1984–1987

Giving away upside to create a dream

The first Air Jordan deal was both a breakthrough and a source of new trouble.

Nike guaranteed Jordan at least $2.5 million over five years, or $500,000 annually, while paying a 5 percent royalty on the Jordan line’s gross sales. The guarantee functioned like an advance against royalties. Nike also committed advertising support and stock options. The episode describes additional participation beyond an existing sales baseline; its official correction broadens the scope from basketball to all shoes in the Nike Air line. That correction matters to understanding how far the halo economics extended.

This was not an equal sharing of financial downside. Nike supplied the guarantee while Jordan participated in the upside. Ben challenges the easy use of the word partnership on that basis. The conversation also notes performance protections for Nike, but the hosts work through the precise award conditions uncertainly. The stronger supported point is that extraordinary economics were tied to expectations of an extraordinary player. Jordan even took Nike’s offer back to Adidas, which would offer only around $100,000 annually. He chose Nike reluctantly.

The structure was a historical counterpositioning move in the hosts’ analysis. Converse already had Magic Johnson and Larry Bird on far smaller flat-payment arrangements. Giving Jordan dramatically better upside risked destabilizing those relationships. Nike, with less to protect, could offer a signature franchise and ask its athlete to help build it.

Demand also needed a story. Jordan wore black-and-red modified Air Ships while the actual Air Jordan 1 was still being developed. The NBA objected to shoes outside its color rules. Nike turned that objection into the famous banned-shoe advertisement, visually covering the footwear with black bars and inviting customers to buy what the league would not allow. Ben could not verify which fines were actually paid, if any, nor find proof of the black-and-red precursor being worn in a regular game. The familiar claim that Nike paid $5,000 for every game is not established here.

First-year Air Jordan shoes and merchandise produced $126 million in sales, against an internal goal of $3 million over three years. At 5 percent, that implies $6.3 million for Jordan, matching the stated total of his seven-year Bulls contract. But David immediately complicates the triumph: roughly $150 million across the first three years implies a severe subsequent slump. Nike had pushed too much product into the channel; first-year wholesale revenue exceeded actual consumer demand. David’s guesses about the exact underlying demand are explicitly guesses.

Jordan then broke his foot, missing much of his second season. The expensive, Italian-made Air Jordan 2 was stiff and did not fit his preferences. Strasser and designer Peter Moore had also set up an increasingly independent new-products operation. Their authority collided with Knight’s. Nike’s dual-class structure left control with Knight; Strasser would not displace him. In 1987, Strasser left, taking Moore with him. An apparent rescue of the business had become a product, inventory and personal crisis.

09

1987–2003

The shoe he asked for

Keeping Jordan required listening—and making room for another identity.

Strasser and Moore formed Sports Incorporated. Adidas became its major client, ultimately acquired it, and made Strasser head of Adidas America in Portland. His sudden death only months into that job gave the split a painful finality. Knight regarded working for Adidas as an unforgivable betrayal and did not attend the funeral. The hosts restore Strasser’s indispensable role without smoothing away the relationship’s end.

Jordan, whose strongest Nike relationships had just left, was also considering leaving. Knight turned to Tinker Hatfield, an Oregon athlete trained as an architect who had joined Nike in building design before moving into footwear. Hatfield and Mark Parker had worked on the Air Max and Air Trainer, part of the company’s product recovery. Hatfield approached Jordan as an architect would approach a client: what did he want, and what was wrong with the last shoe?

The answers were specific. Jordan wanted soft leather that did not require painful breaking in, less weight than a high top, support, and a shoe that looked good off the court without sacrificing play. Hatfield returned with the Air Jordan 3. A mid-cut design, softer materials and elephant-print details addressed the requests. Most strikingly, the Jumpman occupied the main branding position. The swoosh receded to the back.

For a company spending heavily to put its mark on athletes, that was a substantial concession. The hosts return to the Bowerman ownership comparison: a smaller claim on something more valuable can be worth far more than complete control of something ordinary. Nike renegotiated Jordan’s agreement, extending it for seven years with a guarantee of at least $18 million and, as David understands it, the same 5 percent royalty. Sales rapidly made the guarantee secondary.

The Jordan identity developed into an increasingly independent brand, with its own products, advertising and later athletes. Spike Lee’s campaigns helped animate the story, while ESPN and SportsCenter provided repeated exposure far beyond a magazine cover. The 1988 Just Do It campaign accompanied a broader recovery. Jordan products were both revenue and a pinnacle that helped Nike sell less expensive goods, as a luxury trunk can help sell a wallet.

The following years were not an uninterrupted ascent. Labor controversies damaged the company; the period around the dot-com crash was difficult. Kobe Bryant began with Adidas, then moved to Nike after buying out his deal—the reported $8 million amount is a rumor in the hosts’ account. Nike signed LeBron James in 2003 and bought Converse for $309 million. Jordan retired that year, when his brand already generated about $700 million annually. By the recording, the hosts put it at $6.6 billion and recently growing roughly 35 percent a year. Those are the episode’s period figures, and the continuing roughly 5 percent royalty is an approximation, not a newly verified contract.

10

1970s–1990s, revisited

The costs outside the company

The outsourced model did not outsource responsibility.

The hosts pause the original chronology to follow the manufacturing system’s consequences. As Japan became more expensive and the yen began floating against the dollar, Nike looked elsewhere for production. Taiwan and South Korea came first, followed by a wider Asian network including China, Indonesia and Vietnam. Knight’s original paper had proposed buying from lower-cost labor markets; global outsourcing made the expanding product range and price points possible.

Ben and David acknowledge the argument that export manufacturing contributed to countries’ economic development. They do not treat that argument as an answer to child labor, hazardous chemicals or exploitative conditions. In the 1990s, reports of such practices became a profound reputational crisis. Nike initially answered that it did not make the shoes. Technically separate factories could not make that defense persuasive to customers who had bought into a promise of greatness.

The hosts call Nike wrong to have turned a blind eye and to have prioritized margin so far above worker conditions. The brand’s inspirational standing made the betrayal more acute. Other apparel companies’ use of similar suppliers was no defense. Nike’s prominence, and the values it represented, raised the expectation that it would take responsibility.

They describe subsequent standards for factory partners, published supplier lists, third-party audits and investment in less toxic glue shared with competitors. Mark Parker’s later position was that ignorance was not bliss: the company had to understand systemic problems and work with its partners. The episode also leaves a difficult boundary question unresolved. Even where conditions are better than workers’ available alternatives, they may remain conditions customers would not accept for themselves. There is a spectrum of decisions, not a single comforting threshold beyond the clearest abuses.

This qualification belongs beside the story of operational ingenuity. Outsourcing was both a strength and the source of a major vulnerability. The same applies to the company’s willingness to push boundaries. Ben later uses Breaking2, Nike’s staged attempt at a sub-two-hour marathon with nonstandard pacing arrangements, as an example of a compelling spectacle outside ordinary competition rules. He also points to the Oregon Project’s doping and alleged abuse controversies as the darker side of an obsession with winning. The hosts do not ask the reader to confuse those very different outcomes.

11

2005–2023

Trying to own the relationship again

Technology brought Nike closer to its customers and into a difficult distribution change.

Tim Cook’s arrival on the board and the 2006 Nike+iPod product marked an early step toward direct digital relationships. The small shoe sensor and iPod connector were awkward, and the product itself was not a major commercial success in the hosts’ telling. Its significance was organizational learning. FuelBand and then mobile apps extended the idea that Nike could participate in a customer’s running or training beyond the moment a retailer sold a pair of shoes.

The acquisition logic changed too. Earlier Nike had bought brands such as Converse, Cole Haan and Starter. It later divested several and sought technology capabilities that could strengthen the central Nike business. By the recording, Ben describes four major apps—Run Club, Training Club, SNKRS and Nike’s shopping app—with roughly 500 million quarterly users. The number is the hosts’ aggregate description, not a claim of unique people across services.

Around 2013–2014, the company intensified a direct-to-consumer strategy. Nike.com and Nike’s own stores could capture the relationship that wholesale partners had historically held. Nike had the scale to attempt a long transition, much as the hosts compare Disney’s move toward direct distribution. Customization also depended on operating a system large and flexible enough to offer individual designs alongside standard lines.

Sneaker culture revealed how much value the products could carry beyond their official price. The hosts cite widely varying estimates of a roughly $2–6 billion resale market. Nike generally neither operated that marketplace nor charged what the most coveted shoes could command there. David interprets this as a deliberate choice to keep the dream attainable for young tastemakers rather than restricting it to wealthy customers. He labels the explanation as interpretation; Ben remains surprised Nike does not capture even a modestly higher price on ordinary premium models.

The 2018 Colin Kaepernick Dream Crazy campaign supplied another example of halo over product. An athlete who had not recently played football became the center of a message about conviction and sacrifice. The response led Nike to reorganize the year’s broader advertising plans. Ben thinks such decisions come from executive and creative judgment about values, rather than a precise calculation of customers won and lost. That is his reading of the company, not access to an internal formula.

Other events complicated the inspirational story. Executive misconduct issues removed a presumed successor to Parker, and the Oregon Project generated further controversy. John Donahoe, previously at Bain, eBay and ServiceNow and already a Nike director, arrived as an outsider to the footwear profession. Covid coincided with a reorganization toward men’s, women’s and kids’ categories. That made coordination with retail buyers simpler. It also raised the question that would dominate the hosts’ closing bear case: who would retain obsessive responsibility for the athlete within each sport?

12

The hosts’ closing analysis · 2023

A brand whose power comes from scale

The swoosh matters, but the hosts resist an easy explanation of its economics.

At the recording, Nike was a roughly $51 billion revenue business growing about 10 percent annually. Approximately 60 percent still went through wholesale and 40 percent directly to consumers. Ben gives a gross margin around 44 percent and operating income of $6.4 billion, or about 12.5 percent of revenue. Those distinctions matter: revenue is not profit, and a globally admired brand is not automatically a luxury-margin or software-margin business.

He also cites about $8.5 billion in inventory, unusually high even for Nike, and $10.7 billion in cash and equivalents. The company once forced to pledge every available asset now had ample cash and increasing dividends. Footwear remained nearly three times apparel by revenue. Women’s sales were substantial enough to exceed Lululemon’s total, yet the hosts still saw Nike as relatively underdeveloped in serving women. Their wholesale-equivalent category figures should not be read as the same thing as actual reported channel revenue.

In Seven Powers terms, they converge most firmly on scale economies. A larger customer base can support larger athlete contracts, advertising and events. Athletes can participate in a larger commercial machine, not simply collect a bigger flat check. More compelling athlete associations reinforce demand, which funds the next round. David compares athletes to Netflix shows: audiences follow their performance and lives, and Nike obtains attention by aligning with them. Scale also helps secure capacity with major audited footwear factories.

Brand is the productive disagreement. Ben asks why Nike does not visibly charge more than Adidas for equivalent shoes and notes that Adidas’ gross margin was higher. A famous identity is not identical to the pure price premium illustrated by a Tiffany ring. David thinks Nike chooses accessibility; Ben thinks ubiquity and growth may be worth more to it than harvesting each available margin dollar. Neither claims to have settled the explanation. David’s argument that the brand could not be killed meets Ben’s explicit disagreement. Their unsupported swoosh-tattoo comparison is enthusiasm, not data.

They reject strong switching costs or network effects. Customers can change shoes, and another person wearing Nike does not mechanically improve a buyer’s product. Historical counterpositioning helped the Jordan deal, but the clearest continuing power is the ability to spread large demand-creation costs across immense sales. Nike spent roughly $4 billion annually on that activity in the period they discuss.

The manufacturing analogy is a “fabless” shoe company: Nike specifies products and materials while outside factories assemble them. The hosts identify an important exception: Nike makes the nitrogen-filled air bags itself in Oregon to protect the trade secret, then sends them to the shoe factories. They do not claim that every other technology follows the same arrangement.

The product-versus-marketing question remains deliberately untidy. David distinguishes genuinely differentiated prestige products, such as breakthrough racing shoes, from ordinary shoes supported by the halo. Ben cites the Monarch as an unglamorous volume product, far from elite competition. Knight’s formulation is that Nike is a marketing company whose product is its most important marketing tool. Ben disagrees about which tool comes first, putting athletes at the center. David adds that even a story about a bad shoe and an athlete’s departure can become another episode in the celebrity narrative. Product quality matters, and so does the way its story is told.

Celebrity attention also carries liability. Ben contrasts Nike’s focus on celebrating athletes with Adidas’ broader pursuit of people who attract eyeballs, including musicians. He interprets Nike’s refusal to grant Kanye West the creative control he wanted as consistent with that athlete-centered identity. He explicitly stops short of saying Nike foresaw the precise crisis that later devastated Adidas’ Yeezy business. The hosts see the ability to participate in music and culture through athletes as another advantage, while acknowledging that sponsor relationships can do severe harm when they go wrong.

Their final bull case is that the scale, media skill and cultural meaning remain formidable even when an individual product falls behind. Basketball and sneakerhead culture look particularly strong to David. The bear case is more specific than declaring the brand finished: a category-based reorganization may weaken attention to running, while Brooks, On and Hoka serve those niches well. Renewed inventory at Macy’s and Foot Locker raises questions about whether direct distribution is advancing as promised or excess stock is being cleared. China presents slowing demand, stronger domestic brands and a difficult fit between Nike’s public values and political constraints.

Ben’s suggestion that Donahoe might be a stabilizing rather than twenty-year CEO is explicitly a personal impression. David’s final reservation is structural: an excellent physical-product business still carries materials, labor and logistics costs. The episode ends its business argument with durable advantages and open questions, rather than a guarantee of inevitability. Its incidental trivia and closing recommendations return the hosts to other stories and their next episode; the central Nike question remains how to keep serving athletes closely while operating the biggest machine.

Revisit the hosts’ argument

Where does the advantage come from?

More customers support larger athlete and media commitments. Those commitments can reinforce demand across a broad product range. Both hosts identify scale economies as Nike’s clearest continuing power.
The identity is powerful; the price premium is less clear. David favors an accessibility explanation. Ben questions why equivalent Nike shoes do not command visibly higher prices. The episode leaves this unresolved.
No strong switching costs or network effects protect the shoes. Physical costs constrain margins, and scale does not eliminate product mistakes, athlete risk, inventory problems or loss of focus on a sport.
