Building blocks for the internet

A retailer’s struggle to move faster helped create a new way to buy computing—and one of the world’s most consequential infrastructure businesses.

01 · 2000–2002

A bookstore with an infrastructure problem

The familiar spare-server story explains too little.

At the turn of the millennium, Amazon had a demanding retail business and a fragile financial position. It had to keep adding products and customers while controlling the cost of running a large website. The hosts describe a move away from expensive proprietary servers toward Linux and different hardware as part of the effort to survive. But that infrastructure change was not yet Amazon Web Services. It did not turn Amazon’s machines into a general-purpose cloud overnight.

Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal begin by rejecting the most convenient explanation of AWS: that Amazon simply rented out servers left idle outside the holiday season. A retail application installed on a company’s own machines is not automatically a secure, metered service for strangers. And an outside customer needs its application to work in December, too. The hosts cite Werner Vogels explicitly calling the excess-capacity story a myth. AWS was intended to be a business of its own.

The more useful beginning is a set of overlapping efforts. In early 2002, Tim O’Reilly encouraged Jeff Bezos to embrace a web in which developers could use other companies’ services through application programming interfaces. Amazon already had affiliates linking to its product catalog. Giving them programmatic access to images and product information could help both sides sell more, without negotiating a separate partnership for every application.

Amazon launched a developer-facing business called Amazon Web Services in 2002. The episode describes an early developer conference with just eight attendees and the operation sitting within Amazon Associates under Colin Bryar. This was a real origin of the name and the developer relationship. It was not the launch of the cloud infrastructure business people now mean by AWS. The hosts keep that distinction central: exposing a retail catalog and selling computing infrastructure are different things.

02 · 2002–2003

The company becomes a set of services

A way to move faster inside Amazon suggested something to sell outside it.

Amazon’s growing software organization was struggling with a shared, tightly coupled codebase. A team making a change could break another team’s work, and new features required more coordination. Holiday code freezes helped protect the site but limited the company’s ability to ship. The problem was technical and organizational: more people did not automatically produce more progress when everyone depended on everyone else’s implementation details.

Andy Jassy arrived at this problem from an unusual direction. A Harvard MBA who had helped launch product categories, he was not a distinguished engineer chosen to solve infrastructure. When the post-bubble retrenchment threatened his marketing role, Bezos kept him as his technical assistant, or “shadow.” Jassy spent that period alongside Bezos as the company confronted its inability to move quickly. The assignment became a bridge from category management to a very different business.

Rather than respond only with more coordination, Amazon sought to reduce the need for it. Teams would expose functionality through documented service interfaces instead of reaching directly into one another’s databases or code. The hosts recount Steve Yegge’s later description of Bezos’s API mandate, including the requirement that interfaces be designed so they could eventually be offered externally. That retrospective account conveys the force of the change; the transition itself was a multiyear effort, not a memo that instantly rebuilt the company.

Infrastructure had to follow the same logic. Independent teams could not all wait for a central group to predict their server needs months in advance. They needed a pool of resources that could be requested as a service. Amazon’s internal problems therefore helped reveal an external possibility: other developers were spending time on the same undifferentiated work. A service that let them obtain storage and computing without building the machinery themselves might be valuable well beyond Amazon.

03 · 2003–2006

Success has several origin stories

The executive proposal and the engineers’ work do not collapse into one clean invention.

In the official company account discussed by the hosts, Jassy put together a six-page proposal in 2003 for a broader infrastructure business. He requested 57 people and, unable to fit financial projections into the document, worked through them at the meeting. Bezos and the leadership approved the effort. Jassy went on to recruit a team, including Adam Selipsky and Jeff Lawson, and to build the organization that would make the business real.

Another account begins with network engineer Benjamin Black and his boss Chris Pinkham. In 2003 they wrote about restructuring Amazon’s infrastructure and the possibility of selling virtual servers to outside developers. The hosts cannot establish exactly how that document and Jassy’s proposal intersected. They resist presenting either as the one uncontested moment of invention. An executive vision and a technical proposal could both matter within a decentralized company.

Pinkham wanted to return to South Africa. Amazon arranged a Cape Town operation, where he and Chris Brown worked on the service that became EC2. The hosts discuss the tensions around credit for this work, including Jassy’s insistence that the broader AWS proposal already included compute. Their conclusion is that an institution designed to let teams invent should expect several contributions. The thousands of decisions required to build and operate the service matter more than claiming ownership of a one-sentence idea.

The launch sequence is clearer than the ownership dispute. Simple Storage Service, S3, launched in March 2006. Elastic Compute Cloud, EC2, entered beta that August. CloudFront, the content-delivery service, followed in 2008, and Relational Database Service, RDS, in 2009. The hosts use those dates to challenge a polished story in which the complete suite arrived together. A plan to build a broad system is different from having all of it available on day one.

04 · 2006–2009

A credit card instead of a data center

The new customer was someone too small for the old sales process.

S3 let a developer store objects without buying, configuring, or maintaining the underlying server. EC2 offered computing capacity that could be started and stopped as needed. These were deliberately basic building blocks, rather than a requirement to adopt a new programming language or rebuild an application around a novel platform. Developers could use familiar tools and choose how much of the infrastructure to replace.

The business model made the technical offering more radical. The episode recounts engineer James Hamilton trying S3 for a personal project and receiving a $3.08 bill, followed by seven cents the next month. There was no request for proposals, vendor negotiation, or search for data-center space. Those tiny amounts were meaningful precisely because the old infrastructure sales model had been designed around much larger purchases and commitments.

Startups were the natural first customers. They could build something new without an installed infrastructure estate to migrate, and their demand might grow from almost nothing to a substantial business. AWS embraced that community through developer evangelists, help, and credits. The hosts remember the company being present at the gatherings where new applications were taking shape. In 2007, Justin.tv—later Twitch—participated in the first AWS Startup Challenge.

This was not merely cheaper equipment. It changed the cost of finding out whether an idea worked. Developers could get an application online before knowing if they would need a data center’s worth of resources. If demand disappeared, they could stop using capacity rather than be left with a room of purchased machines. The hosts connect that change to the speed of hackathons and the wave of startups they watched emerge.

05 · 2009–2010s

The startup tool grows up

Enterprise adoption demanded trust, contracts, and a different sales organization.

The same primitives could also help an established business move an existing application onto someone else’s infrastructure. The hosts call this “lift and shift.” A company could keep much of its application architecture while trading an upfront hardware commitment for ongoing usage charges. Later, it might adopt managed databases or redesign around services such as Lambda, where code runs without the customer managing an always-on server instance.

Ben distinguishes building on, in, and for the cloud. The distinctions describe increasing use of managed or cloud-native capabilities, not three compulsory stages that every application must complete. The hosts also qualify the sales pitch: a cloud provider does not remove every operational burden, and an application is not automatically replicated around the world merely because it runs on AWS. Geography, network traffic, and architecture still matter.

Netflix became an AWS customer in 2009. Reed Hastings’s appearance at the first re:Invent in 2012 helped validate more than technical scale. Amazon competed with Netflix in video, yet Hastings defended AWS as the right infrastructure choice. That endorsement addressed a fear shared by businesses wondering whether they could trust a supplier whose parent might also be a competitor. NASA, universities, and later the CIA provided other forms of credibility for institutional customers.

Amazon also had to learn enterprise sales. A credit card was not enough for organizations that needed negotiated contracts, billing arrangements, compliance, reliability commitments, and support. The hosts argue that working with research institutions helped build those capabilities. The result was a company that could serve both a developer experimenting with a few dollars and an institution committing a significant part of its operations.

06 · The long head start

Why the incumbents hesitated

The hosts distinguish business-model resistance from a bet on the wrong product.

The old infrastructure vendors had a reason to resist the new economics. Proprietary systems and database licenses could carry very high margins, supplemented by maintenance, upgrades, and audits. A metered service with lower margins could look unattractive to them while looking excellent to a retailer accustomed to thin profits. The hosts frame this as counterpositioning: the entrant’s model threatens the incumbent’s existing business.

Microsoft’s problem was different in important ways. It had the enterprise relationships AWS needed to build, but its server and tooling businesses initially pulled toward protecting existing products. The hosts also criticize Azure’s early emphasis on a new platform rather than straightforward infrastructure customers could move onto. They credit the later decision to give Azure protection and leadership under Satya Nadella, allowing it to become AWS’s strongest competitor.

Google had the technical experience of running enormous computing systems, but the hosts argue that its advertising economics and weaker enterprise sales capabilities made cloud a less natural organizational priority. Its initial App Engine offering likewise asked developers to adopt a particular platform. Their criticism is about the early offering and incentives; they explicitly recognize that Google Cloud subsequently became a viable competitor.

AWS’s primitives served two markets at once: startups could assemble new applications, and enterprises could move older ones. A platform designed too far ahead of customer needs could miss both. In the hosts’ telling, Amazon earned years of comparatively limited competition by matching what customers were prepared to use before asking them to adopt the more ambitious services that would come later.

07 · 2010s–2022

The hardest thing to move is the data

Storage becomes a commercial anchor, not just another service.

Databases became a major part of AWS. Beyond renting computing and object storage, Amazon could operate databases customers already understood and introduce its own services. The hosts discuss Redshift as both a data warehouse and an attack on Oracle’s position. They also describe Aurora and DynamoDB as part of a broader progression from general infrastructure toward more differentiated services.

The physical movement of information made the relationship sticky. A company’s stored data could grow faster than its ability to send it over a network. AWS offered Snowball appliances for transferring large datasets, then Snowmobile for much larger migrations using a truck. Those examples make the switching problem concrete: moving a large data estate is not the same as changing a subscription setting.

Even Amazon’s own migration from Oracle databases did not finish until 2019, thirteen years after the infrastructure-service launch. The hosts use that example to show how long database transitions can take. Putting data into a provider’s environment can therefore shape later choices, including where to run machine-learning work. Ben argues that AWS need not always have the absolute best machine-learning tool if it offers a strong enough one close to the customer’s existing data.

The strategy extended into hardware. Amazon acquired Annapurna Labs in 2015 and developed custom chips, including training and inference designs discussed in the episode. The point was to improve the economics and capabilities of the services customers consumed. Yet the hosts also notice a change in posture: the disruptive supplier that once attacked proprietary incumbents was now offering more proprietary, tightly integrated services of its own.

08 · 2015–2022

The cloud becomes visible in the accounts

High operating margins do not remove the need to finance the machines.

Amazon began separately disclosing AWS financials in 2015, an event the hosts jokingly call the “AWS IPO.” It was not a separate stock-market listing. The episode describes a roughly $6 billion annual revenue run rate at that disclosure, around 70% growth, and a 19.2% operating margin. The numbers revealed a different profit engine inside a company most people still understood as a retailer.

By the 2022 recording, the hosts put AWS at an approximately $80 billion revenue run rate with operating margins around 30%. They distinguish that pace from the $62 billion of revenue reported for 2021. They also highlight more than $100 billion of contracted future revenue. Backlog is not cash already collected, recognized revenue, or profit; it represents business expected to be delivered under contracts.

The investment pattern was different from retail. Amazon could collect a retail customer’s money before paying a supplier, but AWS often had to build or lease capacity before earning usage revenue from it. The hosts discuss capital leases and the effort to align infrastructure financing with receipts. A more profitable service could still require enormous capital and put pressure on current free cash flow.

Jassy became CEO of AWS in 2016, then succeeded Bezos as CEO of Amazon on July 5, 2021. Selipsky returned to lead AWS. The hosts see continuity between the retail and cloud businesses: both require large infrastructure investments spread across many customers. A fulfillment network and a data-center network are different assets, but Amazon’s willingness to build ahead of demand helped make both possible.

09 · The hosts’ September 2022 assessment

The advantages—and the bill for success

An unregulated-utility analogy comes with unresolved questions.

The hosts identify four central powers: early counterpositioning, scale economies, switching costs, and brand. A larger customer base spreads infrastructure costs; a trusted leader is a safer long-term choice; and a large data estate is difficult to move. They explicitly reject a direct network effect as the central explanation. Another company using AWS does not inherently improve a customer’s application; the benefit primarily arrives through scale.

Repeated price cuts are part of their explanation. The episode counts 23 reductions by 2012, 40 by 2013, and 51 by 2015. The hosts argue that sacrificing some current revenue could help win demand and make future capacity investment more predictable. Later margin expansion could coexist with competition because costs fell and customers adopted higher-level services. Their explanation is an interpretation of the economics, not a claim that every cost saving was passed through.

Success also created openings for others. The hosts regard Snowflake’s emergence as an important miss for AWS. They argue that Redshift focused too much on the old data-warehouse battle, while Snowflake offered a different customer experience. AWS’s enterprise obligations and sprawling menu of services could make it harder to produce a simple, cohesive product. The decentralized organization that enabled expansion also contributed to an intimidating catalog.

Multi-cloud and hybrid-cloud strategies complicated the picture. Customers might spread risk across providers, but they could give up benefits from tightly integrated proprietary services. AWS wanted customers to use those capabilities and sometimes extended them into customers’ own data centers. The hosts return to a basic question: does “cloud” describe where a machine sits, or the service interface through which computing is obtained? The answer was becoming less straightforward.

They also qualify the familiar Amazon experiment-and-abandon story. An enterprise service is hard to kill once customers rely on it. Maintaining something like SimpleDB, even after a better alternative exists, can protect trust and future business. That obligation helps explain both the durability of the relationship and the accumulation of services. Customer commitments constrain how freely AWS can discard experiments.

David calls AWS an unregulated utility for the internet; Ben sees the analogy in its fixed costs and huge scale. That is their framing, not a declaration that cloud has no regulation or no competitors. They connect the business to Bezos’s willingness to fund bets with asymmetric upside, while giving credit to the operational execution that made this particular bet succeed.

Both award AWS an A+, but their final discussion leaves a larger Amazon question open. Can a company organized to reinvest continuously eventually generate enough distributable cash to justify its value? Ben questions how to think about that transition; David is more confident in the long-term economics. Their references to Amazon’s roughly $1.5 trillion value are tied to the 2022 discussion. The companion ends with that disagreement intact, rather than converting admiration for AWS into a current investment recommendation.
